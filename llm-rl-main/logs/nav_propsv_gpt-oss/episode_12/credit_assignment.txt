[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts consistently begin with a brief forward push followed almost immediately by a sharp right turn, after which the agent interleaves short forward bursts (action 0) with quick corrective turns (action 2) and settles into a period of low‑amplitude adjustments before the episode ends; low‑reward rollouts either omit the early turn altogether or delay it, instead committing to long, uninterrupted forward thrusts that drive the agent straight into the outer corner of the L‑shaped layout. The successful trajectories also show a rhythmic alternation of forward and turn actions every few frames, keeping the orientation aligned with the corridor, whereas the poor trajectories display prolonged sequences of the same action (often forward) punctuated by abrupt, large‑scale direction changes that overshoot the intended path. By turning early, the high‑reward agent positions itself on the correct side of the bend and can fine‑tune its heading with small oscillations, which prevents wall collisions and yields steady positive reward accumulation. In contrast, the aggressive, uncorrected forward motion of the low‑reward rollouts quickly exhausts the safe region, leading to sudden loss of control, collisions, and large negative rewards.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts all begin with an extended series of forward actions (action 0) before any turn is taken, allowing the agent to build momentum and progress toward the goal; low‑reward episodes start with an immediate sharp turn (action 1 or 2), which quickly diverts the trajectory and leads to early termination. When a turn does occur in the successful runs, it is timed after several consecutive forward steps and is executed as a single, smooth adjustment rather than a rapid back‑to‑back sequence, keeping the path stable; the failing runs exhibit multiple consecutive turn actions or abrupt direction changes within a few timesteps, creating chaotic oscillations. The high‑reward trajectories also show brief, isolated corrective turns that promptly return to forward motion, whereas the low‑reward trajectories contain clusters of turns that never resume forward movement, causing the agent to stall or hit walls. Finally, successful episodes maintain a mostly monotonic increase in the state metric, reflecting consistent forward progress, while unsuccessful ones display sudden drops or spikes in the same metric, indicating loss of control and collisions. These timing and sequencing differences explain why the former accumulate large positive rewards and the latter incur heavy penalties.

[WORST POLICY]
The high‑reward rollouts all contain brief interruptions of the forward thrust (action = 2) with a neutral action (action = 0), creating short pauses that let the agent re‑orient before a final failure, whereas the low‑reward episodes apply the forward thrust continuously without any such pause. This intermittent stopping keeps the early‑stage state values much smoother and confined to a narrow range, while the low‑reward runs show large, sustained oscillations in the state throughout the episode. The higher‑reward trajectories also include at least one modest turn or corner‑following maneuver, giving the agent a momentary corrective adjustment that is completely absent from the chaotic, straight‑line motion of the low‑reward rollouts. By limiting overshoot and providing occasional re‑alignment, these patterns reduce collisions and keep the agent nearer to viable paths, which plausibly explains why the high‑reward rollouts achieve less negative returns.
