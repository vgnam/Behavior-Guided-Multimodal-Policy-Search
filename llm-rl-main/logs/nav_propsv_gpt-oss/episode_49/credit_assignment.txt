[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts consistently feature an extended run of the “forward” action (action 0) that carries the agent deep into the corridor before any corrective turn is made, whereas low‑reward episodes tend to insert a sharp turn (actions 1 or 2) almost immediately after the first step, causing early collisions or dead‑ends. When the high‑reward agent does turn, the correction is timed after a measurable drift—often a single brief overshoot followed by a single compensating action—so the turn stabilises the trajectory rather than over‑correcting; the low‑reward rollouts either never correct a drift or apply multiple rapid turns in succession, producing jittery, unstable motion. Moreover, the successful rollouts display a pattern of occasional, brief oscillations that are bounded and quickly damped, while the failing rollouts either lack any oscillatory adjustment (remaining stuck) or exhibit continuous, unbounded oscillations that never settle on a forward path. These timing and action‑selection differences keep the high‑reward agent moving toward the goal for many steps and only incur small penalties for correction, whereas the low‑reward agent’s premature or excessive turns terminate the episode with large negative rewards.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts consistently begin with an early, decisive forward thrust toward the goal, followed by a sharp turn (often using action 0) and an immediate corrective adjustment that quickly brings the agent back on course; low‑reward rollouts either linger before advancing or miss this early turn, allowing drift. Throughout the episode the high‑reward trajectories maintain regular, modest oscillations—alternating between actions 1 and 2 at roughly fixed intervals—to fine‑tune position, whereas the low‑reward runs show irregular, growing oscillations or long stretches of a single action that eventually cause loss of control. When the agent overshoots the target, high‑reward examples promptly reverse direction with a corrective action, keeping distance errors small, while low‑reward examples often continue past the target before attempting a late correction, incurring larger penalties. This combination of an early aggressive approach, timely directional corrections, and disciplined micro‑adjustments keeps the agent near the optimal path and yields substantially higher rewards.

[WORST POLICY]
The high‑reward rollouts all contain a brief, pronounced change of direction – a sharp turn or sudden thrust – that occurs early in the episode, whereas the low‑reward rollouts never deviate from a monotonic forward action and simply bounce back and forth. This early decisive maneuver produces a large jump in the state variable, quickly driving the agent into a terminal condition and truncating the episode, which limits the accumulated penalty. In contrast, the low‑reward trajectories remain stuck in a repetitive oscillation, applying the same action continuously and persisting for the full horizon, thereby accruing the maximum negative reward. The presence of a single, timed correction (or aggressive thrust) in the high‑reward cases thus explains why those rollouts end sooner and earn a less severe score than the endlessly wandering low‑reward runs.
