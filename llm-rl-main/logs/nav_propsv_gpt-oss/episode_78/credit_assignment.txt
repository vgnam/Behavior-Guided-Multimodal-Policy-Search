[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts consistently insert a corrective action (typically a brief turn of the opposite sign) within a few steps after any drift away from the centre of a corridor, whereas the low‑reward episodes either postpone the correction or repeat the same turn for many frames, causing the agent to veer into walls. In the successful runs the agent interleaves forward moves (action 0) with short, alternating left/right adjustments (actions 1 and 2) at a roughly regular interval, keeping its speed moderate; the poor runs often string together long bursts of forward thrust or repeated right turns without intervening fine‑tuning, which leads to abrupt collisions. The timing of these adjustments is also distinct: high‑reward trajectories react within one or two timesteps to a negative reward cue, while low‑reward trajectories wait several timesteps before responding, allowing the error to grow. Moreover, the high‑reward behavior shows a pattern of “cautious stabilization” where the state values settle into a narrow band before the episode ends, whereas low‑reward rollouts display wide oscillations that only narrow briefly before diverging again. These differences plausibly raise the cumulative reward by preventing costly crashes and ensuring steady progress toward the goal.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts keep a prolonged sequence of forward‑thrust actions (action 0) at the episode start, only inserting minor corrective turns (actions 1 or 2) after several steps of positive reward, whereas low‑reward rollouts begin with an immediate sharp turn or an aggressive thrust that quickly leads to a wall collision. The successful trajectories space their corrective actions evenly, using brief, low‑amplitude adjustments that preserve forward momentum, while the failures cluster abrupt direction changes together, creating chaotic, high‑frequency steering switches. When corrections are needed, the high‑reward runs apply a single small turn and then resume forward motion, whereas the low‑reward runs over‑correct with consecutive large turns that reverse direction and cause repeated negative‑reward encounters. This timing difference means the high‑reward agent maintains a stable heading through narrow passages, reducing the chance of hitting obstacles, while the low‑reward agent’s erratic steering produces frequent collisions and premature episode termination. Consequently, the pattern of “steady forward thrust punctuated by sparse, gentle tweaks” consistently correlates with higher cumulative reward.

[WORST POLICY]
The higher‑reward rollouts all contain a brief, purposeful burst of forward thrust followed by a change of action (often a turn or pause) that lets the agent stabilize before the episode ends, whereas the low‑reward episodes keep issuing the same forward‑movement action for most of the trajectory, producing huge swings in the state value. In the better rollouts the agent’s state changes modestly after the first action, indicating that it avoids long periods of unchecked acceleration; the poorer rollouts show rapid, repeated accelerations and decelerations that create chaotic oscillations. The occasional corrective turn or pause in the high‑reward cases prevents the agent from drifting far off course, while the low‑reward cases lack any such corrective decision and continuously over‑correct, driving the agent into collisions or out‑of‑bounds. Because the high‑reward behavior limits exposure to large penalties by curbing sustained motion and adding timely adjustments, it accumulates less negative reward than the erratic, constantly thrusting pattern seen in the low‑reward rollouts.
