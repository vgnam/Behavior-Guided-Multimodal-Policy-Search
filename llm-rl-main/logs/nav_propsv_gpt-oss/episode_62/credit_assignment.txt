[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts all begin with a sustained forward motion that carries the agent several steps before any sharp turn is taken, allowing it to accumulate positive reward early and stay away from obstacles; low‑reward rollouts either turn immediately or stay static and then make a sudden large change that ends the episode. When a turn is required, the successful runs introduce it later and follow it with a brief period of corrective “oscillatory” actions that quickly bring the trajectory back to a steady forward direction, whereas the poor runs either omit this corrective phase or make the turn and then continue jerking without regaining stability. The good trajectories also show a clear pattern of re‑stabilising after each correction—forward actions dominate for many consecutive steps—while the bad trajectories either end abruptly after a single corrective action or accelerate uncontrollably, leading to a rapid state spike and failure. This timing difference—early, sustained forward progress, delayed and compensated turns, and a return to consistent forward actions—keeps the agent in safe regions longer and therefore yields much higher cumulative reward.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts all begin with an extended stretch of forward actions (action 0) that keeps the agent aligned with a wall and builds momentum before any sharp turn is attempted, whereas low‑reward rollouts insert abrupt right or left turns almost immediately and break that forward drive. The successful trajectories also postpone jittery, corrective actions until well after the agent has settled into a consistent wall‑following path, while the failing runs intersperse frequent oscillations (alternating right/left or forward/backward) throughout the episode. When a turn is finally made in the high‑reward cases it is a single, deliberate rotation followed by a long forward segment, contrasting with the low‑reward cases where turns are repeated in rapid succession, causing the agent to drift and lose orientation. This disciplined timing preserves smooth motion, reduces unnecessary negative‑reward contacts, and allows the agent to stay within high‑reward corridors; the erratic, early, and repeated corrections in the low‑reward rollouts waste steps and lead to collisions or premature termination. Consequently, the presence of sustained forward movement and delayed, purposeful turning is the key behavioral signature that separates the high‑reward performances from the low‑reward ones.

[WORST POLICY]
The high‑reward rollouts all break the monotone “always thrust forward” pattern: after an initial forward move they insert a turn (or a brief pause action 0) within the first few frames, which keeps the agent hugging a wall and prevents a high‑speed collision, whereas the low‑reward rollouts keep action 2 for many consecutive steps and only switch to action 0 at the very end, causing a sudden crash. In the better runs the agent’s forward thrust is short‑lived and is followed by a corrective maneuver that stabilises its distance to the nearest wall, while the worst runs show long, unchecked bursts of thrust that produce large oscillations in the state value and overshoot the safe corridor. The occasional early use of action 0 in the high‑reward cases acts as a deliberate “brake” before hitting a boundary, whereas in the low‑reward cases the brake is applied too late to avoid the penalty. These timing differences create smoother, wall‑following trajectories that accrue less negative reward compared with the aggressive, uncorrected thrusts that dominate the low‑reward rollouts.
