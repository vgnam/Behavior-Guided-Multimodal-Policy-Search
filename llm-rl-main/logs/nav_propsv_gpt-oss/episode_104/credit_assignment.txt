[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts consistently combine short bursts of forward thrust (action 0) with immediate corrective actions (actions 1 or 2) that re‑align the agent after each movement, creating a rapid “push‑and‑steer” rhythm that keeps it centered in narrow passages; low‑reward rollouts either maintain a prolonged forward thrust without such corrections or delay corrective actions until after a large deviation, causing the agent to drift into walls or dead‑ends. In the successful episodes the agent executes sharp turns early, often within a few timesteps of entering a new corridor, whereas the poor episodes postpone turning until the trajectory is already misaligned, leading to abrupt crashes. The high‑reward trajectories also exhibit periodic oscillations—alternating forward and corrective actions every 1–3 steps—that prevent the buildup of momentum that would otherwise carry the agent into obstacles, while the low‑reward runs show monotonic motion that accumulates unchecked speed. Finally, successful rollouts intersperse brief pauses or backward nudges (action 2) after a forward surge, effectively “braking” before tight corners; the failing rollouts lack these braking cues, so they overshoot corners and incur large negative rewards. These timing‑specific action patterns give the agent finer positional control and thus substantially higher cumulative rewards.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts all begin with a long stretch of action 0 (forward thrust) that lasts through the first half of the episode, keeping the state monotically decreasing and building positive reward before any turn is attempted; low‑reward episodes either introduce a turn or switch to action 2 almost immediately, causing abrupt state changes and early penalties. When a direction change is needed, the successful rollouts insert a brief, single‑step turn (action 1 or 2) and then return to action 0, whereas the poor rollouts either linger in turning actions or oscillate between actions 0 and 2 for several consecutive frames, producing over‑correction and negative rewards. The high‑reward trajectories also avoid any sudden reversal late in the episode—there is no abrupt switch back to action 0 when the state is already low—while the low‑reward runs commonly feature a final abrupt forward action that spikes the state upward and triggers a large penalty. This disciplined timing preserves momentum and prevents the destabilizing spikes that the low‑reward agents incur. Consequently, sustained early forward motion combined with minimal, well‑timed turning actions explains the systematic reward gap between the two groups.

[WORST POLICY]
The high‑reward rollouts all contain a quick change of action within the first few frames – an early forward thrust followed by a sudden left‑turn or speed increase that drives the agent into a wall and ends the episode almost immediately, truncating the accumulation of negative reward. In contrast, the low‑reward rollouts never deviate from the same forward action (action 2) for the whole episode, producing long, oscillatory trajectories that keep the agent alive far longer and therefore accrue far larger penalties before termination. This timing difference—early, aggressive maneuver versus prolonged uniform motion—creates a systematic gap in total reward because the worst policy is penalized per‑step. The presence of an abrupt directional switch (or any non‑repeating action) in the high‑reward cases, and its complete absence in the low‑reward cases, is the key behavioral component driving the reward disparity.
