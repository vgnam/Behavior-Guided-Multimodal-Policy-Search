[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts consistently begin with a clear forward thrust (action 0) and maintain long stretches of forward motion before any turn, whereas low‑reward runs either start with a backward step or immediately intersperse forward and lateral actions, causing premature oscillations. When a turn is required, the successful trajectories execute a single, decisive turn (often a right‑hand sharp turn) at a well‑timed point and then resume forward motion, while the failing episodes show multiple rapid back‑and‑forth adjustments (alternating actions 1 and 2) that over‑correct and create wobble. The top rollouts keep the state value steadily decreasing (indicating progress toward the goal) and only exhibit brief, isolated negative‑reward spikes during turns; in contrast, low‑reward paths display sustained fluctuations in state value, reflecting repeated drift away from the target. Finally, the high‑reward behaviors avoid any abrupt backward movements after the initial progress, whereas the low‑reward examples include late‑stage backward steps that trigger large negative rewards and terminate the episode. These differences—steady forward bias, timely single turns, minimal oscillation, and monotonic state reduction—explain why the former achieve much higher cumulative rewards.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts all maintain a steady forward bias, inserting only modest, well‑timed turns that keep the agent near the centre of the track, whereas the low‑reward episodes contain a single large, abrupt maneuver (often a sudden rightward thrust) that drives the agent straight into a wall. In the successful runs the agent’s corrective actions are small and spread over several steps, producing smooth oscillations that gradually re‑align the trajectory; the failures show over‑corrections that occur in one step, resulting in a loss of control. The good trajectories also delay any sharp directional changes until after the agent has built up a stable forward momentum, while the poor trajectories make an immediate, aggressive turn from the start, causing early termination. Finally, high‑reward episodes exhibit consistent, incremental progress toward the goal rather than a burst of speed followed by a crash, which explains why they accumulate large positive rewards and the low‑reward ones end with large penalties.

[WORST POLICY]
The higher‑reward rollouts all contain at least one brief interval of purposeful, directed thrust—often a single forward or modest turning action—that produces a smooth, monotonic change in the state before any correction is needed, whereas the lowest‑reward episodes consist almost exclusively of the same action being repeated for the entire episode, creating continual, large‑amplitude oscillations in the state value. In the better rollouts the agent occasionally switches actions (e.g., from a forward thrust to a stabilising maneuver) at moments when the state curve begins to flatten or reverse, which damps overshoot and prevents the runaway swings that dominate the bad rollouts. The timing of these switches is early enough to keep the trajectory within the corridor of the maze, while the poor rollouts show no such early intervention and instead let the state drift far from the desired path before any correction is attempted. Because the occasional corrective action limits collisions and keeps the agent inside the navigable region, the episode accumulates a less severe penalty; the relentless, identical action in the low‑reward cases forces the agent to repeatedly bounce between extreme positions, incurring the maximal negative reward each time.
