[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts all begin with a sustained forward action and keep the forward bias for many consecutive steps, only inserting brief, small‑angle corrections instead of large, sudden turns; in contrast, low‑reward rollouts contain an early, abrupt turn (often the very action that triggers termination) or a rapid switch to backward motion. The successful trajectories also limit the magnitude of positional reversals, showing at most minor drifts before resuming forward progress, whereas the poor rollouts exhibit pronounced backward steps and repeated back‑and‑forth oscillations that waste time and push the agent toward obstacles. When a turn is required, high‑reward episodes execute it smoothly and immediately follow with another forward move, while low‑reward episodes either over‑rotate or linger in the turning state, creating prolonged indecision and loss of momentum. Finally, the high‑reward runs avoid any final “panic” action that produces a large negative reward, whereas the low‑reward runs end with a sudden, uncontrolled action that crashes the agent, directly accounting for the stark reward gap.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts consistently keep a steady forward action for long stretches (often > 200 timesteps) and only introduce turns or minor corrective actions after the state has progressed smoothly, whereas low‑reward rollouts insert abrupt action switches—especially rapid accelerations or sharp turns—within a few timesteps, causing sudden spikes in the state value and loss of control. The successful trajectories also show a pattern of delaying large directional changes until the agent has built momentum, using brief, low‑amplitude corrections (single‑step actions 1 or 2) rather than prolonged bursts of action 0 that dominate the failing episodes. Moreover, high‑reward episodes avoid early spikes above a state threshold (≈ 2.5) and instead maintain the state in a moderate range before gradually climbing, while low‑reward runs often overshoot this range early and then crash. This disciplined pacing prevents collisions and keeps the agent within the navigable corridor, directly translating into higher cumulative reward.

[WORST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts all contain at least one moment where the agent switches away from the default “action 2” thrust – either inserting a brief forward push (action 0) or a corrective turn – and then settles into a short, smoother forward segment before the episode ends. In contrast, every low‑reward rollout sticks with action 2 for the entire horizon, producing a persistent oscillatory motion with no purposeful re‑orientation or speed change. The occasional action change in the better runs lets the agent gain a few timesteps of positive progress (or avoid a wall) that reduces the cumulative penalty, whereas the unwavering thrust in the worst runs forces the agent to continually bounce off walls and accrue the maximum negative reward. This timing pattern – brief, purposeful deviation followed by a controlled glide versus relentless, unvarying thrust – plausibly explains why the high‑reward episodes finish with a less severe score.
