[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts all begin with a relatively long, uninterrupted run of **action 2 (forward thrust)** that quickly carries the agent into the high‑reward region, followed by a single, well‑timed **action 0 (directional correction)** exactly after a reward spike and then a return to forward thrust; low‑reward trajectories fragment that forward run into many short bursts, constantly swapping between actions 0, 1 and 2 without a clear post‑spike correction. The successful rollouts also keep **oscillations short and bounded**, using only a brief over‑correction before re‑stabilising, whereas the poor rollouts exhibit prolonged, repeated over‑corrections that drift the agent away from the intended path. In the high‑reward cases the agent’s state values stay within a narrow band after the initial thrust, indicating that it maintains momentum once a good direction is found; the low‑reward cases show large swings in state values, reflecting loss of control and wasted motion. These timing and action‑selection differences allow the high‑reward policy to reach rewarding waypoints quickly and stay on course, while the low‑reward policy wastes steps in erratic turns and fails to capitalize on early progress.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts all begin with brief, exploratory thrusts followed quickly by a transition to steady, wall‑following motion, whereas low‑reward runs launch into a single large forward thrust that ends abruptly. The successful trajectories keep their actions modest and alternate smoothly between small forward pushes and gentle corrective turns, while the failures contain sudden, high‑magnitude direction changes that produce large spikes in state values. In the good rollouts the agent consistently aligns its heading with nearby boundaries and then proceeds parallel to them, reducing the chance of collision; the poor rollouts ignore this cue and head straight into corners or open space, leading to crashes. Moreover, the high‑reward episodes delay any sharp turns until after a few steps of stable movement, whereas the low‑reward episodes execute sharp turns early, destabilising the dynamics. This combination of early exploration, gradual speed build‑up, and disciplined boundary tracking enables the agent to accrue far higher cumulative rewards than the erratic, impulsive behaviors seen in the low‑reward rollouts.

[WORST POLICY]
The high‑reward rollouts all contain at least one early action switch – they start with a forward thrust (action 2) and then change to a different command (often action 0) within the first few dozen steps, whereas the low‑reward rollouts keep issuing the same forward action for the entire episode. This brief turn or braking introduces a sudden change in the state trajectory that truncates the period of uncontrolled drift, so the agent spends far fewer steps accumulating large negative step‑rewards. In the high‑reward cases the state value spikes quickly and then the episode ends, limiting exposure to wall collisions or oscillations; the low‑reward trajectories maintain a persistent, roughly sinusoidal fluctuation in position for hundreds of steps, repeatedly incurring penalties. Consequently, the mixture of an early corrective action and an early termination in the high‑reward rollouts reduces total loss, while the monotonous, prolonged forward thrust in the low‑reward rollouts drives the reward down to the worst values.
