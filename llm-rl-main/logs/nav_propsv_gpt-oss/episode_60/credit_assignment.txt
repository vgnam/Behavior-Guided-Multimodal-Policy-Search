[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts consistently make **action 0 (forward thrust) the dominant move**, using it early to lock in a large positive reward and then only inserting a few well‑timed action 2 turns to negotiate bends; the low‑reward episodes instead alternate rapidly between actions 1/2, producing continuous turning and frequent back‑and‑forth steps that never settle into sustained forward progress. In the successful runs the **turns (action 2) are spaced out and occur after the agent has built momentum**, whereas the poor runs show **clusters of consecutive turns or immediate reversals**, indicating over‑correction and loss of heading. The high‑reward trajectories also display a **short burst of stability after the first few frames**, maintaining a relatively flat state‑value curve, while the low‑reward trajectories keep the state value swinging widely throughout the episode. Finally, the better rollouts obtain **large positive reward spikes within the first 5–10 steps**, establishing a favorable trajectory, whereas the low‑reward rollouts either miss these early spikes or replace them with small or negative rewards, causing the cumulative return to lag. These differences in action selection frequency, timing of turns, and early stabilization plausibly explain the systematic reward gap.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts repeatedly begin with a brief forward thrust followed almost immediately by short, rapid corrective actions that keep the agent centred in the corridor, whereas low‑reward episodes either delay the initial forward move or replace it with a single large jump that pushes the agent into walls. The successful trajectories maintain a steady rhythm of “micro‑thrust‑pause‑adjust” cycles—tiny forward actions punctuated by brief opposite‑direction or turning actions—so that any drift is corrected within a few frames; the failing runs show long stretches of uncorrected motion and only one‑off, coarse corrections that occur too late to prevent a collision. In the high‑reward cases the agent’s turn actions are timed just before a bend, producing smooth navigation around corners, while the low‑reward cases turn either after the corner has been missed or with excessive magnitude, leading to abrupt direction changes and crashes. Finally, the best rollouts exhibit a pattern of continuous, modest positive rewards that accumulate throughout the episode, whereas the poor rollouts contain isolated spikes of negative reward from single, aggressive moves that dominate the total score. This combination of early, measured thrusts, frequent fine‑grained adjustments, and well‑timed turns enables the agent to stay on a viable path and collect far higher cumulative reward.

[WORST POLICY]
In the higher‑reward rollouts the agent almost always breaks the initial forward thrust with a different action—usually a turn or a brief “0” action—within the first few steps, whereas the worst rollouts keep applying action 2 for hundreds of steps. These early corrective actions redirect the vehicle away from walls and prevent the long‑lasting oscillations that dominate the low‑reward trajectories. Because the high‑reward episodes terminate after only a handful of steps, they incur far fewer per‑step penalties, while the low‑reward runs accrue large cumulative penalties from persistent back‑and‑forth motion. Additionally, the better rollouts exhibit occasional sharp, aggressive thrusts that quickly change the state, contrasting with the smooth, repetitive forward motion of the poor rollouts that yields sustained instability. The combination of early action diversity, rapid state changes, and short episode length plausibly explains why those rollouts achieve a less negative total reward.
