[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts consistently contain long uninterrupted sequences of the forward action (action 2) followed by only brief, single‑step corrective turns (actions 0 or 1), whereas low‑reward rollouts are dominated by rapid alternation between forward and turn actions, producing many short forward bursts and frequent sharp pivots. In the successful runs the agent commits to a direction early and only corrects once the deviation becomes measurable, while the poorer runs exhibit immediate over‑corrections—often turning back toward the previous position within a few timesteps. The timing of the turns in high‑reward episodes is clustered around clear junctions or corners, giving the agent sustained progress along straight corridors; in contrast, low‑reward episodes show turns scattered throughout the trajectory, creating oscillatory “wiggle‑around” behavior that wastes steps. This disciplined forward‑then‑turn rhythm reduces wasted motion and keeps the agent on a smoother path toward the goal, directly translating into the observed reward gap.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts consistently begin with a short burst of forward motion (action 2 or 1) that quickly establishes positive momentum, then follow with timely, brief corrective turns (action 0 or 2) before the forward thrust loses speed; this alternating “push‑then‑steer” rhythm keeps the agent advancing while staying centered in the corridor. In contrast, low‑reward rollouts either launch into a single, large forward jump without an immediate correction or linger too long on one action, causing the state to drift toward walls or dead‑ends and terminate abruptly. The successful trajectories also exhibit frequent, micro‑adjustments—small, rapid turn actions spaced only a few steps apart—that prevent overshoot and maintain a smooth forward trajectory, whereas the poor runs show either absent or delayed adjustments, leading to oscillations that grow unchecked and culminate in crashes. By maintaining a balanced cadence of short forward bursts and prompt steering corrections, the high‑reward policy preserves stability and accumulates reward, while the opposite timing and lack of corrective turns in low‑reward episodes produce loss of control and negative returns.

[WORST POLICY]
In the higher‑reward rollouts the agent quickly reorients and then either stops or makes only a brief forward push—using action 0 or a single action 2—rather than continuously applying the forward command, which prevents it from driving into walls and limits the penalty incurred. By contrast, the lowest‑reward episodes persist with action 2 for many steps, producing large oscillations in the state value and frequent collisions that drive the reward far lower. The better rollouts also exhibit short, smooth trajectories with few abrupt jumps in state, while the worst rollouts contain rapid, large state changes that signal loss of control. Finally, the higher‑reward runs tend to terminate after one or two steps, curbing the accumulation of negative reward, whereas the poorer runs linger and accrue repeated penalties.
