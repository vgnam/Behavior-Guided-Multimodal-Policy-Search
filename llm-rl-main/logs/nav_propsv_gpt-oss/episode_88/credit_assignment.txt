[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts all feature long uninterrupted stretches of forward thrust (action 0) punctuated by brief, purposeful turns—usually a single sharp right turn—after which the agent promptly resumes forward motion, keeping its state values relatively stable; low‑reward rollouts, in contrast, contain frequent back‑and‑forth or backward actions (e.g., action 1) and rapid, successive direction changes that produce a noisy, oscillatory trajectory. The successful runs also time their corrective turns early (within the first few hundred frames) and then allow a period of stabilization, whereas the poor runs delay or repeat corrections, leading to over‑correction and drift. When a turn is made in the high‑reward cases, the agent quickly re‑aligns and proceeds without lingering wobble, while the low‑reward episodes exhibit lingering wobble and repeated over‑steering that culminate in collisions. This disciplined pattern of “forward‑most, turn‑once‑then‑stabilize” preserves momentum toward goal states and avoids the chaotic oscillations that incur large negative rewards. Consequently, the presence of sustained forward motion and minimal, well‑timed turning explains the large reward gap between the two groups.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts consistently contain long uninterrupted stretches of the forward action (action 0) that slowly raise the state value, punctuated only by occasional, small corrective turns (actions 1 or 2) that are spaced several timesteps apart, whereas low‑reward rollouts flip rapidly between forward and turning actions, producing jittery, over‑corrected motions and early spikes or drops in the state value. The successful trajectories also delay any “fail” termination, only invoking it after many stable steps, while the failing runs include an abrupt “fail” action immediately after a single thrust or a sudden sharp turn. Moreover, the high‑reward episodes exhibit smooth, incremental orientation changes that keep the agent away from walls, whereas the low‑reward episodes feature abrupt, sharp turns that send the agent into collisions or dead‑ends. These patterns together preserve momentum and positional stability in the good rollouts, while the erratic timing and aggressive turning in the bad rollouts quickly degrade control and trigger large negative rewards.

[WORST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts all contain at least one early deviation from the default “action 2” thrust—typically a sharp turn or a brief different action within the first few seconds—that redirects the agent away from the central corridor and lets it skim a wall before stopping, whereas the low‑reward rollouts keep the same action 2 for the entire episode and never alter their heading. This early reorientation appears together with a short burst of forward thrust followed by an immediate pause, which prevents the agent from building up the large oscillations seen when the thrust is applied continuously. The high‑reward trajectories also show intermittent sensor‑guided alignment (the red line staying close to a wall) and quick corrective adjustments, while the low‑reward runs lack any wall‑following cues and instead wander back‑and‑forth with no corrective feedback. Because the occasional turn and timely stop break the monotonic oscillatory pattern, the agent avoids the sustained collisions that generate the very large negative rewards in the low‑reward cases. Consequently, the presence of early, purposeful action changes and brief, controlled thrusts explains why the higher‑reward rollouts finish with less severe penalties.
