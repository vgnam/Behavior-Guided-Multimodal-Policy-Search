[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts repeatedly begin with a strong forward thrust (action 0 or 2) and then keep a steady stream of forward actions, inserting only brief, small corrective actions (the occasional action 1 or 2) at regular short intervals, which keeps the state value within a modest band. In contrast, low‑reward episodes either accelerate in one direction for many steps before a sudden, large‑magnitude correction or make the first correction much later, producing extreme state excursions and abrupt direction flips. The successful rollouts also time their adjustments early—within the first few frames—so that any drift is damped before it can amplify, whereas the failing rollouts let errors accumulate and then try to recover with an oversized maneuver that leads to loss of control. This pattern of continuous forward drive combined with frequent, modest, early corrections yields smoother trajectories and avoids the catastrophic over‑corrections that dominate the low‑reward cases, directly explaining the large reward gap.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts keep the forward action (action 0) dominant for long stretches, only inserting brief, mild turn actions (1 or 2) after the agent has built up momentum and is safely away from walls; this steady advance lets the agent accumulate positive reward while staying within a stable state band. In contrast, low‑reward rollouts either rush forward straight into a wall or make abrupt, large‑amplitude turns almost immediately, so they lack the early period of controlled forward motion that prevents collisions. The successful runs also time their corrective turns later, using short‑duration adjustments that gently steer the trajectory rather than over‑correcting, which keeps the state values from diverging. Unsuccessful episodes either over‑correct (large swings between actions) or never correct at all, producing oscillatory or runaway trajectories that quickly hit obstacles and incur large penalties. Consequently, the combination of prolonged forward thrust with sparsely‑spaced, small‑magnitude turn actions is the key behavioral pattern that drives the large reward gap.

[WORST POLICY]
The high‑reward rollouts all contain an early deviation from the default “thrust‑forward” action 2—either a gradual turn (a decreasing orientation angle) or a decisive switch to action 0 within the first‑few dozen to one‑hundred steps—whereas the low‑reward rollouts keep action 2 unchanged for hundreds of steps. This early change limits the length of time the agent accumulates step‑wise penalties, so the episode ends sooner and the total reward is less negative. In the better rollouts the agent also exhibits a purposeful orientation adjustment, steadily rotating toward a viable corridor before the final stop, while the poor rollouts show no orientation modulation and simply oscillate in place. Finally, the occasional use of action 0 in the high‑reward trajectories acts as a hard stop that prevents further collisions, a behavior that never appears in the low‑reward sequences, causing those runs to linger and accrue larger penalties.
