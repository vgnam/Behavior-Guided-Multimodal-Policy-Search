[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts all begin with a sustained forward thrust that carries the agent well into the corridor before any turn is attempted, allowing it to gather momentum and keep the state values steadily increasing; in contrast, low‑reward episodes introduce sharp turns or large directional changes almost immediately, causing abrupt state drops and collisions. When a turn is required, the successful rollouts execute a single, smooth adjustment that aligns with the upcoming corridor and then resume forward motion, whereas the failures show repeated, jittery corrective actions that oscillate back and forth and often overshoot the desired heading. The good trajectories also delay major steering inputs until the agent has clearly identified a corner (typically after several frames of stable forward motion), while the poor runs react prematurely to perceived obstacles, leading to over‑correction and loss of control. Finally, the high‑reward episodes maintain a monotonic increase in reward throughout the episode, indicating continuous progress toward the goal, whereas the low‑reward cases exhibit sudden negative spikes right after an abrupt maneuver, suggesting that premature or excessive turning directly precipitates the large penalty.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts constantly interleave forward moves (action 0) with short, frequent corrective turns (actions 1 and 2) every 2–4 steps, keeping the state value in a moderate range and preventing the agent from drifting into walls; in contrast, the low‑reward examples contain long monotonic stretches of action 0 (sometimes hundreds of steps) followed by a single abrupt turn that produces a large state jump and a collision. The successful trajectories also begin their first turn almost immediately after the initial forward step, whereas the poor runs delay any turning until after a prolonged straight segment, allowing momentum to carry the agent into unsafe regions. Moreover, high‑reward episodes exhibit a pattern of brief, low‑amplitude oscillations that continuously correct the heading, while the low‑reward cases show either no oscillation (flat forward motion) or a single over‑correction that flips the orientation sharply. This consistent early‑and‑regular correction prevents extreme state changes that trigger heavy penalties, explaining why the mixed‑action, tightly timed pattern yields substantially higher total reward.

[WORST POLICY]
The higher‑reward rollouts all contain a distinct mid‑episode shift away from the default “forward” action 2—either a brief turn (action 1) to follow the inner L corridor or an early deceleration (action 0) that slows the vehicle before the final frames—whereas the low‑reward runs remain locked on action 2 for the entire episode, producing unchecked forward thrust. This timing cue lets the better rollouts correct their heading or reduce speed just as they approach corners or obstacles, preventing the large overshoots and collisions that dominate the low‑reward trajectories. In the high‑reward cases the turn or slowdown occurs after a short burst of acceleration, creating a smoother, bounded path; in the low‑reward cases the absence of any such corrective action yields persistent oscillations and large swings in state values. Consequently, the presence of a timely heading‑adjust or braking decision correlates with a less negative total reward, while its omission leads to the erratic, over‑correcting behavior that drives the worst scores.
