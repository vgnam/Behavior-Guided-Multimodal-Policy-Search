[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts keep action 0 (forward) dominant for the first large segment of the episode and only introduce a turn (action 2) after the agent has already built up a steady increase in its state value, whereas low‑reward rollouts either postpone the first turn until after a long straight run or insert sharp turns early, causing sudden drops in the state value. When turns do occur in the successful runs they are brief, modest corrections that quickly return to forward motion, while the poor runs show clusters of consecutive turns or back‑and‑forth adjustments (actions 1/2) that create prolonged oscillations and destabilise the trajectory. The high‑reward trajectories also avoid any extended sequence of alternating left/right actions; instead they exhibit a smooth, monotonic progression with only occasional, well‑timed micro‑adjustments, whereas the low‑reward episodes feature repeated over‑corrections that amplify error and lead to collisions. Finally, the successful rollouts terminate with the agent still moving forward, while the failing ones end with a last‑minute aggressive turn that produces a large negative reward, indicating that premature or excessive turning is the key factor separating the two groups.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts consistently interleave short forward thrusts (action 0) with frequent, modest turn actions (actions 1/2), creating brief “stabilisation” windows where the agent proceeds straight for a few steps before correcting its heading; low‑reward runs lack these windows, instead issuing long, uninterrupted forward pushes that quickly drive the agent into walls. In the successful episodes the agent makes an early directional adjustment—often a sharp turn away from the initial edge—so it stays within the maze corridors, whereas the failures begin by moving straight ahead from the corner and only turn after a collision has already occurred. The good rollouts also display a rhythmic oscillation of corrective turns that keeps the state value near the centre of the map, preventing large velocity spikes, while the bad rollouts exhibit a single, aggressive acceleration that sends the state far from the centre and ends the episode prematurely. Finally, the high‑reward trajectories show a pattern of re‑evaluating and fine‑tuning the heading after every few steps, which appears to preserve control and accumulate steady positive rewards, whereas the low‑reward trajectories show no such periodic re‑assessment, leading to uncontrolled drift and large negative penalties.

[WORST POLICY]
The high‑reward rollouts all feature a brief burst of a strong forward thrust followed almost immediately by a sudden change of action that ends the episode, so the agent spends only a few steps accumulating penalty before terminating. In contrast, the low‑reward rollouts keep the same forward action for a long stretch, producing prolonged, large‑amplitude oscillations in the state value that keep the episode alive and accrue far more negative reward. The early “stop‑early” decision in the high‑reward cases limits exposure to the environment’s costly dynamics, whereas the sustained, repetitive forward command in the low‑reward cases forces the agent to repeatedly over‑correct and bounce between high and low states. Consequently, the brief, decisive termination in the better rollouts yields a substantially higher (less negative) average return.
