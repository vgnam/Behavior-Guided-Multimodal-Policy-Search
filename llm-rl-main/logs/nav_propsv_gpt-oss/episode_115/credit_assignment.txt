[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts consistently contain long, uninterrupted stretches of the forward action (action 0) – often lasting dozens of timesteps – punctuated only by brief, gentle corrective turns (actions 1 or 2) that keep the agent’s heading steady, whereas low‑reward rollouts are dominated by rapid, alternating direction changes and frequent sharp turns that break up forward motion. In the successful trajectories the agent makes only occasional, small backward actions (rarely and usually to fine‑tune position) while the poor trajectories show many abrupt backward or over‑correcting moves that create oscillatory, jerky motion. Timing-wise, the high‑reward runs delay any major turn until after a sustained forward segment, allowing steady progress toward the goal; the low‑reward runs introduce turns almost immediately, resulting in back‑and‑forth jitter and loss of momentum. Moreover, the high‑reward behavior tends to bias right‑ward adjustments that align the agent with the corridor layout, whereas the low‑reward behavior frequently mis‑aligns with the maze, causing collisions or dead‑ends. These differences plausibly raise reward by preserving forward velocity and minimizing unnecessary corrections in the high‑reward rollouts, while the excessive, untimely turning and oscillation in the low‑reward rollouts waste time and incur penalties.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts all contain long uninterrupted stretches of the forward‑thrust action (action 0) that gradually raise the state value, only punctuated by brief, low‑amplitude corrective turns (actions 1 or 2) taken after the agent has already made progress; in contrast, low‑reward rollouts either begin with an immediate turn or a sudden large change in state and never sustain forward thrust for more than a few steps, causing an early plunge in the state value and premature termination. The successful episodes also time their corrective actions later—usually after the agent has moved several frames forward—so the corrections act as fine‑tuning rather than abrupt reorientations, whereas the failing runs apply turns immediately or erratically, producing jerky, unstable trajectories. Moreover, high‑reward trajectories maintain a monotonic increase in the cumulative reward signal, reflecting steady forward motion, while low‑reward paths show sharp negative spikes that coincide with abrupt state drops or collisions. These consistent differences suggest that persistent forward propulsion combined with delayed, modest steering stabilizes navigation and yields the large reward advantage.

[WORST POLICY]
The high‑reward rollouts all contain an early, decisive action (often a single forward thrust followed by a sharp turn or pause) that quickly redirects the agent away from the nearest wall, whereas the low‑reward rollouts persist with the same “action = 2” forward command for the entire episode, causing the state to swing back and forth and repeatedly hit boundaries. In the better rollouts the aggressive thrust is brief and is followed by a change of control input (e.g., a turn or stop) within the first few timesteps, while the poorer rollouts never alter the control signal, resulting in sustained oscillatory motion. This early corrective maneuver prevents the agent from building up large positional errors and avoids collisions, which yields a less negative cumulative reward. Conversely, the lack of any timely correction in the low‑reward cases lets errors accumulate, producing larger overshoots, repeated collisions and therefore much harsher penalties.
