High‑reward rollouts all contain long uninterrupted stretches of the forward‑thrust action (action 0) that gradually raise the state value, only punctuated by brief, low‑amplitude corrective turns (actions 1 or 2) taken after the agent has already made progress; in contrast, low‑reward rollouts either begin with an immediate turn or a sudden large change in state and never sustain forward thrust for more than a few steps, causing an early plunge in the state value and premature termination. The successful episodes also time their corrective actions later—usually after the agent has moved several frames forward—so the corrections act as fine‑tuning rather than abrupt reorientations, whereas the failing runs apply turns immediately or erratically, producing jerky, unstable trajectories. Moreover, high‑reward trajectories maintain a monotonic increase in the cumulative reward signal, reflecting steady forward motion, while low‑reward paths show sharp negative spikes that coincide with abrupt state drops or collisions. These consistent differences suggest that persistent forward propulsion combined with delayed, modest steering stabilizes navigation and yields the large reward advantage.
