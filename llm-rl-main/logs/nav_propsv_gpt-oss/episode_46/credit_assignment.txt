[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts consistently alternate a brief forward thrust (action 0) with an immediate corrective turn (action 1 or 2) every one or two steps, keeping the vehicle tightly aligned with the winding track; low‑reward rollouts either omit these early turns or delay them, letting a longer straight run (three‑plus forward actions) carry the agent off the intended lane. The successful runs also exhibit rapid micro‑adjustments—tiny back‑and‑forth oscillations that correct any drift—whereas the poor runs show a single large displacement followed by only a coarse, late correction. Early in the episode the high‑reward trajectories make a turn as soon as the curvature of the path is encountered, while the low‑reward trajectories press forward past the corner and only later suffer a big negative penalty. These timing differences prevent collisions and maintain progress, directly translating into the large reward gap between the two groups.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts keep action 0 (forward thrust) as the dominant move and insert only brief, single‑step turns (actions 1 or 2) spaced several timesteps apart, which produces smooth, incremental state changes and lets the agent stay within the navigable corridor. In contrast, the low‑reward episodes contain either long stretches of the same action that drive the state to extreme values or sudden, large‑magnitude jumps (often a single turn or a backward move) that create abrupt position changes and early termination. The successful rollouts also avoid any prolonged backward‑movement or high‑magnitude negative‑reward actions, whereas the failures frequently include a brief backward thrust or an immediate large negative reward after a turn. By maintaining gradual forward progress with only minimal corrective turns, the high‑reward policy preserves stability and accumulates positive reward, while the erratic, abrupt actions of the low‑reward rollouts cause loss of control and large penalties.

[WORST POLICY]
The high‑reward rollouts all contain an early, sharp change in action – typically a switch from the forward thrust (action 2) to a reversal or stop (action 0) within the first few timesteps – which drives the agent into a failure state quickly and truncates the episode, limiting the amount of accumulated negative reward. In contrast, the low‑reward rollouts keep action 2 active for the whole episode, producing long, persistent forward thrusts that generate many small penalties as the agent oscillates and drifts without ever forcing an early termination. The brief, controlled thrust that precedes the abrupt turn in the high‑reward cases gives the agent a momentary gain in state value before the crash, whereas the low‑reward cases show no comparable corrective action and instead wander erratically for hundreds of steps. Because the total reward is summed over the episode length, the early‑crash pattern yields a comparatively higher (less negative) return, while the prolonged, uncorrected forward motion inflates the cumulative penalty and produces the worst scores.
