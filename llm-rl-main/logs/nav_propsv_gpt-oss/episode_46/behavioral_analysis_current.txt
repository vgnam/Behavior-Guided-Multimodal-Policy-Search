The high‑reward rollouts all include an early “positive‑reward” cue (e.g., a +5 or other small reward) that coincides with a modest, incremental state change rather than a sudden jump, indicating that the agent first aligns its heading before applying thrust. They also tend to keep the agent near a wall or follow a gentle turn, using a single forward action that is paced and stays within the safe corridor for at least one step before termination. In contrast, the low‑reward rollouts show an immediate, large‑magnitude change in state (a sudden drop or spike) with no preceding positive cue, and the agent crashes into a boundary on the very first action. This abrupt, uncontrolled thrust eliminates any chance of corrective maneuvering and therefore yields the larger negative returns. The consistent presence of a small initial reward and restrained, wall‑hugging or turning behavior in the better rollouts plausibly explains why they lose less reward than the reckless, instant‑collision patterns seen in the poorer rollouts.
