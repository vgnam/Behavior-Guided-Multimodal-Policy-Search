[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts all begin with a sustained forward action (action 0) that carries the agent several steps before any turn, and when a corner is reached they execute a single, well‑timed sharp turn (usually to the right) and immediately resume forward thrust, keeping backward actions rare or absent. In contrast, low‑reward rollouts show early, repeated oscillations between forward, turn, and backward actions, often hesitating or reversing direction before committing to a turn, which creates jittery state trajectories. The successful rollouts also limit the length of corrective sequences, applying just one or two quick adjustments instead of prolonged back‑and‑forth motions that dominate the poorer rollouts. Because continuous forward progress preserves positive reward while brief, decisive turns avoid collisions, the smoother, forward‑biased pattern yields substantially higher returns than the erratic, over‑correcting behavior seen in the low‑reward episodes.

[BEST POLICY]
In the successful rollouts the agent repeatedly moves forward for a short stretch and then executes a sharp turn (the “action 0” pattern) that yields an immediate boost in its state value, creating a regular turn‑and‑gain cycle that continuously corrects its heading. By spacing these corrective turns throughout the episode, the agent maintains alignment with the corridor and avoids large deviations that would end the episode early. In contrast, the low‑reward runs either make a single abrupt jump in state early on or drift without purposeful turning, showing no periodic corrective turn and quickly losing orientation. They also exhibit immediate, chaotic oscillations that never settle into smoother motion, leading to early termination and preventing the accumulation of the turn‑based reward spikes seen in the high‑reward trajectories.

[WORST POLICY]
The high‑reward rollouts all keep a single, steady action (usually a forward thrust or a smooth, consistent turn) for most of the episode, producing a smooth, monotonic increase in the state value and avoiding any sudden reversals; in contrast, the low‑reward rollouts repeatedly switch actions, often inserting an abrupt “stop/turn” (action 0) late in the episode that creates a sharp state‑value jump and a collision. The successful trajectories also exhibit minimal oscillation – their state values change gradually and stay within a narrow band – whereas the failing runs show large, rapid fluctuations and overshoots that indicate over‑correction and loss of directional stability. When the high‑reward agent reaches a wall it follows it with a gentle, continuous curvature, effectively using the boundary as a guide, while the low‑reward agent either crashes into the wall or makes a sudden, uncontrolled maneuver away from it. Because steady, low‑variance motion reduces the chance of hitting obstacles and keeps the agent moving toward the goal, it yields higher returns, whereas erratic switching and late abrupt turns produce the large negative rewards observed in the poor rollouts.
