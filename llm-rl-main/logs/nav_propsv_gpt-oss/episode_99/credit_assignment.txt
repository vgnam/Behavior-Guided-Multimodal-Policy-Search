[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts consistently begin with a sustained forward action (action 0) that builds momentum, followed shortly by a single, well‑timed corrective turn (action 1 or 2) just before each corner, whereas low‑reward episodes either delay the initial forward push or replace it with an abrupt turn that sends the agent drifting away from the optimal path. In the successful runs the agent alternates quickly between forward moves and brief corrective turns, never lingering on a turn for more than one or two steps; the poorer runs contain extended sequences of the same turn action, producing over‑rotation and a loss of alignment with the corridor. The high‑reward trajectories also exhibit small, incremental adjustments—tiny “wiggles” that keep the agent centered—while the low‑reward ones show large, sudden direction changes that lead to collisions or the dramatic –200 penalty at the episode’s end. Finally, the effective policy makes its corrective actions exactly when a corner is approached, using the reward signal to anticipate the turn, whereas the low‑reward behavior either reacts too late or over‑reacts, causing the agent to overshoot and incur negative rewards. These timing and action‑selection differences explain why the former consistently achieve twice the reward of the latter.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts consistently feature long uninterrupted stretches of action 0 (forward) followed by only occasional, gentle turns, allowing the agent to make steady progress before any correction is needed; low‑reward rollouts break forward motion with rapid alternations of actions 1 and 2, producing jittery, back‑and‑forth oscillations that waste time and steer the agent off‑course. In the successful episodes the first major turn occurs after the agent has already built up momentum, whereas in the failing episodes a sharp turn is made almost immediately, often followed by immediate over‑corrections that undo the previous step. High‑reward trajectories also show a clear “settling” phase where the agent stabilizes its orientation and avoids late‑stage abrupt direction changes, while low‑reward runs contain late‑stage spikes of action 1 or 2 that precipitate collisions or boundary exits. This combination of sustained forward thrust, delayed and smooth turning, and the absence of frantic oscillations plausibly yields higher cumulative rewards by minimizing wasted movements and preventing catastrophic failures.

[WORST POLICY]
The higher‑reward rollouts all contain a short phase of a controlled forward thrust that is quickly followed by either a modest slowdown or a corrective turn, allowing the agent to settle into a stable trajectory before the episode ends; in contrast, the −500 rollouts keep applying the same forward‑type action (action 2) for the whole episode, producing large overshoots and repeated oscillations around a central point. The successful runs also show at least one deliberate change of direction—usually a right‑hand turn that lines the agent up with a corridor—whereas the low‑reward runs never deviate from their initial heading, leaving them to bounce against obstacles. Moreover, the better rollouts exhibit a brief pause or reduction in acceleration after the first few steps, which damps the momentum that would otherwise drive the agent into the walls; the worst rollouts maintain high‑speed, unmodulated motion throughout, causing the continual “jumping” seen in the state values. These timing and action‑variation differences keep the agent inside the navigable region longer, preventing early termination and therefore yielding the relatively higher (less negative) rewards.
