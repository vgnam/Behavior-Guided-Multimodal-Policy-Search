[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts keep a sustained forward thrust for several steps before making any turn, creating long streaks of positive reward and only modest, well‑timed corrections; in contrast, low‑reward rollouts often begin with an abrupt divergence or immediate over‑steer that produces a rapid negative spike and short, unstable bursts of reward. The successful trajectories apply corrective actions after a few consistent forward moves, whereas the failures apply large corrective actions instantly or oscillate erratically, causing frequent state jumps and collisions. High‑reward episodes maintain momentum and avoid prolonged periods of stillness, while low‑reward episodes contain pauses or jittery back‑and‑forth motions that waste time and increase exposure to hazards. Finally, the better rollouts limit extreme state changes near the episode’s end, whereas the poorer ones experience a late large negative reward, indicating loss of control; this steadier, measured control plausibly preserves path fidelity and accumulates higher total reward.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts all feature long uninterrupted blocks of action 0 (steady forward thrust) that dominate the episode, with only brief, single‑step turns (actions 1 or 2) inserted after several forward steps to make minor course corrections; this smooth, forward‑biased rhythm keeps the agent advancing toward the goal while preventing large orientation swings. In contrast, the low‑reward examples either start with an immediate turn or insert abrupt, multi‑step turn sequences early on, and they never sustain more than one or two consecutive action 0 steps, causing the trajectory to wobble and often collide with obstacles. Moreover, the successful rollouts delay any sharp directional change until after the agent has built up momentum (e.g., turning only after 4‑6 frames of forward motion), whereas the failures show sudden action 1 spikes that instantly reverse the state value and lead to termination. This pattern of prolonged forward motion with minimal, well‑timed corrections therefore explains why the high‑reward rollouts accumulate large positive returns while the low‑reward ones suffer large penalties.

[WORST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts all contain at least one brief non‑forward action (typically action 0) that brakes or turns the agent just before a wall, whereas the low‑reward episodes keep issuing the forward thrust action 2 for the entire horizon and never introduce a corrective stop. The moments when the high‑reward agent switches from forward to a corrective action are timed early enough to limit the amplitude of its state oscillations, producing only modest back‑and‑forth swings; the low‑reward runs exhibit large, unchecked swings because they never break the monotonic forward sequence. In the higher‑scoring traces the agent also makes an early, gentle directional adjustment (a slight rightward or upward drift) that steers it away from the inner corner, while the worst rollouts head straight toward the corridor’s bend without any turn. These early corrections reduce the chance of a hard collision, so the episode ends with a less severe penalty, whereas the uncorrected, continuously aggressive forward motion in the low‑reward rollouts inevitably leads to the maximal negative reward. Consequently, intermittent braking/turning and timely, small‑magnitude course corrections are the key behavioral differences that account for the reward gap.
