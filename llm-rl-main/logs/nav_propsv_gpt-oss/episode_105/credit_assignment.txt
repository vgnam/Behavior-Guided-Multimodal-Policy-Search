[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts keep long uninterrupted stretches of action 0 (steady forward motion) and only insert a turn (usually action 2 to the right) after several positive‑reward steps, producing straight‑segment‑then‑turn trajectories; low‑reward runs either accelerate without a sustained forward phase or make abrupt turns after only a few steps, which quickly drives the state into a failure region. The successful runs also use corrective actions (action 1) sparingly and only after they have built up momentum, whereas the poor runs oscillate with frequent back‑and‑forth corrections, indicating over‑steering and loss of stability. When a turn is needed, the high‑reward agents execute it at a point where the surrounding walls allow a smooth redirection, while the low‑reward agents tend to turn too early or too late, colliding with obstacles. Finally, the high‑reward trajectories show gradual, moderate growth in the state value, avoiding sudden spikes, whereas the low‑reward trajectories feature rapid state jumps that correspond to aggressive thrusts and immediate crashes. These consistent timing and action‑selection differences plausibly explain why the former accumulate large positive rewards and the latter incur large penalties.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts keep a steady forward drive for most of the episode (e.g., prolonged sequences of action 0 or 2) and only insert brief, low‑amplitude turns (action 1) when a corner is reached, whereas the low‑reward rollouts make an early, large‑magnitude turn or reversal and never return to sustained forward motion. The successful trajectories also show short, corrective oscillations that are quickly damped, while the failures exhibit persistent drift away from the goal with no stabilising feedback. Timing‑wise, the good rollouts spend hundreds of steps progressing toward the target before any adjustment, but the bad rollouts deviate within the first few dozen steps and continue to diverge. This pattern suggests that maintaining long, uninterrupted forward thrust and limiting aggressive direction changes prevents the agent from leaving the navigable corridor, directly yielding the large reward gap.

[WORST POLICY]
The high‑reward rollouts all terminate after only a few timesteps, typically by executing an abrupt direction‑changing action (often action 0) shortly after an initial forward thrust, which causes an early collision and therefore limits the amount of negative reward that can be accrued; in contrast, the low‑reward rollouts keep applying the forward action (action 2) for many consecutive steps, wandering around the corridor for a long time before finally failing, so the penalty compounds. The early switch from a steady forward command to a sharp turn in the high‑reward episodes also prevents the agent from building up large state values, whereas the low‑reward episodes show steadily rising state numbers that only spike near the very end. High‑reward trajectories display a brief, decisive burst of motion followed by an immediate corrective (or terminating) action, while the low‑reward ones exhibit prolonged, monotonic forward motion with only minor oscillations and no early corrective maneuver. Because the environment penalises time spent alive, the swift “go‑then‑crash” pattern of the high‑reward rollouts yields a less negative total return than the drawn‑out forward‑only behaviour of the low‑reward rollouts.
