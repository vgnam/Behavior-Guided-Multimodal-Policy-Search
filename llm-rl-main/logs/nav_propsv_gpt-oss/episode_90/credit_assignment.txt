[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts repeatedly interleave action 0 (the forward‑moving action that yields a steady +5 reward) with short bursts of action 1, using the latter only to correct the state when it dips below a modest threshold, whereas low‑reward rollouts either persist with a single forward action for too long or make abrupt, isolated turns (actions 1 or 2) without preceding state feedback. In the successful episodes the agent times its corrective turns early—typically within the first few steps after a slight negative reward—so the trajectory stays near the centre of the corridor, while the failing runs delay or completely omit such early corrections, allowing the state to drift into walls and incur large penalties. The high‑reward trajectories also display brief, purposeful oscillations (alternating 0/1) that keep the agent’s velocity moderate, preventing the overshoot that characterises the low‑reward runs where the agent either rushes straight ahead or executes a single sharp turn late in the episode. By maintaining a pattern of frequent, low‑amplitude forward actions punctuated by timely, state‑driven corrections, the policy sustains positive cumulative reward and avoids the catastrophic boundary collisions seen when those corrective actions are absent or mistimed.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts repeatedly interleave a modest forward action (action 0) with brief lateral corrections (actions 1 or 2), producing a slow, oscillatory progression that keeps the agent inside the safe corridor and lets it fine‑tune its heading before each larger move; low‑reward rollouts, by contrast, issue a single aggressive forward thrust that jumps the state value dramatically and leaves no room for corrective steps, often driving the agent straight into a hazard. In the successful trajectories the corrective actions appear early and recur throughout the episode, creating a pattern of continual small adjustments that gradually steer the agent toward the goal, whereas the failures show a long stretch of no correction followed by a sudden, large‑amplitude action that terminates the episode. The high‑reward behavior also includes a final phase of stabilized motion—either sustained forward steps or only minimal side tweaks—allowing the agent to consolidate its position, while the low‑reward episodes end abruptly without any stabilizing period. This combination of incremental forward motion, frequent timely corrections, and end‑of‑episode stabilization explains why the oscillatory, corrective strategy yields far higher cumulative rewards than the reckless, single‑burst thrusts seen in the poor rollouts.

[WORST POLICY]
The high‑reward rollouts all contain a brief, purposeful forward thrust followed quickly by a change of action – usually a turn that keeps the agent hugging a wall or a boundary – whereas the low‑reward rollouts persist with the same forward‑thrust action (action 2) for many steps, producing long stretches of unchecked acceleration and ensuing oscillations. This early turning in the good rollouts limits the distance travelled before a collision and therefore caps the accumulated penalty, while the monotone thrust in the bad rollouts lets the agent drift far into open space, lose orientation and repeatedly over‑correct, inflating the negative reward. In the better rollouts the agent also tends to terminate the episode after only a few timesteps (often after the first sharp turn), exposing it to fewer time‑step penalties, whereas the poor rollouts run much longer, accumulating many oscillatory penalties before a final crash. Finally, the successful trajectories show deliberate wall‑following and short, decisive maneuvers, contrasting with the erratic, center‑focused, and purely forward‑biased motions of the low‑reward cases, which explains why the former achieve comparatively higher (less negative) returns.
