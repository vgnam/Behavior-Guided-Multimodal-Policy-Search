[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts keep action 0 (forward thrust) for long uninterrupted stretches before any turn, building steady momentum that carries the agent past obstacles, whereas low‑reward rollouts insert actions 1 or 2 (turns or backward moves) almost immediately, breaking momentum and prompting collisions. When a turn is finally needed, the successful rollouts make only brief, low‑amplitude adjustments right after a sustained forward segment, while the poor rollouts oscillate with frequent, large‑angle turns that over‑correct the trajectory. The high‑reward trajectories also delay any backward‑or‑negative‑reward actions until the very end of the episode, using them only as a last‑resort correction, whereas the low‑reward runs intersperse backward moves throughout, causing repeated loss of control. Finally, the successful rollouts align their corrective actions with clear environmental cues (e.g., following a wall), resulting in smooth, predictable paths, while the failing rollouts apply corrections without such cues, leading to erratic, jittery motion that quickly incurs negative rewards. These timing and action‑selection differences explain why sustained forward thrust with minimal, well‑timed corrections yields much higher cumulative reward than frequent, premature turning or backtracking.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts repeatedly interleave short bursts of the forward‑thrust action (action 0) with frequent, modest corrective actions (actions 1 or 2) throughout the episode, keeping the state value within a narrow band and never allowing a long uninterrupted straight run; by contrast, low‑reward rollouts either sustain a single forward action for many steps or execute a single sharp turn late in the episode, which drives the state value outside the safe corridor and produces large negative rewards. The successful trajectories also exhibit early‑stage oscillations that continually realign the agent with the corridor walls, whereas the failing runs show a delayed or missing re‑alignment, letting the agent drift into a boundary before a sudden, high‑impact correction. Moreover, the best rollouts avoid any abrupt, high‑magnitude actions after the midpoint, maintaining smooth, incremental adjustments, while the poor rollouts contain a late, abrupt turn (often action 0 used as a sharp change) that precipitates a crash. This pattern of continuous, low‑amplitude corrections prevents collisions and sustains positive reward accumulation, explaining the stark performance gap.

[WORST POLICY]
The high‑reward rollouts all contain a brief, purposeful burst of forward thrust (action 2) followed almost immediately by a decisive change – either a turn (action 0/1) or a halt – which ends the episode after only a few timesteps, whereas the low‑reward rollouts keep the same forward action for most of the trajectory and only terminate after a long, meandering sequence. In the better rollouts the state value changes smoothly and only once or twice, showing minimal oscillation, while the poorer rollouts exhibit repeated large swings and over‑corrections in the state trace. The high‑reward trajectories also show an early, single direction change that prevents the agent from lingering near walls or corridors; the low‑reward runs lack such timely turns and instead bounce repeatedly against obstacles. Because reward is accumulated negatively over time, the short, controlled episodes incur far fewer penalty steps than the prolonged, oscillatory ones, directly explaining the reward gap. This pattern of “quick decisive action → early termination” versus “persistent constant action → extended chaotic wandering” is the primary behavioral difference between the two groups.
