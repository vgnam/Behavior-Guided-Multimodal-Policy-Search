High‑reward rollouts keep action 0 (forward thrust) for long uninterrupted stretches before any turn, building steady momentum that carries the agent past obstacles, whereas low‑reward rollouts insert actions 1 or 2 (turns or backward moves) almost immediately, breaking momentum and prompting collisions. When a turn is finally needed, the successful rollouts make only brief, low‑amplitude adjustments right after a sustained forward segment, while the poor rollouts oscillate with frequent, large‑angle turns that over‑correct the trajectory. The high‑reward trajectories also delay any backward‑or‑negative‑reward actions until the very end of the episode, using them only as a last‑resort correction, whereas the low‑reward runs intersperse backward moves throughout, causing repeated loss of control. Finally, the successful rollouts align their corrective actions with clear environmental cues (e.g., following a wall), resulting in smooth, predictable paths, while the failing rollouts apply corrections without such cues, leading to erratic, jittery motion that quickly incurs negative rewards. These timing and action‑selection differences explain why sustained forward thrust with minimal, well‑timed corrections yields much higher cumulative reward than frequent, premature turning or backtracking.
