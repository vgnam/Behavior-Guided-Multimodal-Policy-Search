[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts consistently string together longer uninterrupted forward segments (action 0/1) before executing a decisive, single‑direction turn, whereas low‑reward runs interleave turns with forward moves every few steps, creating a rapid back‑and‑forth pattern. In the successful trajectories the agent often makes a brief corrective backward step right at the start, then commits to forward motion that carries it through a corner, while the poorer runs either skip this alignment or repeat backward adjustments throughout the episode. The high‑reward behavior shows a clear alternation of “forward → turn → forward” with each turn followed by several steps of forward progress, reducing wasted thrust and keeping the agent moving toward the goal; the low‑reward episodes display clusters of consecutive turns or immediate reversals that stall progress. Because sustained forward progress accrues larger per‑step rewards, the disciplined forward‑then‑turn rhythm of the high‑reward rollouts yields higher cumulative return, whereas the erratic, overly frequent turning of the low‑reward rollouts dissipates reward potential.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts all begin with an immediate forward action (action 0) that pushes the agent into the high‑reward band (state ≈ 1.0–1.5) within the first few frames, whereas the low‑reward episodes either linger near the start or spend their early steps turning (actions 1/2) before reaching that region. Once inside the favorable band, the successful runs use only brief, corrective turns—typically a single turn every 5–7 steps—to realign with the corridor, while the poor rollouts exhibit continuous back‑and‑forth turning, producing long sequences of oscillatory actions that keep the state swinging between positive and negative reward zones. The high‑reward trajectories also limit the duration of any corrective phase to under 10 steps before resuming forward motion, preventing the agent from drifting into dead‑ends or colliding with walls; in contrast, the low‑reward examples extend corrective phases for many timesteps, often resulting in early termination. This disciplined pattern of front‑loaded forward thrust followed by short, purposeful adjustments keeps the agent on a smooth path through the maze, whereas the opposite—delayed forward motion and prolonged, unnecessary turning—creates instability and large negative penalties, accounting for the stark reward gap.

[WORST POLICY]
The high‑reward rollouts all contain a brief, purposeful forward thrust followed quickly by a change of action (usually a stop or turn) that limits the length of the forward excursion, whereas the low‑reward rollouts keep the forward‑action (action 2) active for the entire episode and never switch to a braking or corrective command. This early action switch in the better rollouts prevents the state value from drifting far from the start and curtails the large oscillations that dominate the poorer runs. Because the high‑reward episodes terminate after a short, controlled movement, they avoid the cumulative penalty incurred by repeatedly overshooting and correcting, which is what drives the very negative scores in the low‑reward cases. Consequently, the presence of an early corrective action—and the corresponding reduction in sustained forward motion—appears to be the key behavioral difference that yields the modestly higher rewards.
