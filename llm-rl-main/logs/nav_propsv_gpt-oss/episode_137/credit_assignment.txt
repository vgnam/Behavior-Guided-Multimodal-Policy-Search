[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts consistently introduce a sharp right turn within the first few frames and then resume sustained forward motion, creating a rhythm of “forward → turn → forward” that keeps the agent centered in the corridor; low‑reward rollouts either never turn or delay the first turn until very late, running straight into a wall or boundary. The successful runs also display brief, high‑frequency corrective oscillations (alternating between slight left/right actions) immediately after each turn, which quickly dampen the deviation and stabilize the trajectory, whereas the poor runs show either no such micro‑adjustments or only a single large correction that overshoots. Timing is another key difference: in the high‑reward episodes the agent executes its second turn shortly after the first (within a few frames), maintaining momentum through a series of bends, while the low‑reward episodes either linger in prolonged straight segments or make a sudden, isolated turn that coincides with a large negative reward. Finally, the high‑reward behavior avoids long periods of unmodulated forward thrust that lead to early termination, instead interleaving turns and modest speed changes, whereas the low‑reward trajectories feature continuous unidirectional thrust until a catastrophic collision ends the episode. These patterns together keep the agent navigating the maze efficiently and prevent the fatal overshoots that dominate the low‑reward rollouts.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts all begin with a long, uninterrupted stretch of forward actions that builds a large positive reward before any turning is attempted, whereas the low‑reward runs either truncate this forward streak early or replace it with a sudden, large‑amplitude turn that immediately drives the state into a failure region. When a turn is needed, the successful episodes insert a single corrective action (left or right) and then promptly resume forward motion, keeping the magnitude of the turn small and the timing early enough to re‑stabilize the trajectory; the poor episodes either over‑rotate with multiple successive turn actions or delay the correction until the agent is already deviating sharply, producing oscillatory drift and eventual divergence. The high‑reward trajectories also maintain relatively smooth state values, avoiding prolonged back‑and‑forth oscillations, while the low‑reward paths spend a substantial portion of the episode cycling between opposite directions, which dissipates accumulated reward and raises the chance of a terminal crash. Finally, the best rollouts never execute a sharp, late‑stage turn that pushes the agent off the intended corridor, whereas the worst rollouts often finish with an abrupt turn that terminates the episode with a large negative penalty, directly accounting for the observed reward gap.

[WORST POLICY]
The high‑reward rollouts all contain a brief, purposeful deviation from the default forward thrust (action 2) – usually a single “0” (stop/turn) applied after a few hundred steps that abruptly reduces the state value and prevents the agent from drifting into the far wall, whereas the low‑reward rollouts never use action 0 and remain at action 2 for the entire episode. In the better runs this corrective action is timed just before the state value begins a runaway increase, creating a short stabilising dip that limits the later overshoot; the worst runs show a monotonic rise in the state value with no such dip. Moreover, the higher‑reward trajectories display an early phase of relatively smooth, monotonic growth (steady forward motion) followed by a controlled correction, while the low‑reward trajectories are dominated from the start by large, erratic swings in the state value with no clear transition to a steadier regime. This combination of an initial stable push and a timely corrective stop curtails the excessive oscillations that otherwise accumulate large negative rewards, explaining why the occasional “0” and its proper placement yield noticeably better returns.
