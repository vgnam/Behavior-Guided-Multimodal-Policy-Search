[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts consistently begin with immediate movement that alternates short forward actions (action 0) and modest turns (actions 1 or 2) within the first few steps, creating a rapid micro‑correction rhythm; they never linger, and they avoid any single, large‑angle turn that stalls the agent. Low‑reward rollouts either start with a slow or negligible drift, remain stationary for several frames, or execute one abrupt sharp turn after a straight segment, which often leads to a wall collision or dead‑end. The early, frequent switching between forward and slight turn actions keeps the agent continuously sampling the corridor space and correcting its heading before errors accumulate, thereby maintaining high cumulative reward. When this oscillatory control pattern is missing or replaced by a prolonged inertial phase or a sudden overshoot, trajectory errors grow unchecked, causing inefficient navigation or crashes and producing the observed low or negative rewards.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts keep long uninterrupted runs of action 0 (steady forward motion) and only insert brief, well‑timed bursts of actions 1 or 2 to correct drift, whereas low‑reward episodes either terminate after a single large jump (often a lone action 2) or constantly flip between 0, 1 and 2 without a stable forward segment. The successful trajectories also begin by orienting toward the maze entrance and then follow a smooth, monotonic path through corridors, while the failures show early, abrupt changes in state value that push the agent out of bounds. In the good rollouts the corrective turns are spaced out (e.g., a few frames of 0 followed by one frame of 1/2), providing enough momentum to stay on the intended lane; the bad rollouts exhibit jerky, reactive oscillations where a turn is immediately followed by another, causing over‑correction. This disciplined pattern preserves progress toward the goal and avoids collisions, which directly translates into the large positive cumulative reward versus the large penalties seen in the erratic, premature‑termination runs.

[WORST POLICY]
The high‑reward rollouts all contain an early, purposeful adjustment of the heading—either a small turn toward the corridor’s bend or a brief change in the thrust direction—followed several timesteps later by a sudden switch to action 0 that instantly collapses the state value and produces a large negative penalty; this decisive but mistimed maneuver creates a short burst of progress that is then punished, yielding a comparatively higher (less negative) total reward. By contrast, the low‑reward rollouts never exhibit such a heading change; they keep action 2 constant for the entire episode, producing a steady but oscillatory motion that never triggers the catastrophic drop but also never makes meaningful progress toward the goal, so the accumulated penalty stays far larger. The presence of an early alignment step in the good runs gives them a brief advantage over the aimless wobble of the bad runs, while the later abrupt action reversal is the key event that separates the two reward regimes. Consequently, the combination of an initial directed turn and a late, sharp action flip is the behavioral signature that consistently appears in the higher‑reward trajectories and is absent—or replaced by uniform, unchanging actions—in the lowest‑reward ones.
