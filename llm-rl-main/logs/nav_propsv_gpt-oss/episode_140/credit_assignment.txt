[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts spend the majority of their horizon executing the forward‑motion action (the “2” action) and only introduce a turn after a long stretch of steady progress, whereas low‑reward rollouts insert the reverse/acceleration action (“0”) within the first few dozen steps, causing an abrupt change in state and immediate termination. The successful trajectories also follow a pattern of a single, well‑timed sharp right turn followed by a rapid return to forward motion, keeping the agent’s velocity aligned with the corridor; the failing runs either omit that turn or perform it too late, then over‑correct with a sudden backward thrust. Minor oscillations and short pauses appear in the high‑reward cases but never lead to a full reversal, while the low‑reward cases exhibit repeated forward–backward oscillations that grow into a large divergence. This contrast suggests that sustained forward thrust combined with limited, purposeful turning preserves stability and avoids collisions, whereas early or excessive reverse actions and erratic over‑corrections quickly drive the agent out of the safe region and incur large negative rewards.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts consistently begin with a short burst of sustained forward actions (action 0) that quickly accumulates positive reward before any turn is made, whereas the low‑reward episodes either start with minimal forward movement or immediately introduce erratic turns that prevent early reward buildup. Throughout the successful runs the agent intersperses turns sparingly and only at clear corner points, keeping the turn‑to‑forward ratio low; in contrast, the failing runs exhibit rapid, repeated switches between backward (action 1) and sideways (action 2) inputs, producing high‑frequency oscillations that destabilize the trajectory. The good rollouts also show timely corrective adjustments—short, purposeful turn sequences that realign the agent with the maze wall—while the poor rollouts over‑correct, swinging back and forth around a point and eventually drifting into walls. This disciplined pattern of front‑loaded forward motion, limited and well‑timed turning, and avoidance of prolonged backward/sideways bursts lets the agent stay on a productive path, whereas the opposite behavior leads to loss of control and large negative penalties.

[WORST POLICY]
The high‑reward rollouts all contain an early change of action – after one or two thrust steps the agent turns (or at least varies its heading) to follow a wall or negotiate a corner, whereas the low‑reward rollouts keep applying the same forward‑thrust action (action 2) for the entire episode. This brief turning maneuver in the high‑reward cases limits the time the agent spends moving forward before it collides, so the episode ends quickly and the cumulative penalty stays relatively small. By contrast, the low‑reward trajectories sustain forward thrust for many steps, producing long, oscillatory drifts and repeated over‑corrections that add up large negative rewards before termination. The presence of an early directional adjustment and the resulting short episode length therefore explains why the high‑reward rollouts achieve less negative scores than the continuously‑thrust, long‑lasting low‑reward rollouts.
