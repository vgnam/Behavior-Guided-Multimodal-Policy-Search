[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts contain long uninterrupted stretches of the forward action (action 0) followed by a single, well‑timed sharp turn (usually action 2) at the maze junction, whereas low‑reward rollouts break the forward motion with many premature or back‑to‑back turns. In the high‑reward cases the decisive turn is delayed until after roughly 10–15 steps, aligning the agent with the inner corridor, while the low‑reward runs turn within the first few steps and waste forward progress. When a turn does occur in the high‑reward trajectories the agent immediately resumes action 0, limiting the number of consecutive turn actions that generate negative –0.5 rewards; the low‑reward trajectories often string together multiple turn actions, prolonging periods of penalty. High‑reward rollouts also include occasional larger corrective turns that re‑center the agent on the optimal path, whereas the low‑reward rollouts rely on many small, hesitant adjustments that keep the agent drifting off‑center. These timing and action‑selection differences yield more frequent +5 reward events and smoother advancement through the maze, producing the observed reward gap.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts repeatedly begin with a long uninterrupted stretch of action 0 (steady forward motion) that keeps the agent centered in the corridor before any turn is executed, whereas low‑reward episodes introduce sharp turns (actions 1 or 2) almost immediately after start or after only a few forward steps, causing the vehicle to hit walls. When a turn is needed in the successful runs, it occurs at a clearly defined waypoint—often after the agent has traversed a straight segment of four or more frames—so the turn is timed to align with a corridor opening; the failing runs turn at arbitrary times (e.g., t ≈ 100) without such spatial cues, leading to mis‑alignment and collisions. The high‑reward trajectories also exhibit brief, controlled corrections after a turn (single corrective actions that quickly re‑establish forward motion), while the low‑reward trajectories show prolonged oscillations or repeated opposite‑direction actions that over‑correct and drive the agent off‑track. Finally, successful rollouts maintain a smooth velocity profile, avoiding successive back‑and‑forth motions; the poor rollouts display jerky forward‑backward swings that waste steps and generate negative rewards. These timing and action‑selection differences keep the agent inside safe passages and prevent crashes, directly accounting for the large reward gap.

[WORST POLICY]
The rollouts that earned the relatively higher rewards all contain a clear mid‑episode decision to break the monotonous forward thrust (action 2) – either by switching to a stop (action 0) or by taking a lateral action that steers the agent away from a wall – whereas the low‑reward episodes keep action 2 for the entire length of the episode. This timely switch truncates the period during which the agent accrues large negative step penalties and often prevents a prolonged collision with the maze boundaries. The higher‑reward trajectories also exhibit an early, purposeful rightward or turning motion, giving the agent a brief chance to stay inside the corridor before the inevitable failure, while the low‑reward runs show no such heading changes and simply drift forward in place. Because the worst policy is penalised heavily for each step spent in an unsafe region, the brief corrective maneuver and early termination seen in the better rollouts reduce the cumulative penalty and therefore produce the less negative returns.
