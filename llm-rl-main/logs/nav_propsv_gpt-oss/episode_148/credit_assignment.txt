[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts all begin with an extended stretch of the forward action (action 0) that carries the agent deep into the maze before any turn is attempted, whereas the low‑reward episodes either turn immediately (action 1 or 2) or execute only a single forward step before changing direction. When turns do occur in the successful runs they are brief, low‑amplitude corrective actions placed after a long forward run and are timed near the natural corners of the layout; in the failing runs the same turns happen early or are followed by large, rapid back‑and‑forth adjustments that push the agent into walls. The successful trajectories also show smooth, monotonic state progressions with only minor oscillations, while the poor trajectories exhibit sudden jumps in state values, indicating abrupt loss of control. Finally, high‑reward behavior tends to postpone any negative‑reward actions until after the agent has already accrued a large positive reward buffer, whereas low‑reward rollouts incur negative rewards almost immediately, preventing recovery. These timing and action‑selection differences explain why the former accumulate large positive returns and the latter collapse quickly.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts all begin with a stretch of forward actions (action 0 or 2) that steadily push the state toward the goal, and they insert brief corrective actions (1 or 2) exactly when the state approaches the upper bound, keeping the trajectory confined to a narrow band (≈0.7–2.2). By contrast, low‑reward episodes either make a single large jump that thrusts the state far outside that band or never apply any corrective action, so the agent drifts unchecked until termination. The successful policies also schedule their corrections early—typically within the first few hundred timesteps—whereas the failing rollouts either postpone corrections until after a major deviation or omit them altogether. This disciplined timing prevents collisions and maintains positive per‑step rewards, while the erratic, uncorrected motions of the low‑reward rollouts generate the large negative rewards observed.

[WORST POLICY]
The high‑reward rollouts all contain an early, purposeful change in action – typically a single backward thrust (action 2) followed quickly by a forward thrust or a sharp turn (action 0) – after which the episode ends within only a few steps, whereas the low‑reward rollouts persist with the same forward‑thrust action 2 for the entire episode. This brief, varied action sequence prevents the agent from entering the long‑lasting oscillatory loops that dominate the low‑reward trajectories, where constant action 2 produces repeated over‑corrections and back‑and‑forth motion. By switching actions almost immediately, the better rollouts avoid the accumulation of large negative penalties associated with prolonged unstable control. In contrast, the low‑reward runs never introduce a corrective action, so the agent’s state value swings wildly and the cumulative reward stays highly negative. Consequently, the presence of an early corrective maneuver and the resulting short episode length are the key behavioral differences that drive the reward gap.
