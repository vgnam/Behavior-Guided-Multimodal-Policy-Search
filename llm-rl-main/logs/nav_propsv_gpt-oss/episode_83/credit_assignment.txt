[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts consistently open with an immediate forward thrust (action 0) followed within the first two–three steps by a small corrective turn (action 1 or 2), establishing a heading that is then refined by a rapid alternation of forward moves and brief adjustments; low‑reward episodes either linger without any forward motion (e.g., the −212.5 rollout) or sustain long un‑interrupted sequences of a single action, so the agent never gains early momentum or corrects its orientation. In the successful runs the corrective turns occur early and are modest, producing a “cautious‑exploration” rhythm that gradually stabilizes the trajectory, whereas the poorer runs either delay turns until much later or apply overly aggressive turns that produce back‑and‑forth oscillations without steady progress. The high‑reward trajectories also feature periodic short bursts of forward motion punctuated by brief turns, keeping the state value monotically increasing, while the low‑reward cases show either flat or decreasing state values during prolonged forward segments, indicating wasted or regressive movement. Finally, the best rollouts use a pattern of alternating left‑ and right‑leaning adjustments that keep the agent centered in the corridor, whereas the low‑reward examples either repeat the same turning direction or fail to turn at all, causing the agent to collide with walls or stall, which directly depresses cumulative reward.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts keep action 0 (forward) dominant for long stretches, only inserting brief turn actions (1 or 2) after several stable steps and immediately resuming forward motion, which preserves a steady forward momentum and avoids collisions. In contrast, low‑reward rollouts begin with or quickly resort to aggressive thrusts (action 2) or abrupt turns that produce large state jumps, leaving little time for corrective fine‑tuning and often driving the agent into walls. The successful trajectories also exhibit tightly timed, small‑amplitude corrections—turns are introduced just enough to negotiate corners and are followed by an instant return to action 0—whereas the poor trajectories show over‑corrections and erratic back‑and‑forth actions that amplify oscillations and destabilize the path. By maintaining continuous forward progress and limiting aggressive, poorly timed maneuvers, the high‑reward rollouts accumulate positive rewards, while the low‑reward rollouts lose reward through rapid, uncontrolled movements that trigger negative penalties or terminations.

[WORST POLICY]
The high‑reward rollouts all contain an early deviation from pure forward thrust – the agent issues at least one turn action (often action 0) within the first few timesteps to align with a wall and begin a brief wall‑following segment, whereas the low‑reward rollouts keep applying the same forward action (action 2) for the entire episode. This early turn lets the agent stay in the corridor a little longer before colliding, so the episode accumulates a less negative sum than the nonstop thrust that drives the low‑reward runs into an immediate crash. In the higher‑reward cases the agent’s speed profile shows an initial moderate thrust followed by a short period of smoother, less erratic motion, while the low‑reward trajectories are characterized by constant high‑frequency oscillations and overshoots with no purposeful slowing. Consequently, the brief phase of controlled, wall‑aligned movement in the high‑reward rollouts delays failure and yields a comparatively higher (i.e., less negative) total reward.
