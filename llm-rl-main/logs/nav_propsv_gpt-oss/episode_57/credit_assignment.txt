[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts consistently alternate a forward action (0) with rapid corrective turns (1 or 2) every few timesteps, producing short forward bursts that are immediately followed by a sharp left‑right adjustment; low‑reward runs tend to string together longer sequences of forward actions before any turn, allowing the agent to drift into walls or lose control. In the high‑reward episodes the corrective turns appear almost symmetrically (left then right, or vice‑versa), keeping the state values oscillating around a safe corridor, whereas the low‑reward episodes show asymmetric or delayed turns that push the state toward extreme values and trigger large negative rewards. Moreover, the high‑reward trajectories begin their corrective pattern almost from the start of the episode, while the low‑reward ones often delay correction until after a substantial forward thrust, at which point a collision occurs. This early, frequent “over‑correction” prevents boundary breaches and sustains positive incremental rewards, whereas the sparse or mistimed corrections in low‑reward rollouts lead to crashes or stagnation, accounting for the large reward gap.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts all begin with a brief “cautious” phase of short forward actions (action 0) followed quickly by a corrective turn (action 1 or 2) that keeps the agent hugging a wall or staying inside a narrow corridor; this oscillatory pattern repeats throughout the episode, allowing the agent to recover from overshoots and maintain a bounded state range. In contrast, the low‑reward rollouts either launch straight with a prolonged thrust (continuous action 0 or 2) or fail to execute timely corrective turns, so the state drifts rapidly toward the arena’s edges and terminates early. The successful trajectories also show frequent, small‑amplitude adjustments—brief back‑and‑forth moves that reverse direction after each aggressive thrust—whereas the failures exhibit monotonic movement with few or no reversals. Because the high‑reward policy continuously re‑orients itself, it avoids collisions and stays in rewarding zones, while the low‑reward policy’s lack of timely turns and corrective oscillations leads to uncontrolled drift and large negative penalties.

[WORST POLICY]
The high‑reward rollouts all contain an early, decisive maneuver—typically a sharp turn (action 2) within the first few frames—that quickly drives the state into a failure region and ends the episode after only a short burst of activity, whereas the low‑reward rollouts linger for many timesteps by repeatedly issuing the same forward‑type action (action 2) and oscillating back and forth without any early corrective turn. This early termination limits the accumulation of per‑step penalties, so the total negative reward is smaller. In contrast, the prolonged oscillatory pattern in the low‑reward cases keeps the agent alive longer, exposing it to repeated step‑wise costs and larger cumulative loss. Additionally, the high‑reward trajectories show a brief period of relatively smooth motion after the initial turn before colliding, while the low‑reward runs exhibit continual abrupt direction changes and over‑corrections throughout, which amplifies instability and drives the reward further down.
