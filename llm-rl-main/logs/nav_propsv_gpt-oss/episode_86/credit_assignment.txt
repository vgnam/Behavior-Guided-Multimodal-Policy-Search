[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts consistently sustain long sequences of action 0 (forward) punctuated by a single, well‑timed left turn (action 2) at each corner, keeping the agent close to the wall and preserving a steady forward momentum; low‑reward rollouts break this pattern with frequent short bursts of forward motion interrupted by rapid alternating turns or occasional backward steps (action 1), producing erratic, over‑corrected trajectories. The high‑reward episodes also avoid abrupt accelerations, instead showing gradual speed changes and smooth curvature, whereas the low‑reward cases exhibit sharp accelerations followed by sudden decelerations that lead to loss of control. When a turn is needed, the successful rollouts execute it once and then resume forward motion, while the poor rollouts often oscillate between left and right turns within a few frames, creating instability. Maintaining a consistent distance from the walls—evident in the smooth wall‑following of the high‑reward paths—prevents collisions, whereas the low‑reward agents drift into narrow corridors and hit obstacles. These differences in action continuity, turn timing, and speed modulation directly translate into longer, collision‑free trajectories and therefore higher cumulative rewards.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts keep a long, uninterrupted stretch of the forward‑motion action (0), only inserting a brief corrective action (1 or 2) when the state begins to drift, which lets the agent steadily advance while staying inside the corridor. In contrast, low‑reward rollouts either fire a forward action at the wrong moment or replace it with sudden aggressive turns or backward moves, causing the agent to overshoot, collide with walls, or terminate early. The successful episodes also delay any large‑angle corrections until well after the initial forward surge, whereas the poor episodes introduce sharp direction changes within the first few frames, creating instability that quickly reduces reward. By maintaining forward momentum and using minimal, well‑timed corrections, the high‑reward behavior preserves positive reward accumulation, while the erratic, mistimed actions in the low‑reward rollouts generate negative rewards and early terminations.

[WORST POLICY]
The high‑reward rollouts all contain a brief, modest forward thrust followed quickly by either a change of action (often to 0) or a small corrective turn, so the agent’s state only drifts a little before termination; in contrast, the low‑reward rollouts keep action 2 applied for many timesteps, producing long stretches of unchecked forward motion that generate large overshoots, oscillations and repeated over‑corrections. This early switch away from the constant thrust limits the cumulative distance travelled and therefore reduces the magnitude of the negative penalties that accrue in the stochastic environment. Moreover, the high‑reward episodes show occasional orientation adjustments (turns) that keep the agent from colliding with obstacles, whereas the low‑reward episodes show virtually no heading changes, causing the agent to repeatedly hit walls or undesirable regions. By terminating sooner and correcting its trajectory, the higher‑reward rollouts avoid the severe penalty spikes that dominate the low‑reward trajectories.
