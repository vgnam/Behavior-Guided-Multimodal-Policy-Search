[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts keep a long uninterrupted stretch of action 0 (the forward thrust) right after the start, only inserting a single corrective action (1 or 2) when a wall is imminent, whereas low‑reward episodes intersperse actions 1 and 2 almost every frame, producing rapid back‑and‑forth thrusts. The high‑reward trajectories show a smooth, monotonic increase in the state‑value signal during forward phases, while the low‑reward trajectories exhibit large, erratic jumps in state value whenever an over‑correction occurs. Finally, the successful rollouts reserve the occasional turn for a clear geometric cue (e.g., a right‑angle corner), whereas the failures turn at ambiguous points, causing the agent to lose alignment and collide, directly lowering the episodic reward.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts keep long uninterrupted stretches of action 0, generating steady +5 rewards while the state remains within a narrow band, and they only turn (actions 1 or 2) at well‑timed corner points where the trajectory must change. In contrast, low‑reward rollouts contain isolated or premature turns, often followed immediately by another action, producing abrupt state jumps, large negative rewards, and early terminations. The successful runs also show a pattern of small corrective adjustments after each turn, allowing the agent to re‑stabilise before proceeding forward, whereas the poor runs either over‑correct (immediate opposite turns) or fail to correct at all, leading to oscillations that diverge from the corridor. By maintaining forward momentum and limiting unnecessary direction changes, the high‑reward behavior preserves proximity to the optimal path and avoids collisions, while the erratic timing and excess turning in low‑reward episodes repeatedly push the agent into unsafe states, directly reducing cumulative reward.

[WORST POLICY]
The high‑reward rollouts all contain a brief, purposeful correction early in the episode – the agent either makes a small turn or briefly switches off the forward thrust (action 0) as soon as its state begins to wobble, which prevents the oscillations from growing into large excursions. In contrast, the low‑reward rollouts keep applying the same forward thrust (action 2) for dozens or hundreds of steps, even after the state values start to overshoot, so the errors compound and the agent later crashes. The better rollouts also settle into a smooth, mostly monotonic forward progression after that early correction, whereas the poorer rollouts display repeated high‑frequency swings between high and low state values throughout the episode. Finally, the higher‑reward episodes tend to terminate relatively early, cutting off the accumulation of large negative penalties, while the low‑reward episodes run much longer, allowing the divergent oscillations to produce far larger losses. These timing and action‑selection differences plausibly explain why the former achieve less negative rewards than the latter.
