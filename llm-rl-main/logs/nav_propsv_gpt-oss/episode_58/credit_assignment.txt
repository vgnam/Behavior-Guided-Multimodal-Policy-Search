[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts all begin with a block of consecutive forward actions (action 0) that builds speed and pushes the state value upward before any turning maneuver is attempted, whereas low‑reward runs either turn immediately or interleave turns with forward moves, causing early loss of momentum. When a turn (action 1 or 2) does occur in the successful episodes, it is followed almost instantly by another forward action, creating a single‑step correction instead of the rapid back‑and‑forth switching that dominates the failing rollouts. The high‑reward trajectories show a monotonic increase in the state variable throughout most of the episode, indicating steady progress toward the goal, while the low‑reward ones contain plateaus or declines in state before termination. Additionally, the successful rollouts keep oscillatory behavior to brief, isolated episodes rather than continuous jitter, which reduces the chance of colliding with walls or destabilizing the agent. These timing‑wise disciplined forward‑first, turn‑then‑recover patterns maintain velocity and spatial alignment, directly translating into higher accumulated rewards.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts keep long stretches of the forward action (0) before inserting any turn, and when a correction is needed they apply a single, brief action 1 or 2 immediately after a small deviation is observed, preserving forward momentum. In contrast, low‑reward episodes either rush into a turn without first stabilising forward motion or accelerate aggressively, producing a rapid succession of large‑amplitude actions that drive the agent into walls or off‑track. The successful runs also exhibit a pattern of early, modest adjustments—tiny turns right after the first few frames—whereas the poor runs delay correction until the error is large, resulting in a sudden, high‑penalty collision. By limiting the duration of corrective bursts and spacing them predictably, the high‑reward behavior maintains a steady accumulation of positive reward, while the erratic timing and magnitude of turns in the low‑reward cases wipe out that gain.

[WORST POLICY]
The high‑reward rollouts all begin with an immediate, strong forward thrust (action 2) and then terminate almost straightaway, so the episode ends after only a few steps and the agent accrues very little per‑step penalty; by contrast, the low‑reward rollouts sustain forward motion for many frames, interspersed with oscillatory corrections and only later execute a sudden turn or stop (actions 0 or 1) that finally crashes after a long trajectory, piling up a large cumulative loss. In the better rollouts the agent rarely switches away from the initial thrust, avoiding the over‑corrections and zig‑zagging that dominate the poorer rollouts, where frequent action changes produce jittery, back‑and‑forth movements. Because the high‑reward episodes end quickly, they expose the agent to fewer state‑value fluctuations and less opportunity for destabilising feedback, whereas the low‑reward episodes accumulate many such fluctuations before the fatal maneuver. This timing difference—early, decisive forward acceleration versus prolonged, erratic control—plausibly accounts for the roughly four‑fold reward gap.
