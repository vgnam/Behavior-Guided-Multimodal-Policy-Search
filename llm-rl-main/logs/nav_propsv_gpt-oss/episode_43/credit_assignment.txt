[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts consistently begin with a sustained forward action (action 0) that lasts for many timesteps before any turn is taken, whereas the low‑reward runs intersperse turns almost immediately or execute long strings of turning actions (actions 1 or 2) right from the start. When a turn does occur in the successful episodes, it is brief—typically a single timestep—followed quickly by a return to forward motion, while the poorer episodes show clusters of consecutive turns or rapid back‑and‑forth oscillations that destabilize the trajectory. The good rollouts also exhibit a “recover‑fast” pattern: after an abrupt change in state value they promptly apply a corrective forward step, preventing the state from drifting far from the desired lane; the bad rollouts either fail to correct or over‑correct, producing large state spikes that precede crashes. Additionally, the high‑reward trajectories keep the magnitude of state changes modest throughout the episode, avoiding the sudden large jumps seen near the end of the low‑reward runs, which are typically followed by large negative rewards. These timing and action‑selection differences plausibly explain why the former maintain stable progress and accumulate reward, while the latter lose control and incur penalties.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts all begin with a long stretch of the forward‑movement action (action 0) that propels the agent deep into the maze before any turning action is taken, whereas low‑reward episodes either start with a backward jump or introduce a turn almost immediately, causing the agent to leave the reward corridor. When a turn is needed, the successful rollouts delay it until the forward streak is complete and then execute a single, sharp action 2 followed by a brief corrective action 1, while the poor rollouts repeatedly alternate between actions 1 and 2, creating rapid, uncontrolled oscillations. The high‑reward trajectories also keep backward motions (action 1 that reduces the state) to a minimum and only use them as short, purposeful nudges to re‑align, whereas the low‑reward episodes contain extended periods of backward or lateral movement that push the state away from the goal region. By maintaining sustained forward progress and limiting noisy direction changes, the high‑reward rollouts stay within high‑reward zones longer and avoid collisions, directly accounting for their much larger cumulative rewards.

[WORST POLICY]
The high‑reward rollouts all contain a brief period of modest, steady actions (often a single “2” or a short sequence of gentle adjustments) early in the episode, after which the agent makes a decisive, larger‑magnitude thrust at a specific time step (typically around t≈20–30) that carries it toward the goal before the episode ends; the low‑reward rollouts lack this timed “big push,” instead applying the same action 2 continuously for the whole horizon without ever escalating the control signal. In the better runs the agent also exhibits a clear change of direction exactly once—switching from a forward‑biased motion to a rapid downward or sideways maneuver—whereas the poorer runs oscillate repeatedly, reversing direction many times and never settling on a single decisive turn. The high‑reward trajectories show a short, purposeful pause or small corrective step right before the large thrust, providing a stable launch condition, while the low‑reward episodes contain no such pause and instead display immediate, unchecked acceleration that leads to overshoot and repeated collisions. These timing and magnitude differences give the high‑reward agent just enough momentum to reach the target region before instability grows, whereas the always‑high‑frequency, unmodulated actions in the low‑reward rollouts cause chaotic oscillations and early termination with large penalties.
