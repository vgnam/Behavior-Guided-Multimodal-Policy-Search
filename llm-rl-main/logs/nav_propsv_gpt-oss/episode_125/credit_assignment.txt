[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts all begin with a brief forward thrust (action 0) followed almost immediately by a purposeful right‑turn (action 1 or 2), positioning the agent on the corridor’s correct heading before it travels far; low‑reward runs either stay straight for too long or turn late, causing them to run into walls or exit the arena. The successful episodes break their forward motion into short, positive‑reward bursts that are punctuated by quick, small corrective actions, whereas the failures exhibit long, uninterrupted forward drifts that accumulate negative rewards and end in crashes. When a correction is needed, the high‑reward policy applies a single, decisive turn rather than a series of oscillating back‑and‑forth adjustments, which keeps the trajectory smooth and avoids the over‑correction seen in the low‑reward trajectories. Backward or negative‑reward actions (e.g., action 2 that pulls the agent away from progress) appear only fleetingly in the top rollouts, while the poor rollouts contain repeated backward steps that waste time and push the agent toward failure states. Finally, the best rollouts maintain a consistent safe distance from walls—evidenced by steady positive rewards for moving away from boundaries—whereas the worst rollouts repeatedly approach or collide with walls, instantly generating large negative penalties.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts consistently begin with a few consecutive forward actions (action 0) that build momentum, then execute a brief, decisive turn before immediately resuming forward motion, whereas low‑reward rollouts either start mis‑aligned or spend many steps alternating between forward and turn actions, creating prolonged oscillations. In the successful trajectories the agent times its turns at clear junctions and limits the duration of each turn to a single step, quickly re‑stabilising its heading; the poorer runs linger in turning states or over‑correct, causing drift and repeated re‑orientation. This disciplined pattern keeps the agent moving toward the goal most of the episode, accumulating positive reward per step, while the erratic, over‑correcting behavior wastes steps and often leads to collisions that terminate the episode with large penalties. Consequently, the presence of sustained forward bursts punctuated by short, well‑timed turns explains the large reward gap between the two groups.

[WORST POLICY]
The high‑reward rollouts all contain an early, purposeful change of action – typically a sharp left or right turn within the first few frames – that steers the agent away from the nearest wall before it accelerates forward, whereas the low‑reward rollouts persist with the same forward‑thrust action (action 2) for the entire episode and never introduce a corrective turn. This early corrective turn gives the high‑reward trajectories a brief period of stable navigation and keeps the state value in a safer range, while the low‑reward agents remain trapped in large oscillations that repeatedly push them into walls. Additionally, the high‑reward episodes show brief bursts of acceleration followed by immediate deceleration or a stop (action 0), indicating an attempt to modulate speed, whereas the low‑reward runs exhibit continuous, unmodulated forward motion with no speed‑adjustment signals. The combination of early directional correction and intermittent speed control reduces wall collisions and limits the cumulative penalty, explaining why those patterns yield less negative rewards than the relentless, monotonic thrust seen in the low‑reward rollouts.
