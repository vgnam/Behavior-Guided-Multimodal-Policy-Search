[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts consistently begin with a sustained stretch of forward actions (action 0) that builds a large positive reward before any turn is made, and whenever a negative reward appears they execute a single corrective turn (action 1 or 2) almost immediately and then resume forward motion, creating a clean “forward‑turn‑forward” rhythm; low‑reward rollouts, by contrast, insert turns too early or chain multiple turn actions together, breaking forward momentum and accruing more negative reward. The high‑reward sequences also keep the state variable within a moderate band (roughly 0.7–3.1), indicating stable orientation, whereas low‑reward runs let the state drift far from this range, reflecting over‑correction and instability. Moreover, high‑reward agents time their turns precisely when a drop in reward is detected, minimizing the duration of the penalty, while low‑reward agents either delay the corrective turn or over‑rotate, prolonging the penalty phase. This disciplined forward‑first, quick‑correct‑return pattern lets the agent traverse distance efficiently and avoid costly oscillations, directly accounting for the observed reward gap.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts consistently begin with a sustained forward burst followed by a series of modest, right‑biased corrective turns that are spaced several timesteps apart, allowing the agent to cover distance while gently steering around obstacles; low‑reward rollouts either start with little or no forward momentum or intersperse forward steps with rapid, abrupt turns that quickly reverse direction and lead to collisions. The successful episodes maintain long uninterrupted sequences of the “move‑forward” action (often action 0) before any turn, whereas the poorer episodes flip between forward and turning actions (actions 1 or 2) almost every step, producing an oscillatory, indecisive trajectory. When a turn is required, high‑reward rollouts execute small, gradual adjustments rather than sharp, immediate rotations, which keeps the agent’s orientation stable and prevents over‑correction; the low‑reward runs display sudden, large‑angle turns that destabilize the path and cause the agent to hit walls. Finally, high‑reward trajectories exhibit a persistent forward drive even after minor deviations, quickly re‑aligning and continuing toward the goal, while low‑reward trajectories lose forward progress after each correction, resulting in wasted steps and premature terminations.

[WORST POLICY]
The high‑reward rollouts all contain at least one early change of action (a brief turn or speed adjustment within the first few steps) whereas the low‑reward episodes stick with the same action = 2 for the entire horizon, producing large, unchecked oscillations in the state variable. This early corrective maneuver in the better rollouts limits the amplitude of subsequent position swings and keeps the agent from drifting into extreme regions of the arena, while the monotonic forward thrust of the poor rollouts lets the state bounce between high and low values until a catastrophic collision occurs. Additionally, the higher‑reward traces show short periods of reduced velocity or pause after an initial thrust, giving the controller time to re‑orient, whereas the low‑reward runs maintain relentless forward motion without any damping, amplifying instability. These timing and action‑variation differences plausibly explain why the former achieve comparatively less negative returns: they avoid the runaway oscillations that dominate the worst rollouts.
