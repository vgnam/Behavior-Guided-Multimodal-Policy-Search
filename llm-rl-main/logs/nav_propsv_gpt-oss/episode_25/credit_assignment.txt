[CURRENT POLICY]
The high‑reward rollouts all contain a regular rhythm of a short forward thrust (action 0) followed quickly—usually within one or two steps—by a single corrective turn (action 1 or 2), producing a “burst‑and‑adjust” pattern that keeps the agent steadily advancing while only briefly tweaking its heading. In contrast, the low‑reward rollouts spend longer stretches executing consecutive turns or linger in over‑correction loops before ever issuing a forward thrust, which creates erratic, jerky trajectories and wastes time away from the goal. The successful rollouts also tend to fire the forward thrust as soon as the state value rises above a modest threshold, immediately capitalising on positive momentum, whereas the poorer rollouts delay this thrust until after multiple oscillations, allowing the state to drift and reducing the chance of hitting reward‑granting waypoints. Finally, the high‑reward episodes avoid long periods of pure turning without interleaved forward moves, a behavior that the low‑reward episodes exhibit repeatedly, leading to slower progress and more negative reward spikes. This disciplined timing of forward actions and minimal, well‑placed corrections explains why the former achieve consistently higher episodic returns.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts consistently begin with a prolonged stretch of forward motion before any corrective turns, allowing the agent to accrue positive reward while staying inside the corridor, whereas low‑reward episodes abort after a single, large‑magnitude action that instantly drives the state value far from the safe region and ends the episode. In the successful runs the agent repeatedly alternates small left‑right adjustments (“oscillatory control”) after the initial progress, keeping its trajectory near the centre of the path; the failing runs show no such gradual back‑and‑forth but instead make one abrupt, over‑correcting move that pushes it into a wall or out‑of‑bounds. Timing also differs: the high‑reward trajectories spread their corrections over many timesteps, giving the policy time to recover from minor deviations, while the low‑reward trajectories apply a decisive action within the first few steps and never regain stability. This combination of early steady advancement, modest incremental corrections, and sustained engagement yields high cumulative reward, whereas immediate drastic moves and premature termination produce the large negative returns observed in the low‑reward rollouts.

[WORST POLICY]
The high‑reward rollouts all contain a **prompt, decisive change in action** – typically a sharp turn or an abrupt switch to action 0 – that forces the episode to end within only a few frames, whereas the low‑reward rollouts keep applying the same forward action (action 2) for the entire horizon and therefore accumulate far more negative step‑penalties. In the better runs the agent’s early thrust is aggressive but quickly redirected toward a wall, producing an early collision and limiting the total cost; the poorer runs show a sustained, almost monotonic forward thrust that bounces back and forth, creating long‑lasting oscillations in the state value. Moreover, the high‑reward trajectories include at least one moment of **action reversal or deceleration**, which is completely absent from the low‑reward examples that never deviate from the forward command. This early termination and occasional corrective action reduce the cumulative penalty and thus explain why those rollouts achieve markedly higher (less negative) rewards.
