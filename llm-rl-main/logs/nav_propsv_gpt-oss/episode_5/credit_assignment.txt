[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts consistently begin with an early forward thrust (action 0) followed almost immediately by a sharp corrective turn when the first negative reward appears, allowing the agent to regain a productive trajectory within just one or two steps. This rapid “negative‑reward‑then‑turn” pattern keeps the cumulative forward motion high, whereas low‑reward rollouts either linger in the negative state or postpone the correction, resulting in prolonged stagnation or drift. In the high‑reward cases the agent interleaves short bursts of forward actions with brief, decisive turns, preserving momentum while only minimally overshooting, while the low‑reward examples show longer stretches of either overly smooth hesitant moves or extended jerky thrusts that do not translate into net progress. Moreover, high‑reward trajectories exhibit a timing rhythm where each corrective turn is followed by another forward action within two steps, creating a tight forward‑corrective loop that quickly nullifies errors; the low‑reward runs lack this rhythm, often inserting multiple successive turns or idle steps before resuming forward motion. This disciplined cadence of prompt correction and immediate forward renewal plausibly yields the observed reward advantage.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts keep action 0 (forward) dominant for long stretches and insert only brief, well‑timed action 1 or 2 (turns) right when a corner is reached, producing smooth forward progress with minimal interruption. In contrast, low‑reward rollouts either execute a single forward thrust and never correct (e.g., the -111.5 episode) or spend many consecutive steps oscillating between actions 1 and 2, which prevents sustained forward motion and leads to premature failures. The successful runs also show a clear pattern of correcting only after a measurable forward run—often around the 100‑step and 200‑step marks—whereas the poor runs either turn too early, turn erratically, or fail to turn at all, causing collisions or loss of control. Maintaining forward momentum while limiting unnecessary or mistimed turning therefore explains the large reward gap between the two groups.

[WORST POLICY]
The high‑reward rollouts all contain at least one moment where the agent switches away from the default “2” command – either inserting an action 0 (or another corrective action) to break a steady forward push or delivering a brief, aggressive thrust that ends the episode quickly. This occasional action change limits the duration of the oscillatory, “2‑only” behaviour that dominates the low‑reward rollouts, where the agent never deviates from action 2 and therefore wanders erratically for many frames. Because the high‑reward episodes terminate sooner, the cumulative per‑step penalty is smaller, producing a less negative total reward. In contrast, the low‑reward trajectories exhibit sustained, repetitive forward actions with large state swings, no corrective inputs, and long‑lasting instability, which continuously accrues the −0.5 per‑step penalty and drives the reward down. Thus, intermittent action variation and early episode termination are the key behavioural differences that explain the reward gap.
