[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts all begin with a sustained forward thrust (action 0) that carries the agent a safe distance into the arena before any turning action is taken, whereas the low‑reward examples either turn or reverse almost immediately, often colliding with a wall. In the successful runs the first directional change occurs after a clear interval (roughly a few hundred timesteps) and is a single, decisive turn (action 1 or 2), while the poor runs exhibit rapid, repeated alternations between forward and backward or between different turn actions, producing jittery, over‑corrected motion. The high‑reward trajectories also reserve corrective turns for moments when the agent’s sensor readings indicate proximity to an obstacle, using brief, sharp adjustments rather than prolonged oscillations; the low‑reward episodes ignore this cue and make large, untimely swings that push the agent into corners. By maintaining momentum early and limiting corrective actions to well‑timed, short bursts, the agent stays away from collisions longer, which directly translates into the higher cumulative reward observed.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts keep a long sequence of forward actions (action 2) before any turn, using only brief, well‑timed corrective turns (actions 0/1) to steer around corners, whereas low‑reward rollouts insert a turn almost immediately or make a single large turn that produces a sudden jump in the state value. The successful runs also show smooth, incremental state reductions, avoiding any abrupt spikes in the state that would indicate a collision, while the failures contain sharp increases in the state after a turn, often followed by immediate termination. In the high‑reward cases the agent’s corrective turns are spaced out and modest, allowing it to maintain momentum and stay inside the corridor; in the low‑reward cases the turns are either too early or too aggressive, causing the agent to overshoot or hit walls. This pattern of sustained forward motion with only small, delayed adjustments plausibly yields higher cumulative reward because it preserves forward progress and prevents the large negative penalties associated with sudden, destabilizing turns.

[WORST POLICY]
The high‑reward rollouts all contain an early, decisive action that pushes the agent hard forward and then either crashes or halts within only a few steps, truncating the episode and preventing the accumulation of many time‑step penalties. By contrast, the low‑reward rollouts keep the same action (the “2” thrust) for hundreds of steps, producing long, oscillatory trajectories that wander back and forth without ever terminating quickly, so the per‑step negative cost piles up. The brief, single‑shot thrust followed by an immediate change (often to a neutral or zero action) in the better rollouts creates a sharp, early failure that caps total loss, whereas the monotonic, unchanging thrust in the poorer rollouts eliminates any opportunity for early termination. This timing difference—early abrupt termination versus prolonged oscillation—explains why the former earn considerably higher (less negative) returns.
