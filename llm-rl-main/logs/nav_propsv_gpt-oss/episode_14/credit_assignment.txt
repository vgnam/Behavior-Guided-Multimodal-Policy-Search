[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts consistently keep a sustained forward drive (the “action 2” bursts) punctuated by only brief, well‑timed corrective turns, whereas the low‑reward runs either pause too long before moving forward or execute long, rapid turn sequences that steer the agent into walls. In the successful episodes the agent’s turns (actions 0 or 1) occur immediately after a forward step and are limited to one or two frames, serving to realign with the corridor rather than to linger on a side wall; the failing episodes show turns that are either delayed until after a collision or repeated for several consecutive frames, producing over‑correction and loss of alignment. The high‑reward behavior also displays a rhythmic pattern—forward → short turn → forward—that keeps the state value centered in the maze, while the low‑reward trajectories exhibit irregular spikes in state value as the agent drifts toward boundaries before crashing. These timing and action‑selection differences plausibly explain the reward gap: maintaining momentum with minimal, precisely‑timed adjustments lets the agent traverse the labyrinth efficiently, whereas excessive or mistimed turning leads to wall contacts and early episode termination.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts keep the forward action (0) for many consecutive frames before any turn, using only a single‑step turn (1 or 2) to adjust the heading and then immediately resuming forward motion, whereas low‑reward rollouts intersperse turns every few frames and often execute back‑to‑back turn actions, creating rapid oscillations. In the successful runs the occasional turn is timed after a clear stretch of forward progress, so the agent corrects only when drift is evident; the poor runs introduce turns early or repeatedly, preventing sustained forward velocity and leading to over‑correction. When a turn is needed, the high‑reward trajectories make the change brief and then quickly re‑align, while the low‑reward trajectories linger in the turned direction or swing sharply again, producing jerky trajectories that collide with walls. Finally, high‑reward agents avoid making a sharp turn near the episode’s end, preserving momentum to the goal, whereas low‑reward agents often execute a late abrupt turn that sends them out of bounds or into obstacles, causing the large negative penalties.

[WORST POLICY]
The higher‑reward rollouts all contain an early, brief directional adjustment—a sharp right turn or a short corrective thrust executed within the first few frames—that quickly changes the agent’s heading and then ends the episode, whereas the lowest‑reward rollouts never deviate from a constant forward action and therefore remain in a monotonic thrust for the entire run. This early turn in the better runs prevents the agent from drifting far into the corridor and reduces exposure to walls or obstacles, while the uniform forward thrust in the poorer runs produces long‑lasting oscillations and repeated over‑corrections. Moreover, the high‑reward trajectories show modest, isolated state changes rather than the large, repeated swings seen in the low‑reward cases, indicating a more restrained use of momentum. Because the better rollouts terminate soon after the corrective maneuver, they incur fewer penalties from collisions or boundary violations, whereas the worst rollouts accumulate large negative rewards by persisting in unstable, oscillatory motion. Consequently, the presence of an early, purposeful heading change and the avoidance of prolonged, identical actions explain the systematic reward gap.
