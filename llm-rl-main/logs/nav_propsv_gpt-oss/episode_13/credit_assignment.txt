[CURRENT POLICY]
In the high‑reward rollouts the agent always follows a negative‑reward cue with an immediate corrective turn (action 1 or 2) within one or two steps, while in the low‑reward rollouts it often ignores that cue and keeps applying forward thrust (action 0) until a crash occurs. Successful episodes interleave very short bursts of forward thrust (action 0) – typically only a few frames – with stabilising adjustments, keeping the state magnitude bounded; the failing episodes contain longer uninterrupted forward sequences that let the state drift toward walls. Whenever a positive reward (+5) is obtained, the high‑reward policy promptly executes a small compensatory action, effectively “locking” the favorable position, whereas the low‑reward policy either repeats the same action or delays correction, allowing error to accumulate. These rapid, fine‑grained corrections prevent overshoot and maintain a safe corridor through the maze, producing a much larger cumulative return. In contrast, the delayed or missing corrections in the low‑reward runs cause growing oscillations and eventual collisions, sharply lowering the episode reward.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts all feature extended sequences of the forward action (action 0) that carry the agent deep into the maze before any corrective moves, with occasional backward or turning actions (1 or 2) inserted at regular, spaced intervals to fine‑tune the trajectory; this steady forward drive yields large positive rewards while the timed corrections prevent drift into walls. By contrast, low‑reward rollouts either launch into an immediate forward thrust that ends abruptly in a collision, or they swing erratically between actions with no prolonged forward segment, producing frequent negative or modest rewards. The successful policy therefore couples long‑lasting forward momentum with deliberately timed, modest adjustments, whereas the failures rely on premature, unstructured action changes that destabilize navigation. This difference in action sequencing—sustained forward thrust versus hasty or chaotic switching—directly explains the large gap in episodic reward.

[WORST POLICY]
The high‑reward rollouts all contain at least one deliberate change of action – typically a brief turn (action 2) followed shortly by a stop or reversal (action 0) – whereas the low‑reward rollouts keep the same forward action (2) for the entire episode. This intermittent corrective action lets the agent pull away from walls or abort a looming collision, truncating the episode before the large penalty incurred by a crash. In the high‑reward cases the agent also initiates an early, sharp deviation from the wall‑hugging path, creating a short period of safer, slower movement that reduces cumulative negative reward. By contrast, the low‑reward trajectories show a monotonic forward thrust with no such adjustments, leading to prolonged oscillations and eventual impact that drive the reward far lower. The presence of timely action switches and early directional corrections therefore appears to be the key behavioral difference that mitigates the worst‑policy’s penalty.
