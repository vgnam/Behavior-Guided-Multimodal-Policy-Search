[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts all begin with a sustained stretch of forward‑type actions (typically action 0 or 2) that keep the state in a moderate range for dozens of timesteps before any sharp turn is introduced, whereas the low‑reward episodes either start with an immediate large‑magnitude action or make a sudden turn within the first few steps, causing a rapid state drop or collision. The successful trajectories also delay corrective turns until after a clear forward momentum has been built up, timing the turn only after the agent has accumulated positive reward for a substantial interval; the failing runs show erratic, back‑and‑forth or over‑corrected turns almost at every timestep, never allowing a stable forward phase. Moreover, high‑reward rollouts avoid abrupt state jumps—state values fluctuate smoothly between roughly 0.4 and 2.5—while the low‑reward cases exhibit spikes that take the state to extremes (near 0 or > 8) and then reverse direction abruptly, indicating loss of control. These patterns suggest that maintaining prolonged, stable forward motion and postponing sharp corrective actions prevents collisions and keeps the agent in rewarding regions of the environment, whereas early or frequent aggressive maneuvers lead to instability and large negative rewards.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts all begin with an extended stretch of the forward action (action 0) that lasts for several hundred timesteps, allowing the agent’s state value to rise steadily before any turn is attempted; in contrast, low‑reward rollouts switch to a turn or a different action within the first few steps, causing an immediate drop in state value and early termination. The successful runs also exhibit only occasional, well‑timed corrective actions (actions 1 or 2) that are spaced far enough apart to let the forward momentum continue, whereas the failing runs display rapid, back‑to‑back switches between actions, producing jittery, oscillatory motion that quickly drives the agent into walls. When turns do occur in high‑reward trajectories they happen near clear environmental landmarks (e.g., corners) and are followed by a brief re‑stabilisation period, while low‑reward trajectories make abrupt, mistimed turns that never allow the agent to recover a stable heading. This pattern of sustained forward progress punctuated by sparse, purposeful corrections preserves cumulative positive reward, whereas the erratic, premature turning and over‑correction in the low‑reward cases generate negative rewards and early episode collapse.

[WORST POLICY]
The better rollouts all end with an early, decisive turn (action 0) after only one or two forward moves (action 2), causing the episode to terminate within a handful of steps, whereas the poor rollouts never introduce a corrective turn and keep applying action 2 for the entire horizon. This early turn creates a sudden state jump that stops further interaction with the environment, limiting the accumulation of negative step‑wise rewards; the long‑running, purely forward runs in the low‑reward cases produce many oscillations and large state excursions that continually add penalty. Moreover, the high‑reward trajectories show a brief, focused burst of motion followed by immediate failure, while the low‑reward trajectories exhibit sustained, erratic oscillatory motion over many timesteps. The combination of a short episode length and an early corrective action therefore explains why the “high‑reward” rollouts receive far less negative total reward than the prolonged, turn‑free low‑reward rollouts.
