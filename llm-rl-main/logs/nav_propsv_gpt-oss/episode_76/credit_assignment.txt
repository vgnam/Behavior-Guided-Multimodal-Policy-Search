[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts consistently feature an early, sustained streak of **action 0 (forward motion)** that lasts several timesteps before any turn is taken, whereas low‑reward rollouts break forward motion almost immediately with frequent **action 1/2 (turns)**. In the successful runs the agent executes **brief, decisive turns** (often a single step of action 1 or 2) precisely when a wall or obstacle is imminent, while the poorer runs exhibit **continuous, small‑amplitude oscillations**—alternating left‑right turns every few steps. The high‑reward trajectories also show **timed corrective bursts**: after a short over‑steer the agent quickly returns to forward action, regaining positive reward, whereas low‑reward episodes linger in the corrective state, accruing multiple negative rewards. Moreover, the better rollouts align **peaks in reward with extended forward segments**, suggesting that maintaining forward momentum directly drives the score, while the low‑reward cases dilute this effect with excessive turning. These patterns imply that prompt, long‑duration forward movement combined with sparse, well‑timed turns maximizes progress, whereas frequent, jittery adjustments prevent the agent from capitalizing on the high‑reward forward action.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts all begin with an immediate forward action and then execute short, well‑timed right‑turns (or left‑turns) after only a few steps, keeping the agent on the corridor while preserving forward momentum; the low‑reward episodes either delay those corrective turns or omit them entirely, allowing the agent to drift into walls. In the successful runs the agent repeatedly alternates between a forward action and a turn within a tight 2‑to‑3‑step window, which creates a smooth “oscillatory” trajectory that stays near the centre of the passage, whereas the failing runs contain long stretches of straight motion or sudden backward thrusts that push the state toward hazardous boundaries. The high‑reward behavior also includes a rapid re‑acceleration forward immediately after each turn, preventing loss of speed and maintaining positive per‑step rewards; low‑reward episodes often linger after a turn or execute a backward move, resulting in a rapid drop in reward and eventual collision. These timing differences plausibly explain the reward gap because early forward progress and prompt corrective turning keep the agent in safe, high‑reward regions, while delayed or absent corrections cause drift, instability, and wall crashes that heavily penalize the episode.

[WORST POLICY]
In the higher‑reward rollouts the agent tends to make an early, modest adjustment—often a single small‑magnitude action (e.g., a brief turn or slight speed change) that briefly stabilises its trajectory before the episode ends, whereas the worst rollouts show long stretches of repeated “forward‑only” actions (action = 2) with no corrective inputs until a sudden crash. This early corrective impulse appears to keep the state values from diverging wildly, limiting the magnitude of the final penalty; in contrast, the low‑reward trajectories let the state drift upward for many timesteps, only crashing when the accumulated error becomes too large. Moreover, the better rollouts exhibit a clear “stop‑or‑turn” action shortly before termination (often action = 0 or a sharp turn), signaling a deliberate attempt to halt or reorient, while the poorer rollouts either never issue such a terminating action or do so only after chaotic oscillations have already amplified the error. Finally, the timing of these interventions differs: high‑reward episodes intervene within the first few dozen steps, whereas low‑reward episodes postpone any corrective move until well after the agent has built up large state excursions, leading to substantially larger negative rewards.
