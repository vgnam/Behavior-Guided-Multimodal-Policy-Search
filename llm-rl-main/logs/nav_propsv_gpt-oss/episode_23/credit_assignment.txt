[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts keep long uninterrupted stretches of action 0 (forward) before inserting a single turn, whereas low‑reward rollouts interleave actions 0, 1 and 2 almost every step, producing rapid back‑and‑forth corrections. In the successful trajectories the agent delays its first turn until it has established forward momentum along the inner wall, while the poor trajectories turn almost immediately and then repeatedly over‑correct. The high‑reward episodes also exhibit smooth, monotonic state progress—few sign changes in position—indicating that the agent stays close to the desired boundary, whereas the low‑reward runs show frequent sign flips and oscillations that pull the agent away from the wall. By maintaining sustained forward motion and limiting unnecessary steering, the policy accrues steady positive reward; the jittery, overly reactive pattern in the low‑reward rollouts wastes steps and generates negative penalties, leading to the observed reward gap.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts keep a long uninterrupted stretch of forward actions (action 0) before any corrective turn, whereas low‑reward rollouts intersperse turns (actions 1 or 2) almost every step, breaking momentum. When a turn is needed, the successful runs execute a single, well‑timed sharp turn (action 2) after several forward steps, positioning the agent near the outer bounds of the arena and then resume forward motion; the poor runs either turn too early, turn repeatedly, or use backward moves (action 1) that send them back toward the centre. The timing of corrections in the high‑reward trajectories is sparse and deliberate, producing smooth state trajectories with only modest fluctuations, while the low‑reward trajectories exhibit rapid, jittery oscillations that cause the agent to linger in unsafe zones. Because the reward function heavily favors forward progress and penalizes collisions or stagnation, maintaining forward thrust for as many steps as possible and limiting corrective actions to essential, well‑spaced turns directly yields higher cumulative reward. Consequently, the presence of sustained forward sequences and minimally timed sharp turns distinguishes the high‑reward behavior from the erratic, over‑correcting patterns of the low‑reward rollouts.

[WORST POLICY]
The high‑reward rollouts all contain an early, strong change of action – a sudden thrust or turn within the first few frames – that propels the agent out of its initial stagnant state, whereas the low‑reward episodes keep the same action (almost always “2”) for the entire horizon, never introducing a deliberate directional shift. In the better runs the agent follows that initial thrust with a timely corrective maneuver (a sharp right turn or a brief pause) that re‑aligns it with the corridor, while the poorer runs exhibit no such adjustments and instead oscillate back‑and‑forth around a single control value. This pattern of an early, high‑magnitude action followed by a purposeful, later correction reduces lingering near‑boundary drift and prevents the cumulative error that dominates the low‑reward trajectories. Because the low‑reward rollouts lack both the initial decisive push and the subsequent re‑orientation, they remain stuck in a repetitive, over‑correcting loop that drives the state values farther from the goal and yields far larger negative returns.
