[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts all begin with a rapid sequence of forward actions that generate an early stream of positive reward, and whenever a negative reward appears they immediately follow it with a brief, decisive corrective action that quickly restores forward momentum; low‑reward episodes either lack this initial forward burst or respond to negative signals with prolonged or absent corrections, letting the penalty accumulate. The successful rollouts also time sharp turns precisely at junctions, using a single decisive turn before resuming smooth forward motion, whereas the poorer rollouts either omit the turn, turn too late, or execute multiple jittery turns that keep the agent oscillating. In the high‑reward cases the agent’s oscillations are short‑lived—brief back‑and‑forth moves are followed by at least five consecutive forward steps—while the low‑reward examples sustain oscillatory cycles for many steps, causing drift away from the optimal path. Finally, the best rollouts never end with a terminal collision; they finish the episode after a stabilising forward run, whereas several low‑reward runs culminate in a sudden large negative reward caused by an ill‑timed forward action into an obstacle. These timing and action‑selection differences explain why the former accumulate far higher total rewards.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts keep long uninterrupted stretches of the forward action (action 0), only inserting a single corrective turn (actions 1 or 2) when a waypoint is reached, whereas low‑reward rollouts intersperse frequent turns and abrupt switches to actions 1/2, often before any positive reward cue. The successful trajectories also time their occasional turn precisely at the moment a positive reward appears, using the turn to capture the waypoint and then immediately resume forward motion; the failing runs either turn too early, miss the reward cue, or make a sudden turn that drives them into a wall. Moreover, the high‑reward episodes display smooth, monotonic changes in the state variable (steady progress toward lower values) with only brief, minor oscillations, while the low‑reward episodes show large, erratic state jumps and oscillatory over‑corrections that destabilize navigation. This contrast means the high‑reward policy maintains a stable forward momentum and only makes necessary, well‑timed adjustments, whereas the low‑reward policy wastes time on unnecessary or mistimed turns that lead to collisions and large negative rewards.

[WORST POLICY]
The high‑reward rollouts break the monotony of the “always‑thrust‑2” pattern seen in the low‑reward runs, inserting early direction changes (often a quick switch to action 0 or a sharp turn) that steer the agent away from prolonged oscillations against walls. Those brief, aggressive thrusts followed by immediate corrective maneuvers let the agent escape the large‑amplitude back‑and‑forth swings that dominate the low‑reward trajectories, reducing the total time spent in unstable states. In addition, the better rollouts tend to terminate sooner, so the accumulated penalty from staying in a failing region is smaller than the long, continuously thrusting episodes of the low‑reward cases. By contrast, the low‑reward rollouts cling to a single action (action 2) for most of the episode, producing persistent forward‑backward jitter and repeatedly driving the state into extreme values that trigger the worst negative rewards. This difference in action diversity, timing of corrective turns, and episode length explains why the high‑reward rollouts achieve noticeably higher (less negative) returns.
