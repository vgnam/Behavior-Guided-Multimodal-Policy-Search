[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts consistently insert brief pause or corrective actions shortly before a corner or obstacle, using a quick switch from forward (action 0) to a slight turn (action 1 or 2) and then resuming forward motion, which keeps the agent inside the safe corridor; low‑reward runs either omit this pre‑emptive pause or delay the turn until after the impact, leading to collisions or large deviations. In the successful trajectories the forward thrusts are short and spaced by frequent, small adjustments, so the agent never builds up excess momentum that would carry it past a wall, whereas the poorer runs contain long uninterrupted forward bursts that culminate in abrupt failures. The high‑reward episodes also display a pattern of early‑stage stabilization—remaining within a tight radius of the origin for several steps—followed by controlled excursions, while the low‑reward cases wander farther from the origin early on, exposing the agent to harsher terrain and higher penalty zones. Finally, when a corrective turn is needed, the high‑reward policy executes it immediately after detecting a state change, whereas the low‑reward policy tends to over‑correct later, producing oscillations that amplify error rather than dampen it, which reduces cumulative reward. These timing and action‑selection differences explain why the former achieve substantially higher returns.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts keep action 0 (the forward move) dominant for extended stretches—often more than ten consecutive steps—only inserting actions 1 or 2 as brief, well‑timed corrections after they have made solid progress, which lets the agent accumulate positive reward steadily while gently steering around obstacles. In contrast, low‑reward episodes introduce actions 1 or 2 much earlier or in long runs, producing sudden state drops or sharp direction changes that quickly destabilize the trajectory and lead to collisions or premature terminations. The successful rollouts also display gradual, incremental adjustments rather than the abrupt, large‑magnitude state jumps seen in the failures, allowing the agent to maintain smooth momentum and avoid harsh penalties. Finally, the timing of corrective actions in the high‑reward cases is synchronized with observable obstacles (e.g., after a forward streak reaches a turning point), whereas the low‑reward cases apply turns without such contextual cues, causing over‑correction and loss of control. These consistent patterns of sustained forward motion, sparsely timed and modest corrections, and avoidance of abrupt state shifts plausibly explain the large reward gap.

[WORST POLICY]
The high‑reward rollouts all contain a single corrective “0” action inserted roughly halfway through the episode (around frames 10‑13), whereas the low‑reward rollouts never deviate from the constant forward “2” action. This brief turn or brake curtails the forward thrust before the agent’s state variable can swing to extreme values, keeping the trajectory tighter and avoiding the large‑amplitude oscillations that dominate the low‑reward runs. By introducing the corrective action early, the high‑reward episodes also end sooner, limiting exposure to the stochastic disturbances that later cause runaway drift. In contrast, the low‑reward rollouts persist with unbroken forward motion, allowing the state to repeatedly overshoot and undershoot, which produces the severe collisions reflected in their more negative rewards.
