The high‑reward rollouts all terminate after only a handful of steps, usually because the agent makes one strong, decisive action (most often a single forward thrust) that drives it straight into a wall and ends the episode early; this limits the time the agent can accrue the large negative per‑step penalties that dominate the reward signal. In contrast, the low‑reward rollouts linger for many frames, repeatedly alternating actions and over‑correcting their orientation, which produces long‑lasting oscillations within a confined region and lets the per‑step cost accumulate to much lower scores. The brief, abrupt crashes in the high‑reward cases therefore yield a higher (less negative) total simply by shortening exposure to the environment’s penalty structure, whereas the prolonged, jittery control cycles of the low‑reward cases keep the agent alive longer but incur far larger cumulative losses. This timing difference—early decisive failure versus extended indecisive motion—is the key behavioral factor behind the reward gap.
