[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts all exhibit a strong forward bias, keeping action 0 (move forward) for extended intervals (often dozens of frames) before inserting only brief corrective turns, whereas low‑reward episodes introduce backward or turn actions much earlier, leading to frequent direction changes and early collisions. The successful runs also time their turns to coincide with natural maze corners—short, single‑step actions that realign the heading—while the poor runs over‑correct with successive turn or backward actions that push the agent into walls. In the high‑reward trajectories the agent’s state values drift smoothly upward, indicating steady progress through the corridor, whereas low‑reward trajectories show rapid oscillations around a narrow range of states, reflecting indecisive back‑and‑forth motion. Finally, the best rollouts avoid any prolonged backward motion (action 2); when they do use it, it appears only as a momentary tweak after a long forward stretch, while the low‑reward rollouts contain repeated backward steps that undo previous progress and incur penalties. These patterns keep the agent moving toward rewarding regions and prevent costly wall contacts, directly accounting for the observed reward gap.

[BEST POLICY]
In the high‑reward rollouts the agent interleaves forward thrusts (action 0) with short corrective turns (actions 1 or 2) within the first few dozen steps, keeping the state value inside a narrow band instead of letting it drift upward; the low‑reward runs either persist with long stretches of pure forward motion or delay the first turn until much later, which causes the state to climb to unsafe levels and results in collisions. Whenever the trajectory approaches a wall, the successful episodes insert a quick opposite‑turn or backward adjustment (a brief action 1 or 2) exactly before the wall is reached, whereas the poor episodes either miss this correction or apply it after impact. The best rollouts also exhibit brief bursts of lateral thrust (action 2) right after entering a corner, realigning the heading early, while the worst rollouts either skip the early lateral move or execute it too late, leading to overshoot. Finally, the high‑reward trajectories maintain a regular, low‑amplitude oscillation in the state variable throughout the episode, preventing the large swings that appear after a few hundred steps in the low‑reward trajectories and thereby preserving stability and accumulating reward.

[WORST POLICY]
The high‑reward rollouts all terminate after only a handful of steps, usually because the agent makes one strong, decisive action (most often a single forward thrust) that drives it straight into a wall and ends the episode early; this limits the time the agent can accrue the large negative per‑step penalties that dominate the reward signal. In contrast, the low‑reward rollouts linger for many frames, repeatedly alternating actions and over‑correcting their orientation, which produces long‑lasting oscillations within a confined region and lets the per‑step cost accumulate to much lower scores. The brief, abrupt crashes in the high‑reward cases therefore yield a higher (less negative) total simply by shortening exposure to the environment’s penalty structure, whereas the prolonged, jittery control cycles of the low‑reward cases keep the agent alive longer but incur far larger cumulative losses. This timing difference—early decisive failure versus extended indecisive motion—is the key behavioral factor behind the reward gap.
