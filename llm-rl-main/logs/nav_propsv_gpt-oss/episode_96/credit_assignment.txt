[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts consistently contain **extended runs of action 2 (forward thrust)** that last several consecutive timesteps before a single corrective turn (usually a brief action 0), whereas low‑reward rollouts break forward motion almost immediately with alternating turns (actions 0 and 1) and frequently reverse direction, creating back‑and‑forth oscillations. The successful trajectories also show **a single, well‑timed right‑turn (action 0) early in the episode** that aligns the agent with the main corridor, after which they maintain a straight heading for long intervals; the poor rollouts either omit this early alignment or perform multiple rapid turns, never sustaining a straight path. Moreover, high‑reward episodes exhibit **minimal successive left‑turns (action 1)**—they use left turns only sparingly to negotiate sharp corners—while low‑reward runs contain clusters of left‑turn actions that over‑correct and send the agent off‑track. These patterns reduce unnecessary direction changes, prevent the agent from retracing its steps, and allow it to accumulate the larger forward‑movement rewards that dominate the total score.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts repeatedly execute the forward action (action 0) for long stretches, only inserting brief corrective turns (actions 1 or 2) after a few steps, which keeps the agent’s state drifting slowly forward within a narrow band; low‑reward rollouts, by contrast, make a single large forward thrust followed by an abrupt, oversized turn or jump that pushes the state far outside the safe corridor. The successful trajectories also space their corrective actions evenly, producing a gentle oscillatory pattern that smooths out drift, whereas the failures show sudden, isolated corrections that create spikes in the state trajectory and often terminate the episode. Timing-wise, the best rollouts maintain a steady rhythm of “forward‑few‑steps → minor turn → forward‑few‑steps,” preventing the agent from lingering near walls, while the poor rollouts either pause too long before a big maneuver or execute a sharp maneuver too early, leading to boundary collisions. This disciplined pacing limits exposure to obstacles and preserves cumulative positive rewards, whereas the erratic timing in low‑reward runs yields large negative penalties when the agent overshoots or crashes. Consequently, the presence of sustained forward sequences with small, regularly timed adjustments is the key behavioral component that separates the high‑reward from the low‑reward performances.

[WORST POLICY]
The high‑reward rollouts all contain an early non‑forward action (a turn, typically action 0 or 1) within the first two or three steps, after which the agent moves forward for a short, relatively smooth segment before the episode ends, whereas the low‑reward rollouts never issue a turn and persist with action 2 (forward) for the entire episode. This brief re‑orientation lets the agent line up with the nearest corridor and avoid a prolonged collision, limiting the time spent oscillating against walls; the monotonic forward thrust in the low‑reward cases forces the agent to repeatedly hit and bounce off obstacles, producing large state swings and accumulating more negative reward. Additionally, the high‑reward trajectories show a single, purposeful turn followed by a brief stable phase, while the low‑reward trajectories exhibit continual, uncontrolled oscillations with no corrective turns. Because the early corrective turn reduces sustained contact with hazards, it yields a noticeably higher (less negative) total reward despite the overall poor policy.
