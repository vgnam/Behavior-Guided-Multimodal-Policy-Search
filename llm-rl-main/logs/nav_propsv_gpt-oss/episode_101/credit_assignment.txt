[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward episodes repeatedly interleave short bursts of the forward‑favoring action 0 with an immediate corrective turn (often action 1 or 2) within a few time steps, keeping the state values oscillating in a modest range; by contrast, low‑reward runs contain long uninterrupted sequences of forward motion before any turn, allowing the state to climb sharply and then crash. The successful rollouts also tend to execute their first corrective turn very early (within the first few dozen steps), whereas the failing rollouts delay that correction until much later, when a collision becomes inevitable. Moreover, the high‑reward trajectories show a pattern of “quick‑fix” adjustments—each positive reward is followed by a brief negative‑reward tweak that stabilises the path—while the low‑reward trajectories lack these stabilising tweaks and instead exhibit monotonic progress that ends abruptly. This disciplined, frequent re‑orientation prevents the agent from overshooting obstacles and thus yields substantially higher cumulative reward.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts consistently interleave brief forward bursts with immediate corrective turns (typically a right‑hand turn followed quickly by a left‑hand adjustment) right before reaching a corner, keeping the agent hugging the inner wall and preventing contact with obstacles; low‑reward episodes lack these timely corrections, driving straight into walls or drifting outward until a fatal collision. In the successful runs the agent frequently inserts a short pause or very low‑speed step after each turn, which stabilises its heading and lets the next burst start from a well‑aligned orientation, whereas the failures show prolonged uninterrupted forward actions that accumulate angular error. The best trajectories also begin with a modest forward move (action 2) before any turn, establishing a safe buffer from the start‑line, while the poorest rolls either start with an aggressive turn or immediately execute a terminal action (action 0) that ends the episode. These patterns create a disciplined, oscillatory control loop that continuously re‑centres the path, whereas the opposite – delayed or missing corrections and excessive straight‑line thrust – rapidly drives the agent into high‑penalty states, explaining the large reward gap.

[WORST POLICY]
The high‑reward rollouts all contain at least one early change in the chosen action – the agent briefly turns or adjusts its heading after the first step instead of repeating the same command, producing a short, directed drift away from the start before the episode ends. In contrast, the low‑reward rollouts never deviate from the constant “action 2” signal, so the state value merely oscillates within a narrow band and the agent never creates a purposeful trajectory. This occasional turn in the better rollouts prevents the agent from getting trapped in the repetitive, bounded oscillation that accrues the maximal –500 penalty, thereby limiting the number of timesteps over which negative reward is collected. Because the worse rollouts maintain a single, unvarying action, they generate larger, erratic swings and stay alive longer, compounding the per‑step penalty and yielding the most negative scores.
