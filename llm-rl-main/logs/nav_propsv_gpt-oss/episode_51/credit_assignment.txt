[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts all begin with a sustained forward thrust (action 2 or 0) and keep that motion for several steps before making a single, small‑angle correction (action 1) exactly as a corner is approached, which lets the agent hug the wall smoothly; low‑reward rollouts either switch abruptly to a hard turn or stop (action 0) early or wait too long, producing large, sudden direction changes that send the agent into walls. The timing of the corrective action is also consistent in the successful runs – it occurs within one or two frames of detecting a turn – whereas the poor runs show delayed or multiple back‑and‑forth corrections that create oscillations and loss of control. In the high‑reward cases the agent’s speed remains roughly constant, only modulating slightly for the corner, while the low‑reward episodes exhibit jerky speed spikes followed by rapid deceleration, which amplifies instability. These smooth, well‑timed adjustments keep the agent on the intended path and accrue positive rewards, whereas the erratic, mistimed turns and speed fluctuations cause collisions or premature terminations and thus much lower scores.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts repeatedly begin with a sustained sequence of action 0 (forward thrust) that quickly establishes forward momentum, followed by brief, well‑timed corrective actions 2 that make only minimal backward adjustments before resuming action 0; this pattern keeps the agent on a smooth trajectory and prevents large oscillations. In contrast, the low‑reward episodes either delay the initial forward thrust, insert long or premature action 2 (or action 1) sequences that pull the agent backward, or replace the small corrections with abrupt, large‑angle turns that cause over‑steering. The successful rollouts also exhibit early stabilization—by the fifth or sixth frame the agent’s position variance has already narrowed—whereas the failing runs show prolonged positional jitter and only later achieve any stability, if at all. Moreover, high‑reward trajectories avoid sustained clusters of action 1 (sharp turns) near obstacles, while low‑reward ones frequently execute such turns at critical corners, leading to collisions or boundary violations. This disciplined alternation of steady forward pushes with tiny, precisely timed back‑corrections yields higher cumulative reward by maintaining speed and staying within the safe corridor, whereas the opposite timing and excessive turning in low‑reward rollouts waste momentum and trigger failures.

[WORST POLICY]
The high‑reward rollouts all contain a short burst of forward thrust followed quickly by a change of action that stabilises the agent’s motion, whereas the low‑reward episodes keep the same forward‑thrust action (2) for the entire episode, producing relentless oscillations. In the better rollouts the aggressive acceleration ends after only a few frames and is replaced by corrective or idle actions, preventing the agent from overshooting and colliding; the worst rollouts never make this switch, so they continuously over‑correct and bounce back and forth. This timing pattern—early termination of high‑power moves and introduction of varied actions—appears consistently in the higher‑reward cases and is absent in the lower‑reward ones. Because the variable‑action pattern limits collisions and keeps the trajectory smoother, it yields less negative reward than the always‑thrust, oscillatory behaviour of the worst rollouts.
