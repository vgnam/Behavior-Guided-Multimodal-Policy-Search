[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts repeatedly execute a decisive turn (action 1 or 2) within the first few dozen steps, immediately earning the +5 turn reward and steering the agent into the next corridor, whereas low‑reward runs either postpone turning or replace it with prolonged forward thrusts that miss the corner bonuses. After the initial turn, the successful trajectories alternate short bursts of forward motion (action 0) with timely corrective turns, keeping the state within a narrow band and preventing large overshoots; the poorer rollouts linger in long forward stretches or make hasty, uncoordinated corrections that produce frequent –0.5 penalties. Mid‑episode, the high‑reward agents settle into a stabilization phase where oscillations diminish and the state drifts only slightly before the episode ends, while the low‑reward agents continue to exhibit large swings and back‑and‑forth adjustments right to the finish. This combination of early corner exploitation, rhythmic forward‑turn sequencing, and late‑episode stabilization yields consistently higher cumulative rewards than the erratic, turn‑deficient patterns seen in the low‑reward rollouts.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts consistently interleave a brief forward action (0) with a corrective turn (1 or 2) every few steps, so the agent never runs many steps straight ahead without checking its heading; low‑reward rollouts often contain long uninterrupted forward streaks or a single “thrust” that pushes the state far before any turn is taken. The early‑stage turns in the successful runs appear within the first‑few frames, keeping the trajectory aligned with the corridor and preventing the large –200 penalty seen when the agent later collides; the failing runs either omit these early corrections or apply a sudden, aggressive turn only near the episode’s end. Successful episodes also keep the magnitude of each turn modest, yielding the small –0.5 penalty, whereas the poor episodes execute a large, destabilising turn that instantly flips the state into an undesirable region. By constantly correcting the heading while maintaining forward progress, the high‑reward policy avoids early termination and accumulates many +5 forward rewards, whereas the low‑reward policy’s lack of timely, incremental adjustments leads to collisions and the dramatic negative rewards.

[WORST POLICY]
The high‑reward rollouts all contain a distinct, early change of action – usually a brief “2” followed by a different command (often “0”) that produces a single large state jump and terminates the episode after only a few steps, whereas the low‑reward rollouts keep action = 2 unchanged for the entire horizon, generating long, noisy oscillations. This early decisive maneuver caps the number of timesteps over which step‑wise penalties are accrued, so the cumulative reward stays around –170 instead of drifting to the worst‑case –500. In contrast, the low‑reward trajectories exhibit sustained, repetitive motion with no action variability, causing the agent to remain in the maze and repeatedly incur the per‑step cost. The abrupt termination in the high‑reward cases therefore prevents the accumulation of additional negative rewards, while the endless oscillatory control in the low‑reward cases maximizes the penalty. Consequently, the presence of a quick action switch and early episode end is the key behavioral component that separates the comparatively better outcomes from the worst ones.
