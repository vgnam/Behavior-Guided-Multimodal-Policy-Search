[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts consistently feature long stretches of the same forward‑movement action (either action 0 or 2) followed by extended, smooth turning blocks, whereas low‑reward episodes abruptly interleave opposite actions after only a few steps, producing sudden, large‑angle turns. The successful trajectories keep corrective actions brief and spaced out, allowing the agent to maintain a steady heading and avoid hitting walls; the failures show aggressive early thrusts that quickly over‑correct, leading to oscillations that drive the agent into obstacles. When a turn is required in the high‑reward cases, the agent executes it gradually and holds the turning action for many consecutive timesteps, creating a predictable spiral or curved path, while the low‑reward cases switch directions sharply and repeatedly, resulting in erratic, jittery motion. This disciplined timing of actions preserves stability and maximises forward progress, whereas the erratic timing in the failures generates collisions and large negative rewards.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts repeatedly interleave a short forward thrust (action 0) with a corrective turn (action 1 or 2) within two to three frames, keeping the agent’s state bounded and hugging the corridor walls; low‑reward rollouts either omit the turn entirely or delay it long enough that the forward drift grows unchecked, leading to large, abrupt state jumps and boundary collisions. The successful episodes also exhibit a rhythmic “push‑and‑adjust” cadence—each positive‑reward segment is quickly followed by a small negative‑reward correction that re‑centers the trajectory—whereas the poor episodes contain long stretches of uncorrected forward motion that accumulate error before a single, oversized correction that overshoots. Additionally, the best rollouts begin with a modest turn that steers the agent into the inner corridor, establishing a safe lane early, while the worst rollouts start with a straight thrust that pushes the agent toward the outer edge and later requires a drastic, destabilising maneuver. This disciplined timing of turns and modest adjustments prevents the agent from drifting out of bounds, directly translating into higher cumulative rewards.

[WORST POLICY]
The high‑reward rollouts all terminate after only one or two decisive actions—a brief forward thrust (action 2) followed almost immediately by a change or stop—whereas the low‑reward rollouts keep applying the same forward action for many timesteps before a late “stop” (action 0) that finally ends the episode. This early termination limits the agent’s exposure to the maze’s hazardous regions, preventing the large cumulative penalties that accrue during the long, oscillatory trajectories seen in the low‑reward cases. In the better rollouts the agent also makes a rapid directional change (a sharp right turn) within the first few frames, while the poorer rollouts remain on a straight path and only wobble because of state fluctuations. The brief, purposeful motion in the high‑reward episodes produces only modest state changes, avoiding the large spikes and over‑corrections that dominate the low‑reward trajectories and that drive the harsher negative rewards. Consequently, the combination of an early action switch and a quick turn keeps the agent out of dangerous states, yielding comparatively higher (less negative) returns.
