[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts consistently begin with a sustained forward thrust that carries the agent deep into the corridor before any turn is made, whereas low‑reward episodes either turn immediately or linger near the start and lose momentum. When a turn is required, the successful runs execute a single, decisive sharp turn (action 1 or 2) at the exact moment a wall or corner is encountered and then resume forward motion, while the poor runs either hesitate with multiple small corrective actions or over‑oscillate with rapid alternating turns. The best trajectories keep the oscillation frequency low—once a correction is applied they maintain a steady heading for several time steps—whereas the low‑reward paths display continual back‑and‑forth adjustments that waste steps and increase collision risk. Finally, high‑reward agents preserve a relatively constant speed after each correction, avoiding the gradual deceleration seen in the failing rollouts; this sustained speed lets them reach the goal before the episode terminates, directly translating into the large reward disparity.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts consistently sprinkle short bursts of **action 0** (the forward thrust that yields the +5.0 reward) among a few corrective turns, so the agent gains frequent positive increments while never lingering on a single steering command for more than a couple of steps; low‑reward runs either omit these forward bursts or let a single action (usually 1 or 2) run unchecked until a catastrophic collision occurs. In the successful episodes the agent’s **timing pattern** is a rapid “forward‑then‑tiny‑adjust‑forward” rhythm that keeps it hugging walls or corridors, whereas the poor episodes exhibit long uninterrupted straight runs followed by a sudden large turn or overshoot that throws it off the safe path. The high‑reward behavior also shows **small‑amplitude oscillations** (minor left/right tweaks) immediately after each positive reward, which act as micro‑stabilizers and prevent drift, while the low‑reward trajectories lack these stabilizing tweaks and instead display either no correction or overly aggressive re‑orientation. Because the former strategy continually harvests the high‑value forward reward and corrects drift before it accumulates error, it produces much larger total returns than the latter, which sacrifices early gains and ends in costly crashes.

[WORST POLICY]
The higher‑reward rollouts all contain at least one purposeful turn early in the episode—usually a brief action different from the default forward thrust—that lets the agent align with a wall before the eventual crash, whereas the low‑reward rollouts stick to the same forward action for the entire run and never make a corrective turn. In the better rollouts the agent’s speed or orientation changes shortly after the turn, showing a brief period of deceleration or re‑orientation before the loss of control, while the poorer rollouts display a monotonic, oscillating forward motion with no clear slowdown or re‑aiming. This occasional deviation from pure forward thrust appears to give the agent a fleeting moment of stable contact with the maze geometry, reducing the frequency and severity of collisions and thus yielding a less negative total reward. Conversely, the constant‑action, uncorrected thrust in the low‑reward runs leads to relentless oscillations and repeated wall impacts, driving the reward much lower.
