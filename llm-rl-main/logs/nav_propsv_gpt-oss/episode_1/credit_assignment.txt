[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts consistently begin with long uninterrupted sequences of action 0 (steady forward thrust) that keep the state value rising steadily, whereas low‑reward rollouts start with abrupt action 2 thrusts or immediate switches to turning actions 1/2, producing sharp state jumps and early collisions. In the successful episodes the agent reserves actions 1 and 2 for brief, well‑timed corrective nudges after many timesteps of smooth progress, while the failing episodes sprinkle those corrective actions densely and prematurely, causing over‑steering and oscillations. The timing pattern in the top rollouts shows a sustained forward phase (often several hundred timesteps) before any adjustment, whereas the bottom rollouts intermix adjustments within the first few frames, leading to rapid loss of alignment with the corridor. This disciplined pacing prevents the agent from hitting walls and allows it to accumulate positive reward, while the erratic, early‑turning behavior of the low‑reward runs generates negative rewards and early terminations. Consequently, the presence of prolonged, monotonic forward action 0 and sparsely placed, precisely timed corrections is the key behavioral signature that separates high‑reward from low‑reward performance.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts all begin with a sustained forward move (action 0) that is only briefly interrupted by small corrective actions (1 or 2) after several frames, allowing the agent to make steady progress while gently realigning to walls or corridors; low‑reward rollouts either replace that early forward streak with an immediate sharp turn or a single aggressive thrust that sends the agent into a boundary. In the successful episodes the corrective actions are timed later—typically after the agent has traveled a few steps—so they serve as modest “course‑corrections” rather than frequent over‑corrections that produce jittery back‑and‑forth motion, whereas the failing runs show oscillations that start almost immediately and continue throughout the episode. The high‑reward behavior also includes a pattern of following the outer wall at a roughly constant distance, using alternating left/right adjustments only when a corner is encountered; the low‑reward trajectories either lose contact with the wall early or collide with it because they make abrupt, large‑amplitude turns. These timing and magnitude differences keep the agent inside the navigable region longer, accumulating positive reward, while the opposite patterns cause early exits or chaotic divergence that dramatically lower the return.

[WORST POLICY]
The high‑reward rollouts all contain an early, decisive maneuver—typically a sharp turn or thrust (often a sudden change from action 2 to a different action) that sends the agent into a failure state within only a few timesteps, so the episode ends quickly and the accumulated penalty is limited. In contrast, the low‑reward rollouts lack this early reversal and instead repeat the same action (usually action 2) for most of the episode, producing long‑lasting oscillations or slow drift that keep the agent alive for many steps and therefore accrue far larger negative rewards. Moreover, the high‑reward trajectories show a brief window of relatively smooth forward motion before the abrupt crash, whereas the low‑reward runs either persist in a noisy oscillatory pattern or maintain a monotonic progression without any abrupt corrective action, both of which prevent early termination. This difference in timing—quick, decisive collapse versus prolonged unstable wandering—directly explains why the “high‑reward” rollouts receive a less severe total penalty than the consistently low‑reward ones.
