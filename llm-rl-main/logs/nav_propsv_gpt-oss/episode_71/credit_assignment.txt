[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts consistently string together brief forward actions (action 0) followed quickly by modest corrective turns (actions 1 or 2), keeping the agent’s state within a narrow corridor and preventing large‑scale drift. They also avoid long idle periods or extended backward moves, instead using short, frequent adjustments that correct orientation before error accumulates. In contrast, low‑reward rollouts feature either prolonged pauses or sudden, large jumps in state—often through excessive backward or aggressive turning actions—that push the agent toward walls or unstable regions. These abrupt or delayed corrections lead to over‑correction, loss of control, and eventual crashes, sharply reducing the cumulative reward. Consequently, the disciplined pattern of regular, small forward thrusts with timely, limited steering present in the successful runs appears absent or reversed in the failing episodes, directly accounting for the reward gap.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts repeatedly interleave the forward‑propelling actions (0 / 1) with brief, low‑amplitude corrective moves rather than issuing a single, large turn; this pattern keeps the trajectory smooth and lets the agent recover from small deviations before they amplify. In contrast, low‑reward episodes contain a sudden, high‑magnitude turn (often a switch to action 0 or 2 after many forward steps) that abruptly changes heading and leads directly to a wall collision. The successful runs also spread their adjustments evenly throughout the episode, using small‑scale oscillations to stay centered, whereas the failures concentrate the decisive maneuver near the episode’s end, giving the agent insufficient time to correct. By maintaining continuous, modest corrections instead of a lone, abrupt change, the high‑reward policy avoids the destabilising spikes that terminate the low‑reward trajectories.

[WORST POLICY]
The high‑reward rollouts all contain **explicit, timed action changes** – brief left or right turns that line up with the maze’s corners and keep the agent hugging the walls, whereas the low‑reward episodes use a single action (‘2’) for the entire run and never adjust direction. This **wall‑following pattern** gives the agent a clear geometric reference, preventing it from drifting into open space and repeatedly hitting obstacles; the constant‑action runs lack any reference and therefore wander erratically, producing large state oscillations. In the better rollouts the agent’s **orientation updates are smooth and gradual**, allowing it to stay on the intended corridor, while the worst rollouts show abrupt or absent orientation changes that result in chaotic trajectories. Because the high‑reward behavior translates into steady progress toward the goal and fewer collisions, it yields a markedly higher (less negative) return than the unvarying, directionless thrust seen in the low‑reward cases.
