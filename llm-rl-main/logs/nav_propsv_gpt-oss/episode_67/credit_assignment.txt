[CURRENT POLICY]
In the high‑reward rollouts the agent string together a relatively long forward segment (action 0) before any turn, then follows each turn (usually action 1 or 2) with another forward stretch, keeping its velocity aligned with the corridor and collecting steady positive reward. By contrast, the low‑reward rollouts are dominated by immediate back‑and‑forth alternations, turning almost as soon as they move forward, which creates short forward bursts and many corrective actions that produce negative reward spikes. Successful episodes also reserve the sharp turn (action 2) for clearly defined corners—typically after the state angle has reached the optimal turning point—whereas the poorer runs fire action 2 prematurely or repeatedly, causing overshoot and loss of progress. Finally, high‑reward trajectories include brief, purposeful pauses after a turn to let the state settle before accelerating again, while low‑reward runs exhibit continuous jerky adjustments without stabilization, leading to accumulated penalties. These timing and sequencing differences keep the agent on the intended path in the high‑reward cases and generate the erratic, oscillatory wandering that depresses reward in the low‑reward cases.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts keep a steady stream of forward actions (action 0) punctuated by only brief, small‑angle corrective turns, so the agent moves smoothly along the corridor and rarely loses momentum; in contrast, low‑reward rollouts contain long bursts of aggressive thrust followed by sudden large turns or over‑corrections, which break continuity and create abrupt speed changes. The successful runs also align the agent’s heading early in the episode and maintain that orientation for many steps, allowing it to collect the bulk of the positive per‑step reward, whereas the poor runs drift toward walls or outer edges before any correction is attempted, leading to early collisions and negative rewards. When a turn is needed, high‑reward trajectories execute it gradually and immediately after detecting an upcoming wall, while low‑reward trajectories either delay the turn or swing wildly, causing the agent to hit obstacles. Moreover, the best rollouts exhibit consistent timing – a pattern of 2–3 forward steps then a single turn – which keeps the agent centered in the maze; the worst rollouts show irregular timing, with long idle or backward‑like actions that waste time and expose the agent to hazards. These differences in action regularity, early alignment, and gentle correction directly translate into higher cumulative rewards for the high‑performing rollouts.

[WORST POLICY]
In the better rollouts the agent abandons the default forward thrust (action 2) within the first two steps, either by issuing a stop (action 0) or a turn, which quickly caps the distance it travels toward a wall. The poorer rollouts never break the monotonic forward action; they keep applying action 2 for the entire episode, producing large back‑and‑forth swings in the state value and frequent over‑corrections. The high‑reward trajectories therefore consist of a single, purposeful translation followed by an immediate termination, while the low‑reward ones exhibit prolonged oscillatory motion that repeatedly drives the agent into obstacles. By correcting early, the high‑reward agent avoids the cumulative penalties of repeated collisions, whereas the low‑reward agent accrues larger negative rewards from sustained unstable movement. Consequently, early action diversification and timely stopping are the key behavioral differences that drive the reward gap.
