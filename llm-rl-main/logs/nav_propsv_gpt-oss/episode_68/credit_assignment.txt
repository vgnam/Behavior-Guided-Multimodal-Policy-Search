[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts repeatedly alternate a brief forward thrust with an immediate, modest turn (e.g., a pattern of action 0 followed quickly by action 1 or 2), using these short corrective nudges to stay centered in corridors and to negotiate corners before contact; low‑reward rollouts either sustain long, unbroken forward thrusts or execute a single large turn only after overshooting, which lets the agent drift into walls. The successful trajectories also exhibit early, frequent stabilization pauses – the agent slows or briefly holds after each adjustment rather than continuously accelerating – whereas the failures show prolonged acceleration or abrupt, high‑speed bursts that terminate in collisions. In the high‑reward cases the agent’s turn magnitude is modest and timed within a few frames of the preceding forward step, creating a smooth zig‑zag path, while the low‑reward episodes contain either overly sharp, late turns or excessive oscillations that amplify positional error. This disciplined interleaving of motion and correction prevents the agent from hitting obstacles, preserving cumulative reward, whereas the lack of timely corrections in the low‑reward rollouts leads to rapid loss of control and large penalties.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts consistently feature long, uninterrupted sequences of action 0 (forward thrust) that keep the agent’s state within a narrow band, only inserting brief corrective actions 1 or 2 when a deviation is detected; this steady forward drive yields large positive step rewards. In contrast, low‑reward episodes either terminate early or spend most of the horizon executing rapid alternations of actions 0, 1 and 2, producing large, erratic state jumps and frequent collisions that generate negative rewards. The successful rollouts also time their corrections just after a slight overshoot—evident from a single corrective action followed by a return to action 0—whereas the failing rollouts either over‑correct (multiple consecutive turns) or fail to correct at all, leading to loss of control. Moreover, high‑reward trajectories avoid prolonged pauses or “stop” actions, keeping momentum, while the low‑reward examples include stops or back‑and‑forth oscillations that waste steps and increase exposure to walls. These timing and action‑selection differences plausibly explain why the former accumulate ~1500 reward points and the latter remain near zero or negative.

[WORST POLICY]
The high‑reward rollouts all contain a decisive, large‑magnitude action early in the episode (often a strong thrust or an abrupt corrective “0” command) that drives the agent into a terminal state within only a few steps, whereas the low‑reward rollouts persist with the same modest forward action (usually “2”) for many frames, producing long, oscillatory trajectories. This early, aggressive move caps the number of timesteps the agent accrues penalties, so the total negative reward stays relatively modest. In contrast, the sustained “2” actions cause the state value to swing wildly back and forth, keeping the agent alive but continuously incurring step‑wise costs that sum to a much larger negative total. Thus, the presence of an early, high‑impact action versus prolonged repetitive actions is the key behavioral difference that explains the reward gap.
