[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts repeatedly interleave short bursts of turning actions (actions 1 or 2) with longer stretches of the forward action (action 0), using the turns only to fine‑tune alignment before resuming forward motion; low‑reward rollouts either persist with a single forward action for too many frames or execute an abrupt, prolonged turn that never settles back into forward progress, leading to collisions. In the successful episodes the agent delays large‑angle turns until after it has already moved a few steps forward, allowing it to gather information about the corridor before reorienting, whereas the poor episodes make a big turn immediately or accelerate into a turn without first establishing a stable forward trajectory. The timing of corrections is also distinct: high‑reward runs apply brief corrective turns every 2‑4 frames, keeping the trajectory roughly straight, while low‑reward runs either omit corrections entirely or apply them too late, resulting in runaway drift and eventual failure. This pattern of “forward‑most, corrective‑turn‑as‑needed” preserves momentum through the maze and prevents the agent from hitting walls, directly explaining the large reward gap.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts consistently alternate short bursts of the forward action (0) with frequent corrective turns (1 or 2) every few frames, producing a steady “wiggle” that keeps the agent hugging walls without hitting them, whereas the low‑reward episodes contain a single, large forward move (often action 2) early on that produces a big state jump and a collision. The successful trajectories also exhibit early, modest adjustments that keep the agent centered in corridors, while the failures show abrupt, over‑steered turns or sudden large‑magnitude actions that send the agent into walls. In the best rollouts the corrective actions are spaced evenly (roughly every 2–4 steps), creating a smooth oscillatory path; the poor rollouts either omit these regular corrections or apply them too late, resulting in a loss of control. This pattern of continuous, low‑amplitude corrections prevents large‑scale errors and allows the agent to accumulate reward, whereas the sporadic, high‑amplitude moves in the low‑reward cases rapidly terminate the episode. Consequently, the presence of frequent, evenly timed turn actions and the avoidance of single, aggressive forward pushes are the key behavioral differences driving the reward gap.

[WORST POLICY]
The high‑reward rollouts all contain a moment where the agent abandons the default “always thrust forward” (action 2) and either turns, reduces thrust, or switches to a neutral action early in the episode, whereas the low‑reward rollouts never deviate and keep applying action 2 for hundreds of steps. This early deviation truncates the episode, preventing the large cumulative penalties that accrue when the agent persists in a monotonic forward thrust and drifts endlessly. In the low‑reward cases the continuous action 2 produces a prolonged, oscillatory drift with repeated overshoots and spikes in the state values, driving the agent into walls or out‑of‑bounds much later and yielding far more negative return. Conversely, the high‑reward trajectories feature only a brief, decisive thrust followed quickly by termination, limiting exposure to the environment’s penalties. These timing and action‑switch differences plausibly account for the observed reward gap.
