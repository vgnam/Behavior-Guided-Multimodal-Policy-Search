[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts are dominated by long, uninterrupted sequences of the forward action (action 0), punctuated only by occasional, modest corrective turns (actions 1 or 2) that occur after many steps of steady progress; this creates a smooth, monotonic increase in the state value and prevents large, sudden jumps. In contrast, the low‑reward episodes either terminate after the very first forward step with a huge negative reward or alternate rapidly between opposite actions, producing back‑and‑forth oscillations and abrupt position changes that quickly drive the agent into failure states. The successful rollouts also time their corrective turns later—usually after a clear stretch of positive reward—whereas the failing rollouts over‑react early, turning or switching actions at the first sign of deviation and thereby destabilising the trajectory. By maintaining forward momentum and only applying small, well‑timed adjustments, the high‑reward behavior keeps the agent within safe corridors, while the premature or excessive corrections seen in the low‑reward cases generate instability and the large penalties that drive down the total return.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts consistently interleave long stretches of action 0 (steady forward thrust) with brief, well‑timed corrective actions of type 2 (small backward or sideways nudges) and occasional single‑step action 1 adjustments, creating a rhythm of forward progress punctuated by rapid stabilization; low‑reward rollouts either omit these corrective bursts or apply action 2 for prolonged periods, producing large, uncontrolled jumps that drive the state far from the target. In the successful trajectories the agent switches from action 0 to a corrective action just before the state begins to drift, whereas the failing runs let the drift continue and only react later, if at all, resulting in chaotic overshoots. The high‑reward episodes also exhibit early‑phase stabilization, with the first 30–50 steps dominated by smooth action 0, while the poor episodes show abrupt state changes within the first few dozen steps, indicating premature aggressive thrusts. This pattern of disciplined forward motion combined with timely, brief corrections keeps the agent inside rewarding zones, whereas the absence or reversal of those corrections leads to loss of control and large negative rewards.

[WORST POLICY]
The high‑reward rollouts all contain an early, purposeful change of action – a sharp turn or a brief reversal of thrust – that steers the agent onto a wall‑following path, whereas the low‑reward rollouts keep the same forward‑thrust action for the entire episode and never execute a corrective turn. This early re‑orientation limits lateral drift and keeps the state values within a tighter band, so the agent spends more time progressing rather than oscillating wildly. In the better rollouts the agent also inserts short periods of reduced or opposite thrust before collisions, which slows its approach to walls and prevents the large overshoots seen in the worst rollouts. By contrast, the low‑reward episodes show persistent, unmodulated thrust that produces high‑amplitude oscillations and frequent over‑corrections, driving the state far from the intended corridor and causing earlier terminations. These timing and action‑change differences plausibly explain why the high‑reward trajectories lose less reward despite still ending in failure.
