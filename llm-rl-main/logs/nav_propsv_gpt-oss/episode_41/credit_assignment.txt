[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts all begin with a brief forward move (action 0) followed almost immediately (within 1–2 steps) by a small corrective turn or backward tweak (actions 1 or 2), after which they resume forward progress and keep any subsequent corrections short and infrequent; this rapid “detect‑and‑adjust” pattern prevents the agent from drifting far off the optimal path. In contrast, low‑reward rollouts either sustain a long burst of forward thrust without early correction or postpone the corrective turn until many steps later, which lets errors accumulate and often ends in a collision. The successful rollouts also intersperse short pauses or minimal‑speed actions that let the state settle before the next move, whereas the failing rollouts maintain aggressive acceleration until the episode terminates. Moreover, the high‑reward trajectories limit the length of any oscillatory segment to just a few steps, using it only for fine‑tuning, while the low‑reward ones exhibit growing oscillations that dominate the episode and destabilize control. This combination of early, brief corrections, occasional pauses, and tightly bounded oscillations keeps the agent on a smooth, forward‑driving path, directly translating into the large reward differences observed.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts repeatedly alternate a short burst of the forward‑movement action (action 0) with an immediate corrective turn (actions 1 or 2), keeping the state value inside a narrow band and preventing large excursions; low‑reward rollouts either omit those corrective turns or delay them, allowing a single forward thrust to drive the state to a spike that terminates the episode. The timing of the turns is also consistent: in successful runs the agent turns within a few frames after each forward step, whereas failures show long runs of forward actions followed by a sudden, unchecked jump. Successful trajectories stay close to the walls of the maze, using frequent, modest turns to “hug” the boundary, while unsuccessful ones drift away from the corridor and only make a late, large‑amplitude correction that proves too late. Finally, the high‑reward episodes exhibit bounded oscillations—small back‑and‑forth adjustments that continually reset the position—whereas the low‑reward episodes display monotonic drift or abrupt divergence, which directly leads to the large negative rewards.

[WORST POLICY]
The high‑reward rollouts all contain an early deviation from a long streak of forward‑thrust actions (action 2), inserting a turn or brake (action 0/1) within the first few dozen steps, whereas the low‑reward rollouts persist with action 2 for hundreds of timesteps before an abrupt switch to 0 that precipitates a massive crash. This early corrective maneuver keeps the agent’s state value from spiralling upward and limits the distance it drifts toward the outer wall, so the collision that does occur carries a relatively modest penalty. In the high‑reward episodes the agent also shows short, purposeful oscillations—small over‑ and under‑steers that are quickly countered—while the low‑reward runs exhibit unchecked oscillations that grow larger and only collapse when the sudden stop is applied. By regularly adjusting its heading instead of riding a single forward command, the agent avoids the large‑magnitude state spikes that trigger the worst negative rewards, explaining why those patterns correlate with the comparatively higher (i.e., less negative) returns.
