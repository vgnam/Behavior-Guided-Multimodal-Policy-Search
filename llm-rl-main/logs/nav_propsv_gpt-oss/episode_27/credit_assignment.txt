[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts consistently begin by moving away from the start quickly and then follow each wall or corner with a single, decisive turn that is executed as soon as the change in geometry is encountered, whereas low‑reward rollouts often linger near the start or overshoot a corner before turning, losing valuable distance. In the successful episodes the agent strings together longer uninterrupted forward sequences (several consecutive forward actions) and inserts only brief, crisp turn actions, while the poorer episodes break forward motion into many short bursts punctuated by frequent, jittery turns that waste steps. When an over‑correction occurs in the high‑reward runs, it is corrected within one or two steps, producing only a small oscillation; in contrast, low‑reward rollouts exhibit prolonged back‑and‑forth swings that persist for several frames before the heading settles. This combination of early, purposeful displacement, well‑timed corner‑aligned turns, and rapid damping of overshoot yields smoother overall trajectories and therefore higher cumulative reward.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts consistently interleave short bursts of forward action 0 with frequent, promptly timed corrective actions 1 or 2, keeping the state within a narrow band and allowing the agent to recover quickly from any drift; low‑reward rollouts either omit these corrective moves or apply them far too late, resulting in prolonged runs of pure forward thrusts or abrupt large‑angle turns that push the agent into walls. In the successful episodes the agent stabilises early, using brief pauses or mild adjustments to maintain a smooth, oscillatory trajectory, whereas the failures show aggressive, sustained motion followed by sudden, unchecked direction changes that cause collisions. The timing pattern in the high‑reward cases—forward for a few frames, then an immediate small correction—prevents the state from leaving the safe corridor, while the low‑reward cases let the state wander unchecked until it reaches a terminal region. Consequently, the disciplined alternation of modest forward steps with rapid corrective actions yields steady reward accumulation, whereas the lack of such disciplined pacing leads to instability and large negative penalties.

[WORST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts all contain an early, purposeful turn (usually a left or right) that aligns the agent with the next corridor segment, whereas the low‑reward rollouts never deviate from the default forward action (action 2) and therefore run straight into walls. In the better rollouts the agent inserts a corrective action (often a brief action 0 or a gentle opposite turn) within the first two‑three frames to curb a slight lateral drift, while the poorer rollouts show no such correction and let the drift grow unchecked. The successful trajectories maintain a relatively smooth, monotonic distance from the wall, only adjusting when a corner is reached; the failing trajectories exhibit continuous oscillations in the state value, indicating repeated over‑corrections without any planned navigation. By turning at the appropriate moment and applying a timely corrective action, the high‑reward episodes avoid collisions and preserve forward progress, whereas the low‑reward episodes’ lack of turning and correction leads to wall impacts and premature episode termination, driving the large reward gap.
