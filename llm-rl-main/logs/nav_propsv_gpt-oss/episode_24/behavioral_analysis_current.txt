**1. SUCCESS PATTERNS**  
Across the majority of the top‑reward rollouts the agent **maintains a steady forward command (action ≈ 0) for a long initial stretch (≈ > 100 steps)**, producing a smooth, monotonic trajectory with little lateral jitter. This stable “cruise” keeps the state value gradually decreasing (i.e., moving toward the target) and avoids any sudden heading changes until well after the halfway point of the episode. When a turn is finally required, the agent executes a **single, deliberate 90° pivot** rather than a series of rapid oscillations, which preserves momentum and prevents the drift that later leads to failure. The combination of prolonged straight‑line motion and a single, well‑timed cornering action yields the highest episodic rewards observed.

**2. FAILURE PATTERNS**  
The low‑reward rollouts share a clear pattern of **early loss of directional stability**: within the first 5–10 steps the agent either veers sharply to the side or collides with a wall, often after a single forward action. This is accompanied by **rapid, high‑frequency oscillations** or abrupt heading changes (multiple successive turns) that prevent the state value from decreasing steadily. The result is an immediate large negative reward and termination, indicating that uncontrolled drift and over‑steering are the primary drivers of poor performance.

**3. DISCRIMINATIVE PATTERNS** (ranked by strength)  
1. **Extended straight‑line cruise (≥ 100 steps of constant forward action)** – present in 4/5 high‑reward rollouts, absent in all low‑reward ones.  
2. **Single, well‑timed 90° turn after the cruise** – appears in 3/5 high‑reward rollouts, never in low‑reward rollouts.  
3. **Early chaotic veering (multiple direction changes within the first 10 steps)** – seen in 4/5 low‑reward rollouts, only in 1 high‑reward rollout.  

The optimizer should strongly **reinforce** pattern 1 (and to a lesser extent pattern 2) and **suppress** pattern 3.

**4. TIMING**  
Pattern 1 (extended cruise) manifests almost immediately after episode start and persists through the majority of the episode, making it a causal driver of success. Pattern 2 (the single delayed turn) occurs later, roughly halfway through the episode, acting as a necessary but secondary trigger that caps the benefit of the early cruise. Pattern 3 (early chaotic veering) appears within the first few timesteps, indicating that preventing this early instability is critical for avoiding catastrophic failure.
