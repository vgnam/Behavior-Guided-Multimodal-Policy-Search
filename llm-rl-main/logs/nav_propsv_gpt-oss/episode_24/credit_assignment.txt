[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts consistently begin with a prolonged forward action (action 0) that carries the agent several steps before any turn, establishing steady progress and accumulating positive reward early, whereas low‑reward rollouts intersperse forward moves with immediate left/right actions (1 or 2), breaking momentum. When a direction change is needed, the successful trajectories execute a single, decisive sharp turn at the exact frame where the state indicates an upcoming corner, then quickly resume forward motion; the poorer runs hesitate, alternating between left and right turns or making multiple small corrections around the same point. The better rollouts show long, uninterrupted forward segments after each turn, limiting the frequency of oscillatory back‑and‑forth adjustments, while the bad rollouts contain frequent, short forward bursts punctuated by rapid, opposite‑direction actions that produce over‑corrections. Moreover, high‑reward episodes maintain a relatively constant orientation for several frames before correcting, whereas low‑reward episodes display rapid orientation flips every few steps, indicating unstable control. These timing and action patterns reduce wasted motion and negative rewards in the high‑reward cases, directly translating into the observed reward gap.

[BEST POLICY]
The high‑reward rollouts all keep a baseline of forward thrusts (action 0) that earn the +5 reward, and they sprinkle in brief, timed corrective actions (1 or 2) only when the state value drifts from the desired corridor, which quickly restores alignment without large overshoots; the low‑reward rollouts either sustain a long, unchecked forward thrust (often action 2) or wait until the very end to make a big turn, producing a sudden large deviation and collision. In the successful runs the agent “hugs” the wall by turning early and then immediately returning to action 0, creating a smooth oscillatory pattern that maintains proximity to safe boundaries, whereas the failures either ignore the wall until a late, aggressive turn or never correct their trajectory at all. The timing of corrections is also distinct: good episodes apply a corrective action as soon as the state exceeds a modest threshold (within the first few frames), while bad episodes let the error accumulate for many steps before a drastic, destabilising maneuver. Finally, the high‑reward behavior shows short bursts of action 1/2 followed by immediate resumption of action 0, keeping velocity low enough to avoid impact, whereas the low‑reward behavior exhibits prolonged periods of the same action leading to excess speed and loss of control, directly accounting for the large reward gap.

[WORST POLICY]
The high‑reward rollouts all feature an abrupt, high‑magnitude forward thrust (action = 2) right at the first timestep, producing a rapid jump in the state value and causing the episode to terminate within only a few frames; this early termination limits the cumulative negative reward. In contrast, the low‑reward rollouts sustain a modest, nearly constant action for many hundreds of frames, yielding long‑lasting oscillations in the state and continual penalty accrual before a late‑stage failure. The better rollouts also include a single, sudden orientation change that drives the agent straight into a wall, whereas the poorer rollouts display continuous corrective adjustments and no comparable sudden turn. Because the aggressive, early‑stage move ends the episode quickly, it yields a much less negative total reward than the prolonged, oscillatory behaviour that dominates the low‑reward trajectories.
