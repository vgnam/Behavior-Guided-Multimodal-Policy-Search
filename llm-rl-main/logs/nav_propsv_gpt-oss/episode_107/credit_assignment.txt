[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts consistently begin with a sustained forward thrust (action 0) that carries the agent well into the corridor before any turn is attempted, and when a turn is needed it is executed with a brief, precise corrective action (one or two steps of action 1 or 2) followed immediately by another forward stretch; this pattern keeps the trajectory near the centre line and prevents contact with walls. In contrast, the low‑reward episodes either introduce a turn too early or apply a large‑angle turn that lasts several steps, allowing the agent to drift into obstacles before any corrective forward motion is taken. The successful rollouts also show rapid damping of oscillations – each small deviation is counter‑acted within a couple of timesteps, whereas the poorer rollouts exhibit prolonged over‑corrections that amplify the oscillation and lead to instability. Finally, high‑reward trajectories contain few abrupt, high‑penalty actions (e.g., sudden backward or sideways moves) and instead rely on smooth, incremental adjustments, while the low‑reward cases feature occasional sudden large‑magnitude actions that produce immediate negative rewards and terminate the episode. These timing and magnitude differences explain why the former maintain steady progress and accrue high returns, whereas the latter incur collisions or overshoots that sharply reduce reward.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts all contain extended uninterrupted stretches of a forward‑motion action (typically action 0 or 2) that begin early in the episode and persist for many steps, allowing the agent to accumulate large positive rewards before any corrective turn is made; by contrast, the low‑reward episodes break forward motion almost immediately with a turn (action 1 or 2) or a pause, producing short positive bursts followed by negative‐reward corrections. The successful runs also show only occasional, sparsely timed corrective actions, whereas the failing runs exhibit rapid, back‑to‑back opposite actions (e.g., 0 → 1 → 0) that create oscillatory “jerky” motion and quickly drain reward. Moreover, the high‑reward trajectories maintain a consistent heading for most of the episode, only making minor adjustments when necessary, while the low‑reward trajectories display abrupt heading changes or over‑corrections that lead to loss of control and crashes. These timing and action‑selection differences plausibly explain the reward gap: steady forward thrust builds reward, whereas early or frequent direction flips generate penalties and destabilize the agent.

[WORST POLICY]
The high‑reward rollouts all contain at least one distinct deviation from the default “action 2” thrust—either an early turn, a sudden stop, or a brief reversal—whereas the low‑reward rollouts keep using action 2 for every step, producing long‑lasting forward motion. This single change cuts the episode length and prevents the agent from accumulating the per‑step penalty that dominates the low‑reward trajectories. In the high‑reward cases the state value spikes briefly and then drops, indicating a quick correction that keeps the agent from drifting far into the maze; the low‑reward runs show large, sustained excursions in the state value because no corrective action ever occurs. Finally, the high‑reward episodes terminate soon after the corrective action, so the total negative reward is limited, while the low‑reward episodes continue oscillating for many frames, compounding the negative reward.
