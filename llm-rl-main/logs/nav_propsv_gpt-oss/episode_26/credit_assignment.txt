[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts consistently begin with a forward move (action 2) followed almost immediately by a brief corrective turn (action 0), establishing a rhythm of short forward bursts punctuated by quick adjustments that keep the agent centered on the corridor; low‑reward rollouts either sustain a long straight thrust without any early correction or make a single turn far later, allowing the trajectory to drift toward walls before any correction is attempted. The successful trajectories also intersperse occasional backward steps (action 1) only as brief stabilising nudges after a turn, whereas the failures rarely use backward actions at all, so they cannot recover from overshoot. Timing-wise, the high‑reward episodes show regular, low‑latency alternation—forward, turn, forward—every few timesteps, creating a smooth oscillatory gait, while the low‑reward cases exhibit long gaps between any directional change, leading to sudden, large‑magnitude deviations and collisions. Finally, the high‑reward agents stay close to the outer boundary and follow it systematically, using the wall as a guide, whereas the low‑reward agents wander away from the boundary early and lose the spatial reference that prevents catastrophic exits. This combination of early, frequent corrective turns, judicious use of brief backward nudges, and consistent wall‑following produces steady progress and high cumulative reward, while its absence results in uncontrolled drift and large negative penalties.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts repeatedly begin with a decisive forward move (action 2) and then maintain long stretches of forward‑biased actions, only inserting brief corrective bursts of action 0 or 1 at regular intervals to keep the state within a narrow band; this keeps the agent progressing toward the goal while preventing drift. In contrast, low‑reward rollouts either start with premature lateral moves (action 0) or insert corrective actions too early or too often, causing the state to oscillate wildly and eventually collide with walls. The successful trajectories also delay major direction changes until the agent has accumulated a stable forward momentum, whereas the failing runs make abrupt turns mid‑episode, breaking the smooth momentum and incurring large negative rewards. Moreover, the high‑reward episodes exhibit a pattern of short, timed “over‑correction” spikes that quickly return the state to the desired corridor, while the low‑reward episodes lack this disciplined reset and instead let errors compound. This disciplined timing of forward thrusts combined with sparing, well‑placed corrections explains why the high‑reward rollouts achieve substantially higher returns.

[WORST POLICY]
The high‑reward rollouts all contain at least one deliberate change of action early in the episode—typically a brief left or right thrust that reorients the agent toward the corridor’s bend—whereas the low‑reward rollouts stick with the same action (action = 2) for the entire run and never execute a corrective turn. In the better rollouts the agent’s thrusts are timed: an initial forward push is followed by a short pause and then a corrective maneuver, which limits overshoot and prevents the agent from repeatedly colliding with the walls; the poorer rollouts show continuous, unmodulated thrusts that produce large oscillations in position. Moreover, the high‑reward trajectories exhibit occasional back‑off or reverse thrust after a drift, smoothing the motion, while the low‑reward trajectories lack any backward or opposite‑direction actions, resulting in persistent jitter. These timing and action‑variation patterns allow the agent to align with the L‑shaped corridor and avoid chaotic, oscillatory motion, which directly translates into a higher (less negative) cumulative reward.
