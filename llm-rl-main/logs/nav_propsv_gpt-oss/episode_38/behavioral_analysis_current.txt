High‑reward rollouts all begin with a modest, steady forward (or rightward) thrust that is maintained for several time‑steps before any sharp turn or correction is attempted, allowing the agent to accrue positive reward and stay alive longer; by contrast, the low‑reward rollouts execute a large‑magnitude action almost immediately, producing a rapid drop in the state variable and an early termination. When a direction change does occur in the successful runs, it is timed after the agent has built up momentum and is accompanied by a single, decisive turn, whereas the failures show abrupt, un‑timed jumps that send the agent into walls or off‑track positions. The higher‑reward trajectories also display smoother, more monotonic progress toward the goal (or at least away from obstacles) with little oscillation until very late in the episode, while the worst rollouts exhibit sudden oscillatory or over‑corrective motions right after the first step. This pattern suggests that a controlled, gradual acceleration and delayed, purposeful steering keeps the agent in safe regions longer, whereas premature, aggressive actions trigger immediate collisions and large negative rewards. Consequently, the timing and magnitude of the initial actions—steady versus abrupt—appear to be the key behavioral difference driving the reward gap.
