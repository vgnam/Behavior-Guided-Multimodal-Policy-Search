[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts repeatedly interleave a short burst of forward thrust (action 0) that yields a +5.0 reward with an immediate corrective turn (action 1 or 2) within one or two frames, keeping the state confined to the narrow “sweet‑spot” band around 1–2; low‑reward rollouts either sustain forward thrust for many steps without a timely correction or postpone the turn until the state has already drifted far outside that band, leading to large negative rewards or collisions. The successful runs also exhibit a regular rhythm of positive‑reward spikes every few steps, whereas the failing runs show long stretches with no +5.0 reward and only occasional, poorly timed turns that cause abrupt drops in state value. By correcting almost as soon as the state begins to oscillate, the high‑reward policy prevents the accumulation of momentum that later results in overshoot, while the low‑reward policy lets momentum build unchecked until a catastrophic turn is forced. This disciplined timing of corrective actions therefore preserves stability and yields the higher episodic returns.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts repeatedly exhibit a **gradual, oscillation‑damped forward thrust** early on, followed by **small corrective turns only after the agent has settled into a stable trajectory**, whereas low‑reward rollouts either **accelerate abruptly** or **make large directional jumps** within the first few hundred steps, causing immediate loss of control. The successful episodes also show a **consistent pattern of short forward bursts interleaved with brief pauses** that let the agent “sample” the corridor before committing to a turn, while the failures lack these pauses and instead execute **continuous aggressive actions (e.g., sustained action 2) that drive the state beyond safe limits**. Moreover, high‑reward trajectories tend to **delay sharp turns until a clear corner is reached**, using modest corrective actions (often action 0) only at well‑timed moments, whereas low‑reward runs apply sharp turns too early or too late, leading to collisions or drifts. This timing discipline prevents overshoot and keeps the agent inside the navigable corridor, directly translating into higher cumulative reward.

[WORST POLICY]
The high‑reward rollouts all contain an early, deliberate change of heading – the agent turns once (or twice) within the first few frames to line up with the corridor or corner, then proceeds with a short, smooth forward thrust before stopping or colliding only after a brief, bounded trajectory. By contrast, the low‑reward rollouts never execute such early turns; they persist with the same forward‑thrust action throughout the episode, producing long, repetitive oscillations that keep the agent wandering back and forth and repeatedly striking walls. This early alignment in the better rollouts limits exposure to the maze’s tight geometry, so the agent accrues fewer penalty steps before termination, whereas the constant‑action, oscillatory behaviour of the worse rollouts forces the agent to spend many steps in unstable, high‑penalty states. Consequently, the presence of an initial corrective turn and a brief, controlled motion segment explains why the high‑reward trajectories lose far less reward than the endlessly thrusting, uncorrected low‑reward ones.
