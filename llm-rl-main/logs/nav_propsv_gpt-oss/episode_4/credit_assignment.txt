[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts all contain a clear, sustained burst of action 0 (forward thrust) that is triggered after an initial positioning phase, whereas the low‑reward runs either never produce such a burst or abort before it can begin. In the top rollouts the agent first follows the outer wall using alternating actions 1 and 2, then—typically around frame 8‑10—switches to a straight‑ahead drive that rapidly advances toward the goal; the low‑reward episodes either continue wobbling with actions 1/2, revert to a chaotic mix of actions, or terminate immediately after a single forward step. The successful trajectories also show a short period of smooth, monotonic forward motion before any oscillatory corrections appear, while the failures exhibit immediate over‑corrections or abrupt resets that prevent accumulation of positive reward. Finally, when oscillations do occur in the high‑reward cases they are bounded by a preceding forward surge, whereas in the low‑reward cases the oscillations dominate the entire episode, leading to missed targets and collisions. These timing and action‑selection differences explain why the high‑reward rollouts gather much larger returns.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts all begin with a sustained forward thrust (action 0) that carries the agent well away from the start region, whereas the low‑reward episodes either truncate that forward phase early or replace it with an immediate sharp turn that drives the agent into a wall. When the agent drifts off the optimal path, the successful rollouts insert a brief, well‑timed corrective action (a single 1 or 2) and then quickly resume forward motion, keeping the trajectory smooth; the poor rollouts either omit the correction or apply a delayed, exaggerated turn that creates a sudden, large negative reward. The high‑reward trajectories also limit back‑and‑forth oscillations, using only occasional, short‑duration adjustments, while the low‑reward cases exhibit repeated alternating left/right actions that amplify positional error and increase collision risk. Finally, the successful rollouts maintain a monotonic increase in state value throughout most of the episode, reflecting consistent progress toward the goal, whereas the failing rollouts show abrupt drops in state right before termination, indicating loss of control that directly depresses the cumulative reward.

[WORST POLICY]
The high‑reward rollouts all contain a short burst of the forward‑thrust action 2 followed almost immediately by a change of action (often 0 or a small rotation), which causes the episode to terminate after only a few steps; the low‑reward rollouts, by contrast, persist with action 2 for dozens of frames, producing long, oscillatory trajectories that accumulate large negative rewards. In the better rollouts the agent makes a single, purposeful turn or a brief wall‑following maneuver—evidenced by a quick switch to a different action—whereas the poorer rollouts never break the monotony of forward thrust and instead swing back and forth along the same axis. This early diversification of actions prevents the agent from drifting into costly collisions or endless loops, keeping the total penalty small. The low‑reward trajectories show repeated overshoots and resets (large jumps in state) that only occur when the agent keeps applying the same action despite worsening position, amplifying the penalty. Consequently, brief, adaptive action changes and early episode termination are the key behavioral differences that explain why the high‑reward rollouts achieve markedly better scores.
