[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts all begin with a sustained sequence of action 0 (forward) that quickly establishes forward momentum, whereas low‑reward runs either start with mixed actions 1/2 or intersperse turns almost immediately, breaking that momentum. In the successful episodes the occasional corrective actions (a single 1 or 2) occur only after several uninterrupted forward steps and are followed by an immediate return to action 0, producing brief, purposeful adjustments; the failing episodes exhibit frequent back‑and‑forth switches between 0, 1 and 2, creating continuous oscillations and no clear recovery to forward motion. The high‑reward trajectories also keep the state value within a narrow band (≈0.7–2.9), indicating a stable distance from walls, while low‑reward paths let the state drift to extremes (near 0 or > 10) before a collision penalty appears. Moreover, successful rollouts place any negative‑reward actions late in the episode and quickly regain positive forward steps, whereas the poor rollouts accrue negative rewards early or repeatedly, never regaining a smooth forward streak. These timing‑consistent patterns—early sustained forward thrust, sparse and isolated corrections, and maintained proximity limits—prevent destabilizing oscillations and collisions, thereby yielding substantially higher cumulative rewards.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts all begin with a sustained forward action (action 0) that carries the agent deep into the arena before any turning is attempted, whereas low‑reward runs either turn early (action 2 or 1) or intersperse forward moves with abrupt backward steps. In the successful episodes the agent introduces turns only after a short, stable forward segment and the turn is modest – a single action 2 followed quickly by another forward step – which keeps the trajectory smooth; the poor runs exhibit large, back‑to‑back turns or immediate reversals that push the state out of bounds. Moreover, the high‑reward trajectories maintain a regular rhythm of “forward → small correction → forward” without long pauses, while the low‑reward ones contain irregular gaps where the agent stalls or over‑corrects, leading to delayed responses and collisions. Finally, the best rollouts avoid any prolonged backward thrusts (negative‑reward actions) and instead use brief, low‑amplitude adjustments, a pattern that is absent in the failing rollouts where backward actions dominate the later frames, causing the agent to lose control and terminate early. These timing and action‑selection differences keep the agent oriented toward the goal and prevent the destabilizing oscillations that drive down the reward.

[WORST POLICY]
The high‑reward rollouts all contain an early non‑forward decision – a sharp turn or lateral thrust within the first few frames – that breaks the monotonic “action 2” forward thrust seen in the low‑reward runs; this early directional change causes the episode to terminate quickly, limiting the accumulation of step‑wise penalties. In contrast, the low‑reward trajectories are dominated by a constant forward action at every timestep, producing long, oscillatory drifts that keep the agent alive for the full horizon and thus accrue the maximum negative reward. The high‑reward episodes also exhibit brief, decisive adjustments rather than the repeated over‑corrections and jittery oscillations characteristic of the low‑reward cases, which further reduces the number of penalising steps. Consequently, the combination of an early, purposeful turn and a much shorter episode length plausibly accounts for the comparatively better (less negative) rewards in the high‑reward rollouts.
