[CURRENT POLICY]
In the high‑reward episodes the agent typically opens with several consecutive forward actions (action 2) that move it smoothly toward a wall, and it only executes a turn (action 0 or 1) when contact with the boundary is imminent, immediately following that turn with another forward action to stay aligned with the edge. This disciplined “forward‑then‑turn‑then‑forward” rhythm keeps the state trajectory confined to a narrow range and allows quick drift correction without large reversals. The low‑reward rollouts, however, often begin with an early turn or a backward‑like motion, generate sudden large jumps in the state value, and then neglect an immediate forward correction, resulting in overshoots, collisions, or erratic oscillations. The consistent presence of timely forward‑after‑turn corrections and the avoidance of abrupt, uncorrected turns therefore separates the successful trajectories from the failing ones and accounts for the observed reward disparity.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts all begin with a long stretch of pure forward actions (action 0) that steadily raises the state value before any turn is taken, whereas the low‑reward episodes either turn immediately (action 1/2) or make a single forward step and then terminate. In the successful runs the agent introduces turns only after it has accumulated momentum, using brief, alternating left/right adjustments (actions 1 and 2) that correct its heading without breaking forward progress; the failing runs either lack these corrective micro‑oscillations or apply a large turn too early, causing a rapid drift toward a wall. The timing of turns is also distinct: high‑reward trajectories space turns out over dozens or hundreds of steps, creating smooth arcs, while low‑reward trajectories cluster turns together or execute a single turn that ends the episode. Finally, the high‑reward behavior shows a gradual, monotonic increase in the state (or position) metric, indicating sustained navigation through the corridor, whereas low‑reward rollouts exhibit abrupt spikes or drops in the state followed by immediate negative rewards, reflecting collisions or loss of control. These differences plausibly explain the reward gap because sustained forward motion builds cumulative reward and delayed, small corrective turns keep the agent inside safe regions, while early or aggressive turning leads to boundary contact and large penalties.

[WORST POLICY]
The high‑reward rollouts all contain an early change of action – typically a single turn (action 0) or a brief backing step within the first few frames – that breaks a long streak of forward thrust (action 2), whereas the low‑reward rollouts keep applying action 2 for most of the episode and only attempt a turn at the very end. This early deviation prevents the state value from climbing to the large magnitudes (e.g., > 8 or 10) that dominate the low‑reward trajectories, keeping the agent’s trajectory short and reducing accumulated step‑penalties. Moreover, the high‑reward runs show only modest, brief oscillations in the state, while the low‑reward runs exhibit repeated overshoots and undershoots, indicating unstable control that inflates the negative reward. Because the environment appears to penalize both time‑spent and extreme state excursions, the combination of an early corrective action and limited oscillation plausibly yields the comparatively higher (less negative) returns observed in the high‑reward rollouts.
