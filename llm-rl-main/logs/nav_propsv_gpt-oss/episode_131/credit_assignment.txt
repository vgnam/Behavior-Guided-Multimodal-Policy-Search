[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts all begin with an extended burst of action 2 (steady forward thrust) that lasts several timesteps before any turn is taken, whereas low‑reward rollouts immediately alternate between actions 0/1, producing rapid, short‑lived direction changes. In the successful runs the first sharp turn (action 0) occurs only after the forward segment has moved the agent close to a waypoint, while in the poor runs turns are triggered prematurely, often while the agent is still near its start location. The high‑reward trajectories also exhibit long idle or small‑adjustment intervals followed by another sustained forward phase, creating a rhythm of “push‑then‑correct”; the low‑reward trajectories lack these pauses and instead perform continuous, jittery corrections with no clear forward momentum. Finally, the high‑reward episodes contain fewer consecutive negative‑reward steps after a turn, suggesting that the timing of turns avoids collisions or boundary violations, a pattern that is absent in the low‑reward rollouts where back‑to‑back turns lead to repeated penalties. These systematic differences in action duration, turn timing, and the presence of purposeful pause‑and‑thrust cycles plausibly explain why the former achieve higher cumulative rewards.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts repeatedly interleave a dominant forward‑thrust action (action 0) with brief, well‑timed corrective turns (actions 1 or 2) whenever the state value drifts beyond a narrow band, keeping the agent’s trajectory centered and preventing long excursions; in contrast, low‑reward rollouts either sustain a single action for many steps (e.g., continuous forward thrust or unchecked turning) or apply corrections too late, allowing the state to wander far from the optimal corridor. The successful runs also exhibit early stabilization, showing a quick reduction of the initial state value within the first few timesteps, whereas the poor runs start with large state values that only later decline—or never do—leading to collisions or failures. Moreover, high‑reward episodes tend to avoid prolonged periods of the same corrective action, instead alternating between left and right turns to fine‑tune the path, while low‑reward episodes display monotonic drift in one direction, reflecting a lack of responsive steering. These patterns plausibly boost reward because they maintain the agent within the narrow, reward‑rich region of the maze and prevent the costly wall contacts that dominate the low‑reward trajectories.

[WORST POLICY]
The high‑reward rollouts all contain at least one moment where the agent deviates from the default “action 2” thrust – either by issuing a single turn (action 0) or by stopping the thrust altogether – which quickly reduces the magnitude of its state changes, whereas the low‑reward rollouts stick to action 2 for the entire episode, producing large, repeated oscillations in the state value. In the better rollouts this corrective or “no‑thrust’’ decision occurs early (within the first few hundred timesteps), truncating the episode and preventing the cumulative penalty that builds up when the agent keeps overshooting; the worst rollouts keep the aggressive thrust on continuously, so the state swings back and forth without ever stabilising. Moreover, the higher‑reward trajectories show either a single, modest forward push followed by an immediate halt or a very gentle, almost negligible movement, which limits collisions with walls, while the low‑reward runs exhibit persistent high‑amplitude swings that repeatedly drive the agent into obstacles. These timing and action‑selection differences plausibly explain why the former earn less negative rewards: they introduce occasional corrective actions that curb runaway motion, whereas the latter’s unvarying thrust leads to sustained instability and maximal penalties.
