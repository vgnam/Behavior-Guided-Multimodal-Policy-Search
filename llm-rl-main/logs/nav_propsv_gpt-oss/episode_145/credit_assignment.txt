[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts consistently contain long uninterrupted stretches of the forward action (action 0) that drive the state value steadily downward before any turn is attempted, whereas low‑reward rollouts break forward motion with frequent immediate switches to turning actions (1 or 2), producing rapid oscillations in the state. When a turn is needed, the successful trajectories time it near a clear corner and execute a single sharp turn, then resume a burst of forward steps; the poor trajectories over‑correct by alternating left/right turns in quick succession, never allowing the forward motion to accumulate. The high‑reward episodes also show a low‑variance state trajectory – the state value declines smoothly with only occasional, brief spikes – while the low‑reward ones exhibit large, erratic fluctuations that often culminate in a “fail” flag. By keeping the agent’s orientation stable for extended periods, the policy avoids collisions and reaches the goal, whereas the constant over‑correction in the low‑reward rollouts wastes time and triggers early terminations. Consequently, sustained forward progress with judiciously timed, isolated turns explains the systematic reward gap.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts repeatedly begin with a sustained forward action (action 0) that keeps the agent in a stable region for many steps before any turn, whereas low‑reward episodes either turn immediately or intersperse turns with forward moves from the start, causing early destabilisation. When a turn is finally executed in the successful runs, it is preceded by a clear state‑value drop and followed quickly by another block of forward actions, creating a rhythm of “forward → corrective turn → forward” that maintains momentum toward the goal; the failing rollouts show erratic turn timing, often executing sharp turns at high speed or after only a single forward step, which leads to collisions or overshoots. The good trajectories also exhibit occasional brief negative‑reward actions (typically action 1 or 2) that are isolated and quickly compensated by subsequent forward moves, indicating purposeful correction rather than persistent oscillation, while the poor runs contain clusters of negative‑reward turns that are not corrected, resulting in prolonged deviation. Finally, the high‑reward agents display smooth deceleration before sharp turns, allowing them to re‑orient safely, whereas the low‑reward agents accelerate into turns without slowing, producing aggressive thrusts that end in crashes; this disciplined timing preserves alignment with the maze’s corridors and yields substantially higher cumulative reward.

[WORST POLICY]
The higher‑reward rollouts all contain at least one deliberate change of action – a turn away from straight‑forward motion or a brief stop (action 0) – that redirects the agent toward a wall or corridor early in the episode, whereas the lowest‑reward rollouts stick to the same forward action (action 2) for dozens of steps without any corrective turn. This early directional adjustment lets the agent follow the maze boundaries instead of plowing straight into obstacles, reducing the magnitude of the penalty when the episode ends. In the better rollouts the timing of the turn is clustered in the first few frames, providing a short burst of controlled movement before any later erratic drift, while the poor rollouts never introduce a corrective maneuver and therefore accumulate larger negative rewards from continuous collisions. Consequently, the presence of an early, purposeful turn (or stop) and the absence of relentless forward thrust are the key behavioral differences that explain the reward gap.
