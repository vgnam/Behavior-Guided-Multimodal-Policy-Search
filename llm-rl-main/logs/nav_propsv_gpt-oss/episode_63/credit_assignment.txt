[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts all begin with a purposeful forward thrust followed quickly (within the first few steps) by a sharp turn that lines the agent up with the inner wall of the L‑shaped corridor, after which they keep alternating forward actions with brief corrective turns to stay close to the wall while preserving forward momentum. In contrast, the low‑reward episodes either omit that early turn – driving straight into the wall and terminating instantly – or delay turning until much later, resulting in large‑amplitude oscillations that waste time and lead to loss of control. The successful rollouts also exhibit a steady pattern of short, timed corrections (single‑step turns) rather than prolonged back‑and‑forth jitter, which keeps the state value decreasing steadily toward the goal. Episodes with low reward either show no corrective turns (pure forward drift) or prolonged, erratic jitter that repeatedly reverses progress. Consequently, early, well‑timed cornering combined with consistent, brief forward‑turn alternation produces sustained forward progress and higher cumulative reward, whereas missing or mistimed turns and excessive oscillation halt progress and incur large penalties.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts consistently contain extended sequences of the forward‑movement action (action = 0) that keep the state value in a narrow band, only inserting a single turn (action = 1 or 2) when the geometry of the path demands it; low‑reward rollouts break this pattern with frequent, back‑to‑back turn actions that cause the state to swing wildly and drift from the corridor. In the successful runs the agent’s first turn occurs later in the episode, after it has built up momentum, whereas the poor runs make sharp turns almost immediately, leading to early over‑correction and loss of alignment with the wall‑hugging trajectory. When a turn is required, high‑reward trajectories time it precisely at the point where the outer boundary curves, producing a smooth change of direction, while low‑reward trajectories either turn too early or repeat the same turn direction multiple times, generating oscillatory “zig‑zag” motion. Finally, the high‑reward episodes maintain a relatively stable state magnitude (≈0.7–2.5) throughout, preventing large excursions that incur negative rewards, whereas the low‑reward episodes allow the state to spike to high values before collapsing, which directly translates into the observed reward gap.

[WORST POLICY]
The high‑reward rollouts all feature a very brief action sequence – typically a single, decisive thrust (often action 0 or a one‑step forward) that pushes the agent out of the start region and then ends the episode almost immediately, limiting the time it can accrue negative per‑step penalties. In contrast, the low‑reward rollouts persist with the same action (usually 2) for many steps, producing long stretches of oscillatory or drifting motion that keep the agent in the environment and expose it to repeated penalties before a late collision ends the episode. The brief, early‑stage “burst‑and‑stop” pattern in the better rollouts also prevents the agent from entering the narrow corridor where it would have to make fine‑grained corrections, whereas the poorer rollouts repeatedly overshoot, reverse direction, and over‑correct inside the corridor. By terminating quickly after a single purposeful move, the high‑reward trajectories avoid the cumulative cost of unstable, prolonged control that dominates the low‑reward trajectories.
