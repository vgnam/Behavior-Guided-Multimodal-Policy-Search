[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts repeatedly interleave forward thrusts with brief right‑turn actions, creating a rhythm of short “forward‑then‑turn” segments that continually re‑align the agent with the corridor’s bends; low‑reward rollouts instead sustain a single forward action (often 0) for dozens of steps, so they march straight into walls. The successful trajectories also insert corrective adjustments almost immediately after any small deviation—typically within a couple of time steps—whereas the failing episodes show no such rapid re‑steering, letting errors accumulate. Early‑stage turns (or even a modest backward thrust) are common in the top rollouts, positioning the agent advantageously before the main navigation, while the worst rollouts start flat‑facing and never alter their heading. Finally, the high‑reward runs exhibit occasional brief negative rewards followed by swift recovery, indicating that the agent deliberately backs off from risky spots, whereas the low‑reward runs experience a single catastrophic collision after a prolonged period of unaltered motion. These patterns together explain why the former consistently accrue large rewards and the latter collapse early.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts repeatedly begin with a sustained forward thrust (action 2) that carries the agent deep into the corridor before any turn is made, whereas low‑reward rollouts often issue an immediate turn or over‑correction within the first few steps. In the successful runs the agent’s corrective turns are spaced out and modest, creating a rhythm of “forward → small turn → forward” that keeps the trajectory centered and avoids contact with walls; the failing runs show rapid, large‑amplitude turns (or jittery alternating actions) that push the agent toward the boundaries almost instantly. The good trajectories also exhibit a clear pause or slower speed after each turn, allowing the new heading to settle before the next forward burst, while the poor trajectories continue turning aggressively without such settling periods, leading to oscillatory or chaotic motion. Finally, the high‑reward episodes maintain a safe margin from obstacles throughout, only adjusting direction when a corner is truly reached, whereas the low‑reward episodes collide with walls shortly after an early, mistimed turn, terminating the episode and incurring large negative rewards.

[WORST POLICY]
The high‑reward rollouts all contain at least one deliberate change of action—typically a sharp turn that moves the agent off a straight‑forward thrust and lets it hug a wall or negotiate a corner—whereas the low‑reward rollouts stay on the same “forward” action for the entire episode. This early corrective turn in the better runs prevents the agent from repeatedly overshooting its lane and colliding with boundaries, so it maintains a more stable orientation and avoids large penalty spikes. In the high‑reward cases the agent also shows brief periods of rapid acceleration that are quickly followed by a deceleration or a directional adjustment, while the poor runs exhibit a monotonic acceleration that only produces growing oscillations in the state value. By breaking the monotony of a single action and inserting timely turns or speed‑modulation, the better rollouts keep the trajectory bounded and reduce wall‑impact events, which directly translates into a noticeably higher cumulative reward.
