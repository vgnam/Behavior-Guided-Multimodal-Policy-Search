[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts consistently begin with a steady forward motion that is maintained for several steps before any turn is made, allowing the agent to build momentum and stay centered in corridors, whereas low‑reward episodes introduce sharp turns or bursts of acceleration almost immediately, causing frequent over‑corrections. In the successful runs the agent executes turns only when a wall or corner is clearly reached, timing the change of direction just enough to hug the boundary without colliding; the poorer runs often turn pre‑emptively or oscillate back‑and‑forth around the same point, wasting steps and incurring negative rewards. The high‑reward trajectories display brief, low‑amplitude adjustments after each turn—small “wiggles” that fine‑tune alignment—while the low‑reward sequences exhibit large, jittery swings that repeatedly push the state outside safe ranges. By preserving a smooth forward‑then‑turn pattern and limiting abrupt velocity changes, the policy avoids collisions and accumulates continuous positive reward, whereas the erratic timing and excessive oscillation in the low‑reward rollouts lead to frequent penalties and occasional crashes.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts repeatedly interleave a stabilising **action 0** with forward moves (action 2) and short corrective turns (action 1), keeping the state value in a modest range (≈1–2) and applying a corrective step just before a potential overshoot; low‑reward rollouts either omit action 0 altogether or use it only once, instead thrusting forward for many steps and allowing the state to spike above 3 before any turn is attempted. The successful episodes also time their turns early—typically within the first few dozen frames—so that the agent negotiates corners while the velocity is still low, whereas the poor episodes defer turning until after a long acceleration phase, producing jerky over‑corrections and loss of control near the end. In the high‑reward cases the agent’s motion is a rhythmic oscillation between forward thrust and brief re‑orientation, which continually resets momentum and prevents collisions; the low‑reward cases show prolonged straight runs followed by sudden, large‑angle turns that generate instability. This disciplined alternation of thrust, correction, and stabilisation yields steady positive rewards, while the unchecked acceleration and missing corrective actions in the bad rollouts lead to abrupt crashes and large negative penalties.

[WORST POLICY]
The high‑reward rollouts all contain a decisive turning maneuver shortly after start – the agent quickly aligns with a wall and executes a right‑hand turn that keeps it hugging the boundary, whereas the low‑reward rollouts persist with the same forward action for many steps and never perform such corrective turns. In the better rollouts the agent’s state values change sharply but only once or twice, indicating brief, purposeful corrections; the poorer rollouts show continuous oscillations of the state value, reflecting a lack of stabilising feedback and repeated over‑corrections. The higher‑reward episodes also tend to terminate early after the turn, limiting the number of timesteps over which negative reward accumulates, while the low‑reward episodes keep moving forward for the full horizon, accruing a larger total penalty. Finally, the successful rollouts keep the agent close to the outer wall after the turn, using the wall as a guide, whereas the low‑reward runs drift far from any wall, resulting in larger, later collisions and more severe penalties. These timing‑specific turn‑and‑wall‑follow behaviors therefore explain why the high‑reward trajectories achieve far less negative return.
