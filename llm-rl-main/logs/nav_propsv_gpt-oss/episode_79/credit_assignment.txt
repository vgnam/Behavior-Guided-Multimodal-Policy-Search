[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts all begin with a brief forward thrust followed quickly by a turn (often alternating left‑right) that steers the agent into the corridor rather than persisting on a long straight segment. After each turn they execute a short, stabilising forward stretch before making another corrective turn, keeping the state values within a modest band instead of allowing large spikes. This pattern of frequent, modest‑amplitude oscillations produces regular progress toward the goal while preventing the agent from running into walls. In contrast, the low‑reward rollouts either continue straight for too long, make an abrupt single correction that overshoots, or exhibit chaotic, large‑amplitude swings that quickly drive the state out of the safe range. The disciplined timing of short forward moves followed by timely, balanced turns therefore explains why the high‑reward trajectories accumulate far more positive reward.

[BEST POLICY]
The high‑reward rollouts all spend the majority of the episode executing long, uninterrupted sequences of action 0 (forward motion), only inserting brief, low‑amplitude corrections with actions 1 or 2 at well‑spaced intervals (e.g., around timesteps 200, 350, 650), whereas the low‑reward rollouts switch away from action 0 almost immediately and alternate rapidly between actions 1 and 2, producing sharp, frequent direction changes. In the successful runs the agent’s first few hundred steps are dominated by smooth forward progress, allowing it to accumulate the per‑step positive reward and stay safely inside the maze corridor; in the failing runs the early thrust (often action 2) propels the agent into walls or off‑track regions, generating large negative rewards and early termination. When corrective actions do appear in the high‑reward trajectories they are short, single‑step adjustments that realign the heading without overshooting, while in the low‑reward trajectories the same corrective actions are clustered together, causing oscillatory over‑correction and instability. This contrast in timing—sustained forward versus immediate, jittery switching—explains why the former builds up high cumulative reward and the latter incurs penalties and crashes.

[WORST POLICY]
In the higher‑reward rollouts the agent almost always begins with a brief, deliberate turn or slight left adjustment before applying forward thrust, while the low‑reward rollouts stay on pure forward‑thrust (action 2) from the start. This early correction lets the agent hug a wall and follow a smooth, low‑curvature segment for several steps, whereas the worst rollouts exhibit continuous large‑amplitude oscillations and a persistent rightward drift with no wall‑following. The higher‑reward trajectories also show a short period of steady, monotonic growth in the state value followed by an abrupt termination, indicating the agent only accelerates after it has aligned itself, while the low‑reward episodes keep accelerating and decelerating repeatedly throughout the episode. Finally, the better rollouts delay switching to the terminating action (e.g., action 0) until the very end, whereas the poor rollouts either never change action or toggle erratically, prolonging instability and leading to far larger negative rewards.
