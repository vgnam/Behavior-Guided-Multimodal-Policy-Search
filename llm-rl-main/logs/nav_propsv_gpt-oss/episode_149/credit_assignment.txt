[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts all begin with modest, steady forward steps followed by only small, frequent corrective turns, whereas the low‑reward examples show either an immediate large thrust or a sudden sharp turn that drives the agent into a wall. In the successful runs the agent interleaves brief bursts of motion with short stabilisation periods, keeping its speed moderate and allowing it to react to upcoming obstacles; the failing runs maintain a high velocity for many timesteps and never insert these damping intervals. The good trajectories also display a pattern of low‑amplitude oscillations—tiny left‑right adjustments that keep the agent centered—while the poor trajectories either lack any oscillatory correction or over‑oscillate with large, erratic swings that quickly diverge from the desired path. Finally, the high‑reward episodes postpone major direction changes until after the first few hundred frames, using the early phase to map the environment, whereas the low‑reward episodes make abrupt directional decisions early, leading to premature collisions and large negative rewards. These contrasting timing and magnitude of movement and correction explain why the controlled, gradually adjusted behavior yields far higher returns.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts keep a steady forward drive for several steps before issuing only modest, short‑duration turns, which preserves momentum and lets the agent stay on a smooth trajectory; in contrast, low‑reward rollouts insert abrupt, large‑angle direction changes or sudden jumps in state value early on, breaking that momentum and often sending the agent into a wall. The successful rollouts also postpone their biggest corrective actions until they are close to the goal, using fine‑grained adjustments that gradually shrink oscillations, whereas the failing rollouts make coarse, over‑correcting moves throughout the episode, causing persistent jitter and divergence from the target. Moreover, the high‑reward trajectories exhibit relatively uniform action magnitudes with no spikes, while the low‑reward ones contain occasional high‑magnitude action bursts that correspond to the large negative rewards at termination. This combination of continuous forward progress, delayed precise corrections, and avoidance of sudden large actions explains why the former achieve much higher cumulative rewards.

[WORST POLICY]
The high‑reward rollouts all contain at least one early deviation from the default “always‑thrust” action 2 – either an immediate action 0 (as in rollout 1) or a quick switch to action 0 after a short burst of forward thrust (rollout 2), or a brief corrective maneuver such as a sharp left turn before the episode ends (rollouts 4–5). By inserting this non‑2 action within the first few hundred steps the agent cuts the length of the episode, avoids prolonged oscillations and prevents a late‑stage collision, which limits the accumulated penalty and yields a less negative total reward. In contrast, the low‑reward rollouts keep applying action 2 continuously for almost the entire horizon, showing no corrective or terminating action until a catastrophic crash occurs near the end. This monotonic thrust produces larger, sustained oscillations in the state, leading the agent to wander far from any safe region and incur a much larger negative reward. Consequently, early action diversification and timely termination are the behavioural components that consistently separate the comparatively higher‑reward trajectories from the worst ones.
