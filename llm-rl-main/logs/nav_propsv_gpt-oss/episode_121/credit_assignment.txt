[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts consistently begin with a steady forward move (action 2) followed by a single, well‑timed sharp turn (action 0 or 1) and then maintain long stretches of forward motion before any subsequent correction, producing smooth, progressive state values; low‑reward rollouts instead switch actions almost immediately, often taking a turn right after the first step and generating abrupt state drops and large negative rewards. The successful trajectories also exhibit brief pauses or small corrective adjustments rather than rapid back‑and‑forth oscillations, which keeps the agent near the intended path and avoids the destabilizing over‑corrections seen in the failing runs. Moreover, high‑reward episodes delay major direction changes until the agent has built up momentum, whereas the poor episodes make early, aggressive turns that lead to collisions or failures. These timing and action‑sequencing differences plausibly explain why the former accrue steady positive reward while the latter incur sharp penalties and early terminations.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts all feature extended periods of forward‑motion (action 0) that carry the agent deep into the corridor before any turn is made, and when a turn occurs it is executed smoothly and only after the agent has approached a corner, allowing the state value to decrease gradually. In contrast, the low‑reward episodes contain abrupt, early turns (often action 2 or 1) or sudden accelerations that reverse direction or collide with walls, producing spikes in the state value and immediate negative rewards. The successful trajectories also show a clear pattern of alternating forward stretches with brief, well‑timed corrective turns, whereas the failures exhibit erratic, back‑and‑forth oscillations with no sustained forward progress. Moreover, high‑reward rollouts avoid over‑correction by limiting successive turn actions; low‑reward rollouts repeatedly flip between opposite turn actions, creating instability. This disciplined timing and the restraint from unnecessary or premature turning keep the agent on a productive path, directly translating into the large reward differences observed.

[WORST POLICY]
The higher‑reward rollouts all contain a brief initial “2” thrust followed quickly (within a few timesteps) by an opposite or neutral action such as “0”, which bends the trajectory away from walls and prevents a hard collision; the low‑reward rollouts never switch actions, staying on “2” for the entire episode and therefore overshooting and oscillating. In the better rollouts the agent makes a single, well‑timed correction right after entering a corner or narrow corridor, whereas the poor rollouts exhibit no corner‑specific adjustments and simply bounce back and forth. The successful trajectories also show short bursts of motion interleaved with moments of little or no thrust, producing smoother, more controlled paths, while the worst trajectories maintain a constant forward thrust that generates large amplitude oscillations. These timing and action‑switch patterns reduce contact with obstacles and keep the agent nearer the desired region, which explains why they achieve substantially higher (less negative) rewards.
