[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts consistently begin with an extended sequence of forward actions (action 0) that lasts for dozens of steps, establishing steady progress along the outer wall before any turn is taken, whereas low‑reward episodes either insert a turn almost immediately or intersperse forward moves with backward or turning actions, causing early deviation from the optimal path. When a turn is finally required, the successful rollouts execute a single, decisive turn (action 2) and then resume long uninterrupted forward thrusts, while the poorer rollouts exhibit rapid back‑and‑forth turning or oscillation (alternating actions 0/2 or 0/1) that repeatedly overshoots corners and wastes steps. The timing of corrective adjustments in the high‑reward cases is delayed until the agent reaches a clear geometric cue (e.g., the end of a wall segment), whereas low‑reward trajectories apply corrections prematurely, leading to repeated small negative rewards for each turn. Finally, the high‑reward behavior shows minimal use of the backward action (action 1), keeping the agent’s momentum forward; the low‑reward rollouts contain frequent backward moves that undo progress and increase exposure to collisions, directly reducing cumulative reward. These contrasting patterns of sustained forward motion, strategic single‑turn corrections, delayed adjustments, and avoidance of backward steps plausibly account for the large reward gap.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts consistently interleave short bursts of forward motion (action 2) with immediate corrective turns (actions 0 or 1) early in the episode, keeping the agent centered in corridors and preventing long runs into walls; low‑reward rollouts either sustain forward thrust for many steps or delay their first turn, allowing the agent to accelerate into obstacles or out‑of‑bounds regions. The successful trajectories also exhibit timely, small‑amplitude adjustments whenever the state values begin to drift, creating a controlled oscillatory pattern that continually re‑aligns the heading; the poorer runs show either no such micro‑corrections or overly large, erratic swings that grow into full loss of control. In the high‑reward cases the agent’s turn decisions are triggered shortly after the onset of a deviation, producing a rhythm of forward‑turn‑forward that maintains progress along the intended path, whereas the low‑reward cases either postpone turning until after a substantial deviation or execute a single sharp turn followed by unchecked forward motion, leading to collisions. These disciplined, early‑and‑frequent corrective actions keep the agent within high‑reward zones and avoid the catastrophic penalties that dominate the low‑reward episodes.

[WORST POLICY]
The high‑reward rollouts all contain at least one deliberate change in heading – a turn or re‑orientation that is triggered shortly after the episode begins, whereas the low‑reward rollouts never vary their action (action = 2) and therefore move straight ahead without any turning. In the better runs the agent briefly modulates thrust, accelerating then pausing to rotate before resuming forward motion; the worst runs apply a continuous forward thrust with no pauses, causing the position to swing back and forth around a single value. These early directional adjustments let the high‑reward agent explore the corridor and avoid immediate wall contact, while the constant‑thrust policy in the low‑reward cases repeatedly collides with or oscillates near obstacles, producing the −500 penalty. Because the occasional turn creates a more varied trajectory that can escape the narrow corridor, it yields a modestly higher (less negative) cumulative reward despite still ending in failure.
