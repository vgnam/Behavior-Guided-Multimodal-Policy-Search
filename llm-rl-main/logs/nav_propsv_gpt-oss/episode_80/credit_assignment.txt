[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts all follow a clear “forward‑then‑sharp‑turn‑forward” rhythm: the agent builds up sustained forward thrust, executes a decisive right‑or‑left turn at the maze corner, and immediately resumes forward motion, which lets it clear obstacles and accumulate reward. In contrast, low‑reward rollouts either turn prematurely or linger in an oscillatory correction phase, producing short, jittery forward bursts followed by erratic back‑and‑forth motions that quickly lead to wall contacts. The successful trajectories also keep oscillations brief and use them only as minor adjustments, whereas the failures exhibit prolonged, aggressive over‑corrections that destabilize the path. Timing-wise, the high‑reward episodes maintain longer continuous forward periods before each turn, while the low‑reward episodes insert turns or reversals too early, truncating progress. This disciplined forward‑turn‑forward pattern preserves momentum and reduces collisions, directly explaining the large reward gap.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts consistently interleave forward thrusts (action 0) with early corrective turns (actions 1 or 2) whenever a corner or wall is approached, keeping the agent glued to the boundary and preventing it from running straight into obstacles; low‑reward rollouts often sustain long, uninterrupted forward actions with few or no corrective turns, which leads to collisions once the agent reaches a wall. In the successful trajectories the agent makes short, frequent adjustments—visible as brief bursts of action 1 or 2—right after entering a new corridor, whereas the failed runs either delay turning until the last frame or never execute a turn at all. The timing of these adjustments is also distinct: high‑reward episodes exhibit a pattern of a turn within a few frames of detecting a change in geometry, while low‑reward episodes show turns only after a prolonged straight segment or not at all. By constantly “hugging” the walls through these timely corrections the agent preserves a smooth, bounded path and accumulates reward, whereas the lack of such wall‑following behavior in the low‑reward rollouts causes premature termination and large negative rewards.

[WORST POLICY]
The higher‑reward rollouts all contain at least one non‑forward action (typically an early “0” or “1”) that redirects the agent after an initial thrust, whereas the worst‑reward episodes stay on action 2 for the entire horizon. This brief change of direction lets the agent reduce its distance to the goal or avoid an imminent wall, creating a modest improvement in the state value before the episode ends. In contrast, the constantly repeated forward action in the low‑reward runs produces large, uncontrolled swings in the state value and repeated collisions, keeping the agent far from any target. Moreover, the better rollouts execute the corrective action within the first two frames, establishing a smoother trajectory, while the poor rollouts never alter their control signal, leading to sustained oscillatory motion. The presence of an early, purposeful turn therefore correlates with the less negative returns by preventing the extreme oscillations that dominate the ‑500 scores.
