[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts keep action 0 (forward) dominant, stringing together long uninterrupted forward runs and inserting only isolated single‑step turns that are immediately followed by forward motion, which lets the agent progress steadily along the corridor. In contrast, low‑reward rollouts contain clusters of consecutive turn actions (1 or 2) and frequent alternations between forward and turn, producing oscillatory trajectories that waste steps and drift away from the optimal path. The successful episodes also time their turns so that each rotation is quickly compensated by a forward step, preventing the agent from lingering in a rotated orientation, whereas the poor episodes exhibit delayed or missing compensations, leading to over‑corrections and repeated re‑orientation. Additionally, the low‑reward trajectories occasionally employ backward‑movement actions (action 2) that directly reverse progress and incur negative rewards, a behavior that is absent from the high‑reward sequences. These differences in action dominance, spacing of turns, and avoidance of reverse motions plausibly explain why the former achieve much higher cumulative rewards.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts repeatedly sustain long sequences of the forward action (0) before inserting a single, smooth turn (1 or 2), whereas low‑reward episodes intersperse turns almost every step and often follow a turn immediately with the opposite turn, creating rapid oscillations. The successful trajectories also avoid any backward‑motion pattern—no instance of the agent reversing direction is observed—while the poorest runs include a clear backward drift or a sudden reversal that leads to wall collisions. When a turn is required, the high‑reward policy executes it gradually, keeping the state values within a narrow band and then promptly returns to forward motion; the low‑reward rollouts over‑correct, producing sharp, successive left‑right swings that push the state out of range and precipitate premature termination. Finally, timing-wise, the best rollouts delay corrective actions until after several stable forward steps, giving the agent time to make progress, whereas the worst rollouts react immediately to minor deviations, resulting in fragmented, unstable paths that incur large penalties. These consistent differences—extended forward runs, single smooth turns, absence of reverse motion, and delayed, moderate corrections—explain why the high‑reward trajectories accumulate positive reward while the erratic, over‑turning low‑reward ones quickly incur large negatives.

[WORST POLICY]
The higher‑reward rollouts all contain an early, purposeful change of direction—usually a single “action 0” (turn) inserted within the first few dozen steps—that steers the agent away from the wall it is initially hugging, whereas the low‑reward rollouts keep using action 2 (forward thrust) for long stretches and only turn once, if at all, just before termination. In the better rollouts the turn is timed just before the agent reaches a corner, producing a brief, sharp re‑orientation that lets the agent stay close to the boundary without colliding; the poorer rollouts exhibit either no turn or a turn that occurs after the agent has already overshot the safe corridor, leading to an immediate crash. Moreover, the high‑reward episodes intersperse short bursts of acceleration with immediate corrective actions, creating a “fast‑then‑adjust” rhythm that prevents the state value from drifting far from the safe region, while the low‑reward episodes show monotonic acceleration followed by a sudden stop, causing large spikes in the state value and a terminal collision. These timing and action‑selection differences keep the agent within navigable space longer, yielding the modestly higher (less negative) returns.
