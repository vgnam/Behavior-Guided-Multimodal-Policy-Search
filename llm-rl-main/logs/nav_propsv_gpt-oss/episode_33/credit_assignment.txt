[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts contain long uninterrupted sequences of action 0 (forward) that are punctuated only by a single, well‑timed turn (action 1 or 2) after several forward steps, whereas low‑reward rollouts constantly interleave short forward bursts with frequent turns, creating a jerky, oscillatory pattern. The successful rollouts also exploit the known positive reward of action 0 (+5) and deliberately minimise the use of actions 1 and 2, which repeatedly incur small penalties (‑0.5); the poorer rollouts waste time by turning excessively and thus accrue those penalties. In the high‑reward trajectories the agent commits early to a corridor—turning twice at the start and then maintaining forward motion for many frames—while the low‑reward episodes show delayed or premature turning that leads to back‑tracking and missed shortcuts. Moreover, the high‑reward rollouts exhibit smoother velocity profiles, with fewer rapid accelerations followed by decelerations, indicating stable control, whereas the low‑reward rollouts display abrupt thrusts and stops that waste steps and increase collision risk. These timing and action‑selection differences directly translate into higher cumulative rewards by maximizing forward progress and minimizing corrective, penalised moves.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts consistently begin with a modest forward push followed quickly by a corrective turn (often an early action 0) that steers the agent away from nearby walls, whereas low‑reward episodes start with an aggressive, sustained forward thrust (repeated action 2) that leaves no time for adjustment and drives the agent into obstacles. Throughout the successful runs the agent alternates forward motions with short, purposeful backward or lateral tweaks, creating a rhythmic “oscillatory” pattern that keeps its trajectory centered in the corridor; the failing runs lack this alternation and instead maintain a single direction until a sudden, uncontrolled crash. The timing of the corrective actions in the high‑reward cases is regular – a few steps after each forward segment – which prevents overshoot, while the low‑reward cases either omit corrections entirely or apply a single late turn that is too late to avoid collision. By continuously using sensor‑driven adjustments rather than relying on a single large thrust, the high‑reward policy maintains safe distances from walls and reaches the goal, directly translating into the large positive cumulative reward.

[WORST POLICY]
The higher‑reward rollouts all contain at least one moment where the agent switches away from the default “action 2” thrust and applies a different control input to re‑orient or brake, creating a brief corrective maneuver that nudges the trajectory back toward the corridor walls; the lowest‑reward episodes never deviate from action 2, so they lack any purposeful steering or speed‑modulation. In the better runs the agent’s first few steps are directed forward along the intended path, then a timed over‑correction (often a rightward or leftward turn) is introduced just before a corner, preventing a hard collision, whereas the poor runs show no such anticipatory turn and instead wander erratically until the episode ends. The successful trajectories also exhibit a pattern of short, smooth forward pushes followed by a pause or reduced thrust when approaching a wall, giving the system time to settle, while the bad trajectories maintain a continuous, jerky thrust that drives the state value up and down wildly. Finally, the high‑reward rollouts display occasional wall‑following behavior—using the environment’s geometry as a guide—whereas the low‑reward rollouts ignore spatial cues entirely, resulting in chaotic, uncontrolled motion that quickly depletes reward.
