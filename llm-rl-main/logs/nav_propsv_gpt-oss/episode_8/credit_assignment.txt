[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts consistently intersperse forward‑motion actions (action 1) with short, decisive turns of type 0 that immediately yield the 5‑point reward, creating a rhythmic “forward‑then‑reward” cadence; low‑reward episodes either omit these rewarding action‑0 bursts or delay them until after a series of erratic turns, which leads to missed opportunities and eventual collisions. In the successful runs the agent executes turns only when a clear positional error is detected and then promptly returns to forward motion, producing brief, well‑timed corrections, whereas the poor runs show prolonged sequences of rapid, alternating turns (actions 0 and 2) that over‑correct and destabilize the trajectory. The high‑reward behavior also features early‑stage stabilization—quickly correcting any deviation within the first few frames—so the agent remains centered in the corridor, while low‑reward trajectories wander off‑path early, gaining momentum that later results in uncontrolled acceleration and wall impacts. Finally, the top rollouts maintain a modest speed profile, using occasional sideways actions (action 2) only as fine‑tuning adjustments, whereas the low‑reward rollouts rely heavily on aggressive sideways or forward pushes without subsequent corrective actions, causing overshoot and loss of control that directly depresses the total reward.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts all begin with a series of modest forward actions followed by small, well‑timed turn actions that line the agent up with the next corridor, producing a smooth wall‑following trajectory; in contrast, the low‑reward rollouts contain one or two large, aggressive actions that suddenly shift the state value far forward or sideways, causing the agent to slam into walls. The successful episodes also display brief, corrective oscillations only after reaching a corner, allowing the agent to fine‑tune its orientation before proceeding, whereas the failures show abrupt state jumps with no intermediate adjustments and terminate immediately after the jump. Moreover, the high‑reward trajectories keep the agent’s position within a narrow band throughout the episode, reflecting consistent speed and direction control, while the low‑reward runs exhibit spikes in the state magnitude that correspond to rapid accelerations and loss of control. These patterns suggest that gradual, incremental motion and timely, modest turns enable sustained progress and reward accumulation, whereas sudden, oversized movements lead to collisions and large negative penalties.

[WORST POLICY]
The high‑reward rollouts all contain a brief, purposeful forward thrust followed quickly by a corrective maneuver—usually a sharp right turn or a sudden change in direction—that redirects the agent away from the wall, whereas the low‑reward rollouts persist with the same forward‑movement action for many steps and never execute such a correction. In the better runs the corrective action occurs early (within the first few frames), limiting the distance traveled before the episode ends, while the poorer runs keep applying the forward action far longer, allowing large, erratic state swings to accumulate. Moreover, the successful episodes show occasional pauses or reduced activity after the turn, giving the agent time to settle, whereas the worst rollouts display continuous, unchecked acceleration that produces oscillations and overshoot. These patterns plausibly improve reward because early redirection prevents collisions and excessive drift, and brief stabilization periods curb the buildup of unstable dynamics that otherwise drive the agent into highly negative outcomes.
