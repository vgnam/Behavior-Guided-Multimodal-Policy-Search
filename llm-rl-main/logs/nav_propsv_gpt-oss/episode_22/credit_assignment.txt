[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts consistently begin with a decisive forward action (action 0) followed almost immediately by a brief, well‑timed corrective turn (actions 1 or 2), allowing the agent to stay close to walls and quickly re‑align after each movement; in low‑reward episodes this early correction is either omitted (as in the uncontrolled thrust of rollout 1) or delayed until after a large displacement, causing collisions or runaway trajectories. Throughout the successful runs the agent alternates forward and turn actions at a moderate cadence—roughly one correction every 2–3 steps—so that oscillations remain small and the path stays inside the maze, whereas the poorer rollouts exhibit either overly rapid flip‑flopping (excessive oscillation) or prolonged straight segments that let errors accumulate and lead to wall impacts. The high‑reward trajectories also make systematic use of wall‑following early on, hugging the outer boundary before spiralling inward, which provides a reliable geometric cue for staying within safe regions; low‑reward runs frequently ignore this cue, venturing into open space and hitting obstacles. Finally, when the agent reaches tighter corners, the high‑reward rollouts execute a short, sharp turn followed by an immediate forward step, effectively “snapping” into the next corridor, while the low‑reward examples either over‑turn or fail to turn promptly, resulting in overshoot and penalty. These timing and action‑selection differences keep the agent’s state values bounded and prevent catastrophic terminations, directly explaining the large reward gap.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts consistently interleave a primary forward action (0) with frequent short corrective actions (1 or 2), creating a rhythmic “forward‑adjust‑forward” pattern that keeps the agent near the centre of the corridor and prevents it from drifting into walls; low‑reward episodes lack these adjustments, instead sustaining a single forward command for many steps or applying an immediate large thrust, which quickly drives the agent into a boundary or off the track. In the successful runs the agent applies modest thrust early on and then uses periodic small‑angle turns (action 2) at regular intervals (≈ every 30–50 steps), allowing it to navigate bends smoothly, whereas the failures either never turn or execute a sudden, aggressive turn only once right before termination. The high‑reward trajectories also exhibit early‑stage oscillations—brief backward or lateral moves (action 1) that correct overshoot—so the cumulative state values stay within a stable range, while the low‑reward cases show a monotonic decrease in state value with no mid‑episode correction, leading to uncontrolled descent or collision. Finally, successful rollouts keep the magnitude of each reward change modest and balanced, reflecting controlled progress, whereas the poor rollouts contain a few very large positive spikes followed by a single catastrophic negative spike, indicating that unchecked acceleration followed by a lack of corrective behavior drives the large reward loss. These contrasting timing and action‑selection patterns explain why the oscillatory, corrective style yields high cumulative reward and the single‑action, uncorrected style results in failure.

[WORST POLICY]
The higher‑reward rollouts all begin with a single, decisive maneuver—typically a sharp right turn that aligns the agent with a wall—after which they stick to one steady forward action and keep a roughly constant orientation, producing a smooth, monotonic trajectory with only minor adjustments. In contrast, the low‑reward rollouts never settle after an initial move; they persist in taking the same aggressive forward action (action 2) while their state value spikes dramatically, causing repeated overshoots, backward drifts, and corrective oscillations that dominate the episode. This steady‑after‑turn pattern limits abrupt velocity changes, keeping the agent away from collisions and reducing the magnitude of negative rewards, whereas the relentless thrust‑and‑oscillate pattern in the bad rollouts creates instability and large penalties. Consequently, the presence of an early aligning turn followed by consistent, low‑variance forward motion is a key behavioral component that distinguishes the comparatively better outcomes from the chaotic, high‑frequency corrections that drive the worst scores.
