[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts consistently begin with a prolonged sequence of forward actions (action 0) that keeps the agent advancing toward the goal before any turns are introduced, whereas low‑reward rollouts often insert a turn (action 1 or 2) within the first few steps, breaking forward momentum and leading to early collisions. When corrective turns do occur in the successful runs, they are timed after a clear decline in state value—typically around the middle of the episode—and are followed by another sustained stretch of forward moves, creating a pattern of “advance‑then‑adjust‑then‑advance”; the failing rollouts either never recover after a turn or make multiple rapid, alternating turns without a stabilising forward phase. The high‑reward trajectories exhibit smooth, monotonic increases in cumulative reward during these forward phases, while the low‑reward trajectories show frequent reward spikes and drops that correspond to oscillatory actions (quick alternation between actions 0, 1, 2). Additionally, successful episodes tend to limit unnecessary lateral motion, keeping lateral actions (1 or 2) to brief, purposeful bursts, whereas the poor episodes display erratic side‑to‑side jitter throughout, preventing the agent from maintaining a coherent path. This combination of early committed forward motion, well‑timed single corrections, and minimal oscillatory turning plausibly explains why the high‑reward rollouts accumulate far greater returns than the low‑reward ones.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts all begin with a short burst of steady forward actions (action 0) that quickly establishes a safe trajectory, after which the agent interleaves brief corrective moves (alternating actions 1 and 2) to keep its state within narrow bounds; low‑reward rollouts either skip these early forward bursts or replace them with a single large thrust that drives the state far from the safe corridor. The successful episodes also exhibit regular, short‑duration correction cycles—typically a few steps of action 1 followed by a few steps of action 2—whereas the poor episodes show either no corrections or a single prolonged corrective phase that comes too late, allowing the agent to drift into danger. Timing is another discriminant: high‑reward runs apply corrections at every change of direction (every 2–4 frames), keeping the motion smooth, while low‑reward runs let the state accelerate for many consecutive frames before any adjustment, producing jerky, unstable trajectories. Finally, the high‑reward behavior maintains moderate, bounded state values throughout the episode, preventing the large negative rewards that occur in low‑reward rollouts when the state spikes abruptly; the absence of these bounded, regularly corrected motions in the failures explains their much lower cumulative reward.

[WORST POLICY]
The high‑reward rollouts all contain at least one early deviation from the default “action 2” forward thrust – they switch to a turning or braking action within the first few frames, producing a brief corrective maneuver that redirects the agent away from a wall or sharp corner. In contrast, every low‑reward rollout applies action 2 continuously for the entire episode, never introducing a lateral or slowing command, which leaves the agent oscillating in place and repeatedly hitting obstacles. This early, purposeful change in action creates a smoother trajectory and prevents the large‑amplitude state swings seen when the policy persists with forward thrust alone. Because the high‑reward trajectories include that timely adjustment, they avoid the catastrophic collisions and prolonged instability that drive the low‑reward rolls to the worst possible score.
