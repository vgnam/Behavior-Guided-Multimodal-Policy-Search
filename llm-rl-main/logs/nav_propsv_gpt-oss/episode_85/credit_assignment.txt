[CURRENT POLICY]
In the successful trajectories the agent keeps a forward‑biased action (typically 0 or 2) and, as soon as a negative reward is observed, immediately follows it with a corrective turn (action 1 or 2) within one or two steps, which prevents it from hitting a wall; the failing runs either ignore the negative signal and continue straight or abruptly switch to a stop action, leading to collisions. High‑reward rollouts also exhibit regular short‑duration oscillations—brief forward moves punctuated by quick opposite turns—that keep the agent centred in the corridor, whereas low‑reward episodes lack this micro‑adjustment and drift into obstacles. The timing of these corrections is early, occurring within the first few timesteps after a reward dip, so the agent can re‑align before the error compounds, while the poor rollouts delay or omit the turn until termination. Finally, successful episodes intersperse occasional brief backward or deceleration steps that act as safety buffers, a pattern missing from the low‑reward runs that maintain monotonic forward motion until impact.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts keep long uninterrupted stretches of the forward‑motion action (typically 0 or 1) before any turn, building steady positive reward while the state stays near the centre of the maze; low‑reward rollouts intersperse turns much earlier, producing rapid oscillations that push the state toward the walls and trigger negative rewards. When corrective turns do occur in the successful runs they are spaced out and followed by a brief period of straight motion, allowing the agent to re‑align without overshooting, whereas the poor runs execute back‑to‑back turns or abrupt direction changes that immediately destabilise the trajectory. Successful episodes also delay any large‑amplitude state changes until the very end of the episode, using smooth, low‑frequency adjustments, while the failing episodes show sudden spikes in state value (large jumps) just before the crash, indicating loss of control. Finally, the high‑reward trajectories exhibit a pattern of “steady forward → minor correction → steady forward” that preserves momentum, whereas the low‑reward ones show “forward → sharp turn → immediate reversal,” a pattern that repeatedly erodes accumulated reward and leads to termination.

[WORST POLICY]
The high‑reward rollouts all contain an early, deliberate change of action – after an initial forward thrust (action 2) the agent quickly issues a corrective turn (action 0 or 1) within the first few timesteps, which steers it away from the immediate wall and limits the duration of uncontrolled motion; by contrast, the low‑reward rollouts keep the same forward‑thrust action for the majority of the episode, producing long‑lasting oscillations and eventual collisions. In the better runs the corrective turn is followed by a short burst of straight, steady movement before any failure, whereas the worst runs show no such stabilising segment and instead drift continuously, causing larger cumulative penalties. The timing of the turn is also critical: high‑reward episodes execute the correction almost immediately (t ≈ 1–3), preventing the agent from building up large state errors, while low‑reward episodes delay or omit the turn entirely, allowing state values to explode and the trajectory to become chaotic. This early, purposeful reorientation reduces wall impacts and keeps the accumulated negative reward lower, explaining why those patterns consistently appear in the higher‑scoring rollouts and are missing from the lower‑scoring ones.
