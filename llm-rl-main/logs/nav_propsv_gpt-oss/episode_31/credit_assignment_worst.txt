High‑reward rollouts all contain an early, decisive change of control – the agent starts with a single corrective action (often “2”) and within a few timesteps switches to a different command (typically “0” or a turn) that ends the episode before the state can drift far from the goal; low‑reward rollouts, by contrast, persist with the same action for the whole horizon, producing long, uncontrolled oscillations. The successful runs also exhibit a brief, monotonic movement toward the target (a smooth decrease in the state value) before stopping, whereas the poor runs show repeated large jumps up and down in the state, indicating over‑correction and lack of stabilization. In the high‑reward cases the agent reacts quickly to the initial wall proximity, applying an immediate corrective push that redirects it inward, while the low‑reward cases wander without such early correction and repeatedly hit the boundaries. Finally, the better rollouts terminate shortly after the corrective action, limiting exposure to cumulative penalties; the worse rollouts continue indefinitely, accumulating large negative rewards as the oscillatory pattern persists. This combination of early corrective switching, short monotonic progress, and early termination plausibly explains why the high‑reward trajectories lose far less reward than the prolonged, oscillatory low‑reward ones.
