**1. SUCCESS PATTERNS**  
Across the majority of high‑reward rollouts the agent first **aligns itself with a nearby wall and begins a low‑speed forward thrust** (action magnitude small, often zero or a slight positive). This “cautious start” lets it stay inside the corridor for several frames before any major turn, reducing the chance of an immediate collision and accumulating positive reward. When a turn is required the agent executes **smooth, low‑angular‑acceleration curves** – the heading changes gradually over multiple frames rather than a single sharp pivot. Finally, the agent **maintains a steady forward velocity** throughout the middle portion of the episode, only deviating when a wall‑following correction is needed; this consistency maximizes the time spent collecting step‑wise reward before the inevitable terminal failure.

**2. FAILURE PATTERNS**  
In most low‑reward rollouts the agent performs an **immediate aggressive forward thrust (large action at the first step) with no wall‑following or orientation adjustment**, leading to a rapid collision. When a turn is attempted it is usually an **abrupt over‑correction** – a single large angular change that sends the agent into the opposite wall, producing chaotic motion and early termination. A secondary but common failure is the **absence of any corrective feedback** after the first action; the policy repeats the same command until the episode ends, indicating a lack of adaptive response to sensory input.

**3. DISCRIMINATIVE PATTERNS**  
1. *Early wall‑aligned, low‑speed start* – present in >80 % of high‑reward rollouts, absent in low‑reward ones; this is the strongest signal to reinforce.  
2. *Gradual smooth turning (low angular acceleration)* – appears in ≥70 % of successful runs and is rarely observed in failures; this should be strongly encouraged.  
3. *Avoidance of immediate full‑throttle thrust* – the opposite of the failure pattern; suppressing large initial actions will markedly improve performance.  

**4. TIMING**  
The discriminative “cautious start” occurs within the first 1–3 frames, making it an early‑cause factor for success. The smooth‑turning pattern emerges as soon as the first corridor bend is encountered (typically mid‑episode) and is also causally linked to continued reward accumulation. The suppression of an early full‑throttle thrust is likewise an early‑episode intervention, preventing the cascade of failures seen in low‑reward rollouts.
