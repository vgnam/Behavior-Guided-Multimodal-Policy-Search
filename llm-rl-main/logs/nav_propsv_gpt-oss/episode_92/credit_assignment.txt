[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts are dominated by long stretches of the forward action (0), with only brief, purposeful turns (actions 1 or 2) that occur after the agent has already built up forward momentum; this sustained forward drive keeps the agent moving toward rewarding goals and avoids premature wall contacts. In contrast, low‑reward rollouts introduce turning or backward actions almost immediately, producing early oscillations and frequent direction reversals that send the agent into corners or walls. The successful runs also show a delayed and sparse use of corrective actions, allowing the state values to remain relatively stable, whereas the poor runs exhibit rapid, repeated swings in actions that cause large, erratic state fluctuations and collisions. Consequently, the pattern of maintaining forward thrust with minimal, well‑timed corrections appears to be the key behavioral difference driving the large reward gap.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts repeatedly begin with a short burst of forward actions (action 0) that quickly establishes momentum, then interleave occasional single corrective turns (actions 1 or 2) only after several forward steps; the low‑reward rollouts either delay the initial forward burst or insert a turn almost immediately, breaking the early momentum and often leading to premature collisions. In the successful episodes the corrective turns are sparse and timed later—typically after the agent has travelled a distance of 1–2 units—so they serve to fine‑tune the trajectory rather than constantly oscillate, whereas the failing runs exhibit frequent back‑and‑forth adjustments, creating a jittery, oscillatory pattern that wastes steps and drives the state into hazardous zones. Moreover, the high‑reward trajectories maintain long uninterrupted stretches of action 0 near the end of the episode, allowing steady progress toward the goal, while the low‑reward cases introduce late‑stage turns or reverse movements that destabilize the final approach and trigger termination. This contrast suggests that early sustained forward motion, judiciously spaced corrections, and a final push without unnecessary turning are the key behavioral components that separate the high‑reward from the low‑reward rollouts.

[WORST POLICY]
The high‑reward rollouts all contain an early transition from the forward‑thrust action 2 to the stop action 0, usually within the first few frames, which truncates forward motion before large position excursions develop; the low‑reward rollouts keep action 2 for many steps, allowing the agent’s state to drift far from its start and then only switch to 0 very late, when the episode terminates abruptly. In the better rollouts the agent’s trajectory shows only brief, modest forward moves followed by a pause or very small adjustments, whereas the poorer rollouts exhibit prolonged, high‑amplitude oscillations—repeated forward‑backward swings that never settle. This early stopping prevents the agent from accumulating the large negative position values that dominate the low‑reward scores, so the cumulative penalty is smaller. Moreover, the high‑reward episodes avoid sustained over‑corrections; when they do correct, the adjustments are minimal and occur shortly after the initial thrust, while the low‑reward episodes repeatedly over‑correct, creating abrupt direction changes that amplify instability. Consequently, the combination of an early action 0 switch and limited, low‑amplitude oscillations consistently yields the comparatively higher (less negative) returns.
