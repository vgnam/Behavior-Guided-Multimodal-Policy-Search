[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts all contain relatively long uninterrupted runs of the forward action (action 0) punctuated by a single, well‑timed right‑turn (action 2) that lets the agent hug the outer wall of the maze, whereas low‑reward episodes are dominated by rapid alternation between forward, left‑turn (action 1) or backward‑type actions, producing short forward bursts and constant back‑and‑forth adjustments. In the successful trajectories the agent delays any corrective turning until it has accrued enough forward progress to justify a change of direction, while the poor trajectories over‑correct almost immediately after each forward step, creating high‑frequency oscillations. The high‑reward behavior also shows almost no backward or left‑turn actions after the initial wall‑following phase, keeping the trajectory monotonic toward the goal; the low‑reward runs frequently include left or reverse moves that pull the agent away from the optimal path. By maintaining forward momentum and limiting unnecessary corrective actions, the agent accrues steady positive rewards, whereas the excessive, mistimed corrections in the low‑reward rollouts waste steps and often lead to collisions or dead‑ends, sharply reducing total reward.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts consistently begin with short bursts of forward action (0) followed almost immediately by a corrective turn (1 or 2), keeping the agent close to walls and preventing it from drifting into open space; low‑reward runs either thrust forward for many steps without any early turn or keep a single forward action for too long before a sudden, large‑magnitude turn that ends the episode. The successful trajectories also alternate forward and turn actions at regular short intervals, producing a rhythmic “step‑turn‑step” pattern, whereas the poor trajectories show long monotonic forward stretches or a single late‑stage turn that over‑corrects and causes a crash. In the high‑reward cases the agent makes its first turn within the first few steps, using it to align with the corridor and then repeats modest adjustments, which continuously yields positive per‑step rewards; the low‑reward cases delay the first turn or skip it altogether, allowing the agent to accumulate negative positional rewards before a catastrophic failure. Finally, the high‑reward rollouts avoid any prolonged period of inaction or excessive acceleration, while the low‑reward rollouts exhibit either aggressive acceleration away from the start or a final abrupt acceleration that leads to loss of control, explaining the large reward gap.

[WORST POLICY]
The high‑reward rollouts all contain an early, decisive change of action (a sharp turn or thrust in a different direction) within the first few frames, whereas the low‑reward rollouts stick with the same forward action 2 for most of the episode. This early maneuver in the better runs quickly redirects the agent away from the wall‑hugging corridor and leads to an abrupt termination, limiting the number of timesteps over which penalties accrue. In contrast, the poorer runs maintain a persistent forward thrust, producing long, oscillatory trajectories that keep the agent moving (and penalised) for many more steps. The brief, varied action sequences in the high‑reward cases therefore reduce accumulated negative reward, while the monotonic, prolonged forward behavior in the low‑reward cases inflates the total penalty.
