[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts consistently contain extended runs of forward‑moving actions (the dominant “action 0/2” bursts) that carry the agent several cells before any turn is taken, whereas the low‑reward episodes break forward motion almost immediately with sharp left or right turns and very short forward segments. In the successful trajectories the agent first stabilises near the centre of the corridor – often pausing or making a small corrective turn after 2–3 steps – and then resumes a longer forward sweep, while the failing runs oscillate back‑and‑forth from the outset without a clear stabilisation phase. The timing of corrective turns in the high‑reward cases is delayed until the agent has built momentum, producing smoother, more purposeful progress; the low‑reward cases exhibit premature, frequent over‑corrections that waste steps and bounce the agent off walls. Consequently, the high‑reward behavior advances deeper into the maze per unit time, accruing more positive rewards, whereas the jittery, early‑turn pattern of the low‑reward rollouts traps the agent in local loops and reduces total reward.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts consistently keep a forward‑oriented thrust for long stretches (e.g., many timesteps of action 0) before making only brief, purposeful corrections, whereas low‑reward episodes either intersperse frequent direction changes or terminate after a single corrective action. The successful runs also exhibit smooth, low‑amplitude oscillations—small sideways or backward nudges that are timed after the forward surge—to stay centered on the corridor, while the failing runs show large, abrupt swings (sharp left/right or backward moves) that immediately push the agent toward walls. When a turn is needed, the best trajectories execute a single, well‑timed cornering action (often a right turn) and then resume forward motion, whereas the poor trajectories either overshoot the corner with multiple rapid turns or linger indecisively, causing collisions. Finally, the high‑reward behavior maintains stable sensor feedback, indicating the agent stays within the navigable lane, while low‑reward episodes display sensor spikes that coincide with erratic movements and early episode termination. These differences suggest that sustained forward thrust with minimal, precisely timed corrections keeps the agent on the optimal path, whereas excessive or mistimed oscillations lead to loss of control and lower rewards.

[WORST POLICY]
The high‑reward rollouts all break the monotone “forward‑thrust” pattern that dominates the low‑reward runs; instead of applying action 2 for hundreds of steps, they insert an early turn or a sudden change of action (often a brief action 0) that redirects the agent away from the wall within the first few timesteps. This early redirection produces a short, controlled trajectory—sometimes a smooth curve into a narrow corridor—that ends the episode quickly, limiting the accumulated penalty. In contrast, the low‑reward rollouts persist with action 2 for long periods (often > 800 steps), generating large oscillations in position and only crashing near the very end, which yields a far larger negative sum. The timing difference—early, purposeful deviation versus prolonged, unchecked forward motion—means the high‑reward episodes incur fewer per‑step penalties and avoid the late‑stage crash that drags down the low‑reward scores. Consequently, the presence of an early, varied action and a brief, directed maneuver is the key behavioral component that consistently raises the reward in the better worst‑policy rollouts.
