[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts consistently begin with a solid stretch of forward motion (action 0) and then execute a sharp right‑turn (action 2) almost immediately when a corner is encountered, keeping the agent tightly on the intended corridor; low‑reward rollouts either linger in forward motion or wander before turning, often inserting extra left‑turns or stops that waste steps. The successful trajectories show only brief, purposeful oscillations—typically a single corrective adjustment lasting one or two frames—whereas the poorer runs contain prolonged back‑and‑forth swings (repeated action 1 or alternating 0/2) that drift the agent toward walls. Timing of the turn is another distinguishing factor: in high‑reward episodes the turn occurs within the first few frames of the corner, while in low‑reward episodes the same decision is delayed, allowing the state value to degrade and sometimes leading to collisions. These patterns give the high‑reward agent steady forward progress and efficient navigation, whereas the erratic, delayed, and overly corrective behaviors of the low‑reward agent squander time and increase the chance of hitting boundaries, directly lowering cumulative reward.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts consistently maintain a baseline of forward‑movement actions (e.g., repeated “action 0”) punctuated by brief, modest turn adjustments (actions 1 or 2) that keep the trajectory aligned with the goal, whereas low‑reward episodes either omit these corrective turns or replace them with a single, aggressive thrust that drives the agent away from the target. In the successful runs the agent stabilizes its path early—typically within the first few dozen steps—so that subsequent motion remains smooth and incremental, while the failing runs exhibit late‑stage over‑corrections, jittery oscillations, or abrupt accelerations that cause divergence and collisions. The high‑reward behavior also features a measured pacing: the agent avoids long periods of stagnation or rapid speed spikes, allowing it to react to subtle state changes, whereas low‑reward trajectories contain extended idle phases followed by sudden bursts of motion that overshoot safe zones. Finally, the effective rollouts show a pattern of using small corrective turns precisely when the state begins to drift upward, preventing the state from reaching high‑penalty regions; the poor rollouts either ignore this cue or respond with large, destabilizing actions, leading to large negative rewards. These contrasting action‑timing strategies explain why the former accumulate steady positive reward and the latter terminate abruptly with large penalties.

[WORST POLICY]
The high‑reward rollouts all contain an early, decisive deviation – either a sharp turn or a non‑forward action (often “0”) within the first few frames – that forces the episode to terminate quickly, capping the number of per‑step penalties and thus keeping the total reward relatively less negative. By contrast, the low‑reward rollouts persist with the forward‑thrust action “2” for most of the episode, producing long, oscillatory trajectories that repeatedly hit the walls or drift without stopping, which accumulates many negative step rewards before the inevitable termination. The abrupt direction changes seen in the better rollouts (e.g., rapid right turns) break the oscillatory pattern and prevent the agent from entering the sustained wobble that characterises the poorer runs. Consequently, the early termination and occasional early “stop” action in the high‑reward cases limit exposure to penalty, while the prolonged, monotonic forward motion with continuous oscillations in the low‑reward cases drives the large negative returns.
