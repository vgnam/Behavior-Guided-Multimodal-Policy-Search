High‑reward rollouts consistently maintain a baseline of forward‑movement actions (e.g., repeated “action 0”) punctuated by brief, modest turn adjustments (actions 1 or 2) that keep the trajectory aligned with the goal, whereas low‑reward episodes either omit these corrective turns or replace them with a single, aggressive thrust that drives the agent away from the target. In the successful runs the agent stabilizes its path early—typically within the first few dozen steps—so that subsequent motion remains smooth and incremental, while the failing runs exhibit late‑stage over‑corrections, jittery oscillations, or abrupt accelerations that cause divergence and collisions. The high‑reward behavior also features a measured pacing: the agent avoids long periods of stagnation or rapid speed spikes, allowing it to react to subtle state changes, whereas low‑reward trajectories contain extended idle phases followed by sudden bursts of motion that overshoot safe zones. Finally, the effective rollouts show a pattern of using small corrective turns precisely when the state begins to drift upward, preventing the state from reaching high‑penalty regions; the poor rollouts either ignore this cue or respond with large, destabilizing actions, leading to large negative rewards. These contrasting action‑timing strategies explain why the former accumulate steady positive reward and the latter terminate abruptly with large penalties.
