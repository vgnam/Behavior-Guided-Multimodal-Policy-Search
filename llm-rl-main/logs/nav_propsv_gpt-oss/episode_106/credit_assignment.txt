[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts keep a steady, low‑magnitude action (often zero or a small corrective value) for long stretches, producing smooth, incremental state changes that let the agent accumulate positive rewards without triggering the large penalty associated with sudden jumps. In contrast, low‑reward episodes contain abrupt, high‑magnitude actions that instantly propel the state far from its current value, leading to rapid destabilisation and immediate failure penalties. The successful trajectories also delay any large corrective action until after a long period of stable motion, whereas the failing runs apply drastic corrections early or at the final step, causing a crash. Moreover, the high‑reward rolls exhibit a pattern of minor, periodic adjustments that keep the agent within a safe corridor, while the low‑reward ones lack these fine‑grained tweaks and instead rely on single, uncontrolled thrusts. This combination of prolonged smooth motion, late‑stage gentle corrections, and continual minor steering allows the agent to harvest reward, whereas abrupt, premature, or absent adjustments produce the negative outcomes observed.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts consistently interleave short bursts of forward thrust with frequent, modest turns (left‑right alternations) that keep the agent centred in the corridor, whereas low‑reward runs tend to issue a single large thrust or maintain straight motion for many frames before a sudden, uncontrolled turn. In the successful episodes the corrective turns occur early and regularly, preventing the vehicle from building up excessive momentum that would carry it into walls; the failing episodes delay or omit these adjustments until the last moment, producing a rapid loss of control. The best trajectories also exhibit a pattern of brief pauses or slower steps after each turn, allowing the state to stabilise before the next action, while the poor trajectories show continuous acceleration without such dampening. Finally, the high‑reward behavior shows an exploratory “back‑and‑forth” oscillation that continually re‑aligns the heading, whereas the low‑reward behavior lacks this re‑alignment and crashes as soon as the initial thrust deviates from the optimal path.

[WORST POLICY]
The higher‑reward rollouts all contain an early corrective action (typically a single “0” or turn command) after the first forward thrust, which redirects the agent away from the left wall before the drift becomes large, whereas the lower‑reward rollouts keep issuing the forward‑thrust action (2) for dozens or hundreds of steps without any timely correction, allowing the state to overshoot and collide. In the high‑reward cases the corrective turn occurs within the first 3–5 timesteps, curbing the exponential growth of position error; in the low‑reward cases the lack of such a turn lets the position spikes accumulate, producing chaotic oscillations that drive the cumulative penalty higher. The brief, decisive change in action also shortens the episode length, so the agent accrues fewer negative step‑wise rewards before termination, while the low‑reward trajectories persist far longer, compounding large penalties. Consequently, early, targeted corrections versus prolonged unchecked thrust explain the systematic reward gap between the two groups.
