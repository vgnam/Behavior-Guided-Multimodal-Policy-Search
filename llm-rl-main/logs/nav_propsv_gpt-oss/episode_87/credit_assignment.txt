[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts all begin with an early, sustained burst of the forward‑thrust action 0 that quickly yields positive sub‑rewards, and they interleave these thrusts with brief, low‑amplitude corrective actions 1 or 2 roughly every 50–100 steps. This regular alternation keeps the agent’s state values confined to a narrow band, preventing the large jumps that precede terminal penalties. In contrast, the low‑reward episodes either delay or skip the initial action 0 burst, and when they do thrust forward they are followed by long stretches without corrective actions, allowing the state to drift and eventually spike dramatically (e.g., jumps to >3.0) before a catastrophic failure. The inconsistent timing of corrections in the low‑reward rollouts—sometimes absent altogether—produces overshoots and oscillations that erase earlier gains. Consequently, the disciplined forward‑thrust‑then‑quick‑adjust rhythm seen in the high‑reward rollouts sustains momentum while avoiding destabilising spikes, directly accounting for the large reward gap.

[BEST POLICY]
The high‑reward rollouts all keep a long uninterrupted stretch of the forward action (action 0) before issuing a turn, so the agent builds momentum along each straight segment and only switches to a turn (actions 1 or 2) after it has aligned with the upcoming bend; the low‑reward rollouts, by contrast, introduce a turn too early or execute a sharp turn abruptly, causing the vehicle to overshoot corners or collide with walls. In the successful runs the agent’s sensor orientation stays roughly parallel to the corridor walls, letting it sense the boundary and adjust smoothly, whereas the failures show the sensors quickly pointing toward a wall and the agent drifting sideways. Timing of corrective actions is also distinct: the good trajectories apply minor, gradual corrections after a few steps of deviation, while the bad trajectories make large, sudden reversals (including backward motion) that abort progress. These consistent differences—sustained forward motion, well‑timed gentle turns, and steady sensor alignment—prevent the agent from losing control and therefore generate much higher cumulative rewards.

[WORST POLICY]
The high‑reward rollouts all contain at least one moment where the agent switches away from the default “forward‑only” action 2 and executes a brief turning or stopping command, usually right before a corridor corner, whereas the low‑reward rollouts never change action and apply 2 continuously for the entire episode. This early, targeted turn prevents the agent from crashing into walls for long periods and produces a short, decisive trajectory that terminates quickly, limiting the accumulation of negative reward. In contrast, the low‑reward sequences exhibit persistent, large‑amplitude oscillations in the state value because the unchanged action cannot correct deviations, causing the agent to bounce back and forth across the maze for many timesteps. The high‑reward behavior also shows a pattern of modest forward thrusts followed by an immediate corrective turn, which keeps the agent’s path aligned with the corridor geometry and avoids the runaway excursions seen in the low‑reward case. Consequently, the occasional action change and early termination in the better rollouts reduce prolonged instability and yield markedly higher (i.e., less negative) total rewards.
