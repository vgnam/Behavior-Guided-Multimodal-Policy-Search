[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts consistently keep a forward‑biased action sequence (mostly actions 0 or 2) punctuated by well‑timed right‑turn actions (action 1) at natural corridor junctions, producing smooth, uninterrupted progress; low‑reward rollouts either insert early backward actions (action 1 used to step back) or make sharp turns without a preceding forward run, causing sudden negative rewards and early terminations. The successful trajectories also show a disciplined timing pattern: a few forward steps are always followed by a single corrective turn before the next forward segment, whereas the poor trajectories exhibit rapid, repeated oscillations—alternating forward and backward or frequent over‑corrections within the same segment. This lack of oscillation in the high‑reward cases keeps the agent’s velocity and orientation stable, preventing collisions with walls or dead‑ends that dominate the low‑reward cases. Consequently, the consistent forward‑forward‑turn rhythm yields higher cumulative reward, while erratic, back‑and‑forth control drains reward and leads to failures.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts repeatedly alternate moderate “turn‑left” (action 0) and “turn‑right” (action 2) commands, creating a smooth oscillatory pattern that continuously corrects the heading while keeping forward thrust modest; this balanced correction prevents the agent from drifting into walls and lets it gradually explore the corridor. In contrast, low‑reward rollouts often issue a single strong forward command (action 1) followed by an abrupt, large‑angle turn, producing a straight‑line thrust that quickly pushes the agent into obstacles and eliminates any chance for corrective adjustment. The successful trajectories also delay aggressive acceleration until after several short forward segments, using brief bursts of thrust only when the oscillation has already centered the vehicle, whereas the failures show early, sustained thrust that overshoots safe zones. Finally, the high‑reward episodes time their direction changes at regular intervals—roughly every few timesteps—so the agent never accumulates a large heading error, while the low‑reward episodes either turn too late or over‑turn, leading to excessive lateral deviation and collisions. This combination of balanced, periodic steering and restrained thrust explains the large reward gap.

[WORST POLICY]
The high‑reward rollouts all contain at least one early **directional correction** – a turn or change of action (often from 2 to 0 or a sharp right‑hand turn) after a few forward steps – whereas the low‑reward rollouts stick with the same forward‑motion action 2 for the entire episode. This brief corrective maneuver prevents the agent from drifting into the wall or a dead‑end, keeping its position trajectory relatively smooth and bounded, while the always‑forward policy generates large, repeated swings in the state value. High‑reward trajectories also show **short bursts of accelerated forward motion** followed by a pause or slower action, giving the agent time to stabilize, while the low‑reward runs exhibit continuous acceleration without any deceleration phase, producing chaotic oscillations. By interleaving forward pushes with timely turns or slower actions, the better rollouts limit overshoot and collisions, which translates directly into a less negative cumulative reward.
