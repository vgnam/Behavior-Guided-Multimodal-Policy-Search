[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts repeatedly exhibit sustained forward bursts (action 2) followed by a single, well‑timed turn, allowing the agent to progress deep into the maze before making any correction, whereas low‑reward rollouts intersperse forward moves with frequent backward actions (action 1) and rapid, jittery turn‑arounds that keep the agent near its start. The successful runs also delay corrective adjustments until after a clear segment has been traversed, resulting in only occasional, brief oscillations, while the poor runs constantly over‑correct at corners, producing many small, back‑and‑forth motions that waste steps and incur negative rewards. In the high‑reward cases the agent’s turn decisions are clustered around maze junctions and are executed with a single, decisive rotation, whereas the low‑reward episodes show multiple micro‑turns within the same junction, indicating indecision and loss of momentum. Moreover, the high‑reward trajectories include short pause periods that stabilize orientation before the next forward burst, a pattern absent in the low‑reward sequences where pauses are replaced by immediate opposite‑direction moves that destabilize the path. These differences—long forward runs, delayed and decisive turning, minimal backward motion, and strategic pausing—reduce unnecessary oscillations and keep the agent on reward‑rich sections of the environment, thereby explaining the observed reward gap.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts all feature a rapid initial forward thrust that is quickly tempered by a sequence of alternating corrective actions (turns 1 or 2) within the first few frames, producing a characteristic oscillatory control pattern; in contrast, the low‑reward episodes either continue straight without any turn (persistent drift) or make only a single late correction before crashing. The successful runs also show a balanced mix of forward moves (action 0) and brief backward or turning adjustments that keep the agent’s state from diverging too far, whereas the poor runs either lack those backward tweaks or over‑accelerate straight toward a wall. Timing matters: the high‑reward agents insert their first turn around frames 8‑10, preventing the buildup of excessive momentum, while the low‑reward agents delay or omit that turn until a sudden, large‑magnitude action forces a collision. Finally, the high‑reward behavior maintains a steady rhythm of short forward bursts punctuated by quick directional changes, creating a “wiggle‑through” motion that navigates obstacles, whereas the low‑reward behavior is either a monotonic drift or an abrupt, uncontrolled swing that ends the episode prematurely.

[WORST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts all contain a brief, decisive forward thrust followed almost immediately by a purposeful change of action (e.g., switching from “2” to “0” or “1”), which produces a clean, straight segment along a wall and then a controlled turn onto the next segment; the low‑reward runs never make such a switch and instead repeat action = 2 for the entire episode, resulting in a continual, uncorrected push that produces large, back‑and‑forth oscillations in the state value. The successful trajectories also keep the agent close to the boundaries of the L‑shaped corridor, limiting lateral drift, whereas the poor trajectories wander freely in the interior and repeatedly overshoot, as evidenced by the wide swings between 1.5 and 7.7. Timing-wise, the high‑reward examples execute their corrective turn within the first few frames, stabilising the motion early, while the low‑reward examples delay any change until after many frames have passed (or never), allowing error to accumulate. Because the environment penalises collisions and excessive drift, the early, varied action sequence and wall‑hugging behavior of the high‑reward rollouts reduce wasted movement and avoid crashes, directly translating into the comparatively higher (less negative) returns.
