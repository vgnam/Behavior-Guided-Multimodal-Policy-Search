[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts repeatedly interleave short bursts of forward thrust (action 0) with timely cornering actions, so that the agent settles into a stable forward segment immediately after each turn and never sustains a long aggressive thrust (action 2) that would overshoot a bend. In contrast, low‑reward runs either linger in aggressive thrust for many steps before correcting, or they reverse direction abruptly after an initial forward push, both of which lead to collisions or premature termination. The successful trajectories also exhibit early, decisive turns at the first and second corners (often left turns) rather than delaying the turn until the agent has already drifted off the intended corridor. Moreover, the high‑reward episodes avoid any late‑stage reversal or sudden stop; once a forward segment is entered, the agent maintains it until the next planned turn, whereas the poor‑performing rollouts introduce a sudden stop or opposite turn just before the episode ends, causing a large negative reward. These timing and sequencing differences keep the agent aligned with the safe path and prevent the uncontrolled spikes that dominate the low‑reward cases.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts repeatedly interleave corrective actions—especially the sharp‑turn action 2—with forward moves, creating a rhythmic “oscillatory” pattern that continuously nudges the agent back toward the corridor centre instead of allowing drift. This pattern appears early in the episode and is repeated at regular intervals, so the agent never stays idle for long stretches and can promptly counteract any deviation. In contrast, low‑reward rollouts either linger in a single state for many timesteps or execute a single aggressive thrust followed by no further corrective actions, leaving the agent to drift until it collides or stalls. The absence of timely turn corrections in the low‑reward cases lets small errors accumulate into large directional overshoots, producing crashes or dead ends. Consequently, the disciplined, frequent use of action 2 to make modest, timely adjustments is the key behavioural component that drives the large reward gap.

[WORST POLICY]
In the better‑performing rollouts the agent breaks the monotony of the default forward action (2) by executing a distinct action or sharp acceleration roughly halfway through the episode, which yields a brief positive reward and a momentary alignment with a wall or goal; this temporary maneuver appears to keep the trajectory from diverging and delays the catastrophic stop. By contrast, the poorest rollouts never deviate from action 2, maintain a uniform forward drift, and exhibit large uncontrolled oscillations that quickly drive the state to extreme values and end in an early crash. The higher‑reward trajectories also tend to persist longer (terminating near 500 steps after the mid‑episode correction), whereas the low‑reward ones often terminate abruptly once the oscillations grow. These timing and action‑change patterns suggest that occasional, well‑timed directional adjustments prevent the agent from hitting obstacles and therefore improve its cumulative reward.
