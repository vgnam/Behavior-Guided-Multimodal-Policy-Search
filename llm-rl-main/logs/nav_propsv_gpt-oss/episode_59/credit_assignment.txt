[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts repeatedly employ short bursts of **action 0** (forward thrust) followed quickly by a corrective **action 2** (right turn) that steers the agent back toward the central corridor, producing a rhythmic “forward‑then‑right” cadence that keeps the state values hovering around the optimal range; in low‑reward rollouts this cadence is missing, with long stretches of a single action (often only 0 or only 2) that let the agent drift into extreme high or low states and terminate prematurely. The successful episodes also execute **timely corrective turns** within the first few timesteps after an initial forward move, preventing the rapid descent seen in the failing runs where the agent continues straight until the height collapses. Moreover, high‑reward trajectories display **small‑amplitude oscillations**—brief back‑and‑forth adjustments that correct overshoot without abandoning progress—whereas the low‑reward cases exhibit either no oscillation (a monotonic slide) or large, destabilising swings that push the state beyond safe limits. Finally, the best rollouts consistently **avoid sustained sequences of action 1** (left turn), which in the poor rollouts lead to prolonged lateral movement and eventual collisions, indicating that disciplined use of forward and right‑turn actions together with rapid correction is the key differentiator in reward.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts keep a steady forward bias (action 0) for long stretches, inserting only brief, gentle right‑hand turns that let the agent hug the outer wall and preserve momentum; low‑reward episodes break this pattern with sudden reversals or sharp left‑hand turns that abruptly cancel forward progress and drive the state toward a collision. The successful runs also time their corrective turns early, using a predictable forward‑turn‑forward rhythm that stabilises the trajectory, whereas the poor runs postpone corrections until late frames, where rapid, erratic oscillations appear and the agent drifts away from the wall. When a high‑reward rollout does over‑correct, the adjustment is modest and quickly followed by another forward step, preventing large negative reward spikes; in contrast, low‑reward trajectories exhibit a single large corrective action that is immediately followed by a drastic state jump and a terminal penalty. Consequently, the combination of sustained forward thrust, early‑stage wall‑following, and small, well‑timed adjustments creates smooth progress and high returns, while abrupt direction changes, delayed corrections, and loss of forward momentum generate the chaotic behavior and low returns seen in the failures.

[WORST POLICY]
The high‑reward rollouts all include a brief burst of forward motion followed by a deliberate change of direction (a left turn or wall‑hugging maneuver), whereas the low‑reward rollouts persist with a single repeated action (action 2) for the entire episode. This early turn lets the agent stay close to a maze wall, limiting large excursions in state value and avoiding the high‑amplitude oscillations that dominate the low‑reward trajectories. In the high‑reward cases the agent’s state changes are modest and monotonic for the first few steps, while the low‑reward episodes show rapid swings between extreme low and high states almost immediately. By keeping the motion smooth and only adjusting heading when needed, the agent reduces the chance of sudden collisions, which explains why those rollouts achieve comparatively higher (less negative) rewards.
