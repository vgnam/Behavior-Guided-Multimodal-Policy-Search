The high‑reward rollouts all contain an early, purposeful change of direction—typically a sharp right turn or a quick use of the brake action—that redirects the agent away from the wall within the first few frames, whereas the low‑reward rollouts persist with the forward‑thrust action for hundreds of timesteps before any corrective action is taken. In the good rollouts the corrective action occurs soon after the initial thrust, limiting the distance traveled into hazardous areas and preventing long‑lasting oscillations, while the bad rollouts exhibit long periods of steady forward motion that produce large, repeated swings in state and only a late, abrupt brake that creates a massive jump and loss of control. This timing contrast means the high‑reward trajectories accumulate far fewer penalty steps before terminating, whereas the low‑reward trajectories accrue many small penalties and then a huge terminal penalty. Consequently, the early, decisive turning/braking in the high‑reward cases keeps the agent near safe regions and curtails cumulative error, while the delayed, absent, or mistimed corrections in the low‑reward cases lead to prolonged instability and the worst rewards.
