[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts all begin with a long, uninterrupted stretch of the forward‑thrust action 0, keeping the state value inside a modest band (≈0.5–2.5) for many frames before any correction is made; low‑reward rollouts either introduce a different action early or switch to action 0 after a few steps, which produces sudden spikes in the state and early collisions. When a deviation does occur, the successful trajectories insert a single, well‑timed corrective action 2 (or occasionally 1) only after the forward motion has re‑stabilised, allowing the agent to nudge back onto the path without overshooting, whereas the poor trajectories either omit these corrections or apply them repeatedly, leading to drift and loss of control. The high‑reward episodes also avoid abrupt state jumps—state changes remain gradual and bounded—while the low‑reward episodes exhibit rapid, large increases in the state (e.g., from ≈1.5 to >3.5) that coincide with negative rewards and terminations. Consequently, the combination of sustained forward thrust, delayed and minimal corrective turns, and smooth state evolution explains why the high‑reward rollouts accumulate far more reward than the erratic, early‑switching, and jittery low‑reward rollouts.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts consistently begin with an early, short forward thrust (action 0) and then immediately follow it with a brief corrective turn (actions 1 or 2), returning to forward motion within a few frames; low‑reward rollouts either delay the first forward thrust or linger on a turn for many steps, allowing the agent to drift far from the optimal path. The successful runs keep the magnitude of state changes modest by alternating forward and correction rapidly, whereas the poorer runs contain long stretches of either sustained turning or sudden large jumps in state value, producing over‑corrections and loss of control. In the high‑reward trajectories the agent’s forward moves are spaced regularly throughout the episode, providing a steady accumulation of positive reward, while the low‑reward episodes rely on a few aggressive bursts that are followed by abrupt negative rewards or crashes. Moreover, the best rollouts end with a brief settling period of minimal movement, stabilizing the final state, whereas the worst rollouts finish with a sharp, last‑minute direction change that triggers a large negative reward. These timing and action‑selection differences keep the agent within navigable corridors in the former case and drive it into collisions or dead‑ends in the latter.

[WORST POLICY]
The high‑reward rollouts all contain an early, purposeful change of direction—typically a sharp right turn or a quick use of the brake action—that redirects the agent away from the wall within the first few frames, whereas the low‑reward rollouts persist with the forward‑thrust action for hundreds of timesteps before any corrective action is taken. In the good rollouts the corrective action occurs soon after the initial thrust, limiting the distance traveled into hazardous areas and preventing long‑lasting oscillations, while the bad rollouts exhibit long periods of steady forward motion that produce large, repeated swings in state and only a late, abrupt brake that creates a massive jump and loss of control. This timing contrast means the high‑reward trajectories accumulate far fewer penalty steps before terminating, whereas the low‑reward trajectories accrue many small penalties and then a huge terminal penalty. Consequently, the early, decisive turning/braking in the high‑reward cases keeps the agent near safe regions and curtails cumulative error, while the delayed, absent, or mistimed corrections in the low‑reward cases lead to prolonged instability and the worst rewards.
