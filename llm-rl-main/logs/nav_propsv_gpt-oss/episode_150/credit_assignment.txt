[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts keep a steady stream of forward actions (action 2) and intersperse them with brief, small‑angle corrective turns (actions 0 or 1) every 2–4 frames, which keeps the agent centered in the corridor and prevents drift toward walls. In contrast, the low‑reward rollouts either omit these regular forward bursts or replace them with sudden large turns or backward moves, allowing the orientation to drift and producing abrupt state jumps that lead to collisions. The timing of corrections also differs: successful runs apply quick adjustments immediately after a forward step, whereas failing runs delay corrections until the agent is already off‑course, resulting in over‑corrections and chaotic oscillations. Because the high‑reward behavior continuously reinforces forward progress while only minimally perturbing direction, it accumulates positive rewards; the opposite pattern—sporadic, aggressive or delayed actions—creates negative rewards and early terminations.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts keep a forward thrust (the “move‑forward” action) for extended stretches and only introduce direction changes when a corner is imminent, producing a smooth‑ish advance that stays centered in the corridor; low‑reward episodes either terminate the forward streak with an immediate, sharp turn (the “hard‑turn” action) or intersperse many abrupt turns, causing the agent to drift into walls. The successful runs also limit the amplitude of their oscillatory corrections—small left‑right nudges that re‑align the heading—whereas the poor runs exhibit large, sudden over‑corrections that create chaotic, back‑and‑forth motion. Timing is another discriminant: in high‑reward trajectories the first turn occurs after the agent has gathered sufficient forward distance, while in low‑reward cases a premature turn is executed early or a late turn is followed by a sudden reversal that ends the episode. By preserving forward momentum, centering the path, and applying only modest corrective oscillations, the high‑reward rollouts avoid collisions and maintain progress, whereas the opposite patterns in the low‑reward rollouts lead to instability, wall contacts, and large negative rewards.

[WORST POLICY]
The high‑reward rollouts all contain at least one deliberate change of action away from the default forward move, producing a sharp left or right turn that lets the agent follow an inner or outer wall for a few steps, whereas the low‑reward rollouts keep using action 2 (forward) throughout the episode and never execute a turn. This early turning—usually within the first few frames—creates a short period of purposeful navigation that delays collision and therefore yields a less negative reward. In the high‑reward cases the agent also begins with a smooth, modest acceleration before the turn, while the low‑reward cases show immediate large jumps in the state value, indicating abrupt, uncontrolled thrusts that lead to erratic oscillations. The combination of varied actions (turns), timed early direction changes, and smoother acceleration in the high‑reward rollouts reduces drift and prevents the extreme penalties seen when the agent merely pushes forward with constant action 2.
