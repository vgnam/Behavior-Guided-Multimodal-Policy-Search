[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts all begin with a long stretch of pure forward thrust (action 0) that lasts dozens of timesteps, establishing steady progress before any turning action is taken, whereas the low‑reward episodes either start with a brief forward thrust followed immediately by a backward or turning command (actions 1/2) or never sustain forward thrust at all. When a correction is needed, the successful runs insert a single, modest turn (action 1 or 2) and then quickly return to forward thrust, spacing these adjustments several steps apart; the failing runs, by contrast, flip between opposite actions on consecutive frames, producing rapid back‑and‑forth oscillations. The high‑reward trajectories keep the state changes smooth and incremental, avoiding large jumps in the position/orientation variable, while the low‑reward trajectories contain abrupt spikes—often accompanied by a negative reward—indicating collisions or loss of control. Finally, the successful rollouts reserve backward actions for rare, last‑minute fine‑tuning, whereas the poor rollouts use backward thrust early and repeatedly, which quickly drives the agent out of the safe corridor and ends the episode with a penalty. These timing and action‑selection differences explain why the former accumulate large positive rewards and the latter terminate with low or negative returns.

[BEST POLICY]
In the successful rollouts the agent spends most of the episode issuing action 0 (forward) and only inserts brief bursts of actions 1 or 2 as tiny corrective nudges, whereas in the failing rollouts the agent uses actions 1/2 early or repeatedly, producing sharp turns that drive it into walls. The high‑reward trajectories show early stabilization – after the first few forward steps the state variance remains low and any correction is quickly damped, while the low‑reward runs let the variance grow and only attempt a large direction change later, which precipitates a crash. When a turn is required, the good rollouts execute it gradually over several steps, keeping the immediate reward positive, whereas the bad rollouts perform an abrupt single‑step turn that incurs a large negative reward and ends the episode. Finally, the successful episodes keep the agent in open corridors and avoid prolonged contact with boundaries, while the unsuccessful ones steer toward corners or outer walls before the sudden turn, causing collisions and heavy penalties.

[WORST POLICY]
The high‑reward rollouts all contain at least one deliberate change of action – usually a sharp turn followed by a period of steadier, lower‑magnitude actions – whereas the low‑reward rollouts keep the same forward‑thrust action (action 2) for almost the entire episode. This brief directional adjustment lets the agent follow walls or spiral curves and keep its state values within a moderate range, while the always‑forward policy produces large oscillations in the state trace and repeatedly drives the agent into obstacles. Moreover, the better rollouts show short bursts of higher‑magnitude moves early on (e.g., the initial sharp turn) and then quickly settle into smoother, slower motion, whereas the poor rollouts never transition to a slower, corrective phase and remain erratic to the end. By inserting timely turns and subsequently reducing speed, the agent avoids the massive -200 penalties that dominate the low‑reward episodes, directly explaining the sizable reward gap. Consequently, the presence of early, purposeful turning combined with a later low‑speed, stable navigation pattern is the key behavioural distinction that yields higher returns.
