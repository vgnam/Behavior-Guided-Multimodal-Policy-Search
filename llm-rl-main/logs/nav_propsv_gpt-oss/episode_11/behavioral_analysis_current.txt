**1. SUCCESS PATTERNS**  
Across the majority of the top‑performing rollouts the agent makes an *initial modest forward move* followed almost immediately by a *directional turn (left or right) that aligns it with the next corridor*. This turn is usually triggered within the first 2–3 decision steps, before any wall contact occurs, and it prevents the forward thrust from slamming into a boundary. The resulting “zig‑zag” motion keeps the agent inside the maze while still progressing, which directly yields the larger positive rewards observed in rollouts 4 and 5.  

**2. FAILURE PATTERNS**  
In most low‑reward rollouts the agent repeats the same *action 0 (pure forward thrust)* for several steps, producing either a negligible state change or an aggressive jump that ends in an immediate collision. No corrective turning is observed; the agent simply drives straight into walls or stalls, leading to the large negative penalties. This lack of re‑orientation and the overly aggressive or stagnant motion consistently correlates with the worst episode scores.  

**3. DISCRIMINATIVE PATTERNS**  
- **(a) Early adaptive turning** – present in ≥4 high‑reward rollouts, absent in all low‑reward ones.  
- **(b) Moderated state change after the first step** – high‑reward episodes show small‑to‑medium increments (≈0.5–1.5 units), whereas low‑reward episodes exhibit either almost no change or a sudden large drop (>4 units).  
- **(c) Diversified action selection** – high‑reward rollouts alternate between forward and turn actions; low‑reward rollouts stick to a single action type (always 0).  
These three signals should be the strongest levers: reinforce (a) and (b) and penalize (c) when it degenerates into monotonic action 0.  

**4. TIMING**  
The most discriminative behavior—early adaptive turning—occurs within the first two to three decision steps, making it a likely causal driver of success. The moderated state change also manifests right after the initial move, reinforcing that early, controlled motion is critical. In contrast, the harmful monotonic‑action pattern persists throughout the episode but is first evident at the start, so suppressing it early will prevent downstream failures.
