[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts consistently contain extended runs of the forward thrust action (action 0) that generate large positive reward spikes, followed only later by brief, precise corrective turns (actions 1 or 2) to fine‑tune the trajectory; in contrast, low‑reward rollouts break forward thrust almost immediately with frequent turns, preventing the agent from accumulating sustained progress and often driving it into walls. The successful episodes also exhibit a clear timing pattern: a stabilizing pause after each forward burst during which the agent makes only small adjustments, whereas the poor episodes show rapid, back‑to‑back oscillations with no such pause, leading to over‑correction and loss of control. When the agent approaches a corner, high‑reward trajectories anticipate the turn and execute a single, well‑timed right‑hand turn, while low‑reward runs tend to overshoot the corner or execute multiple redundant turns, resulting in negative reward spikes. Finally, the high‑reward behavior keeps the agent away from the maze boundaries, using forward momentum to stay centered, whereas the low‑reward behavior repeatedly contacts walls, generating the large negative penalties that dominate the total return.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts consistently alternate forward actions (typically action 0) with brief corrective turns (actions 1 or 2), creating a rhythm of small “wiggle” adjustments that keep the agent centred and prevent it from running into walls; low‑reward rollouts lack this alternation, instead executing long monotonic moves or a single large turn that pushes the agent abruptly toward a boundary. The successful trajectories also delay large directional changes until after an initial stabilising phase, whereas the poor trajectories make a sharp turn early (or never correct at all), leading to immediate collisions or rapid loss of altitude. In the high‑reward cases the agent’s state values remain within a narrow band, reflecting steady‑speed progress punctuated by short, timed reversals that recover from drift, while the low‑reward episodes show sudden spikes or drops in state indicating uncontrolled thrusts. These corrective oscillations appear to provide just enough feedback to counteract stochastic disturbances, whereas their absence or reversal lets errors accumulate, causing crashes and the dramatically lower returns.

[WORST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts all contain an early, purposeful deviation from the default forward action – typically a single thrust or turn within the first few frames that quickly redirects the agent toward a wall and terminates the episode, limiting the number of negative reward steps. In contrast, low‑reward rollouts never break this pattern; they persist with the same forward action (action = 2) for the entire episode, producing long, jittery oscillations that accrue large cumulative penalties. The brief, decisive maneuver in the high‑reward cases creates a rapid loss of control that actually shortens the trajectory, whereas the monotonic, indecisive control in the low‑reward cases keeps the agent wandering without a clear termination point. This timing difference – an early, singular corrective action versus continuous, unchanging thrust – plausibly explains why the high‑reward runs finish with far less negative return.
