[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts all begin with a sustained forward thrust (the dominant early‑stage action) followed by timely, short corrective turns that keep the agent near the target corridor, whereas the low‑reward runs either linger on a single direction for many steps or end with an abrupt, uncontrolled forward action that crashes the episode. In the successful episodes the agent interleaves brief bursts of forward motion with immediate opposite‑direction adjustments, creating a rhythmic “push‑and‑pull” pattern that prevents drift; the failing episodes lack this rhythm, showing either monotonic motion or a sudden reversal only at the very end. The high‑reward trajectories also execute sharp, purposeful turns exactly when approaching a wall or boundary, effectively re‑orienting before collision, while the low‑reward trajectories either turn too late, turn erratically, or never turn at all. This combination of early forward momentum, regular corrective oscillations, and well‑timed boundary‑avoidance turns maintains stable progress and yields higher cumulative reward, whereas the absence or mistiming of those components leads to loss of control and lower scores.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts keep action 0 (forward thrust) dominant, inserting only brief, single‑step right turns (actions 1 or 2) before immediately resuming forward motion, whereas low‑reward episodes insert longer strings of turning or even backward actions that interrupt forward progress. In the successful runs the agent’s turn‑frequency is low and the turns are sharply timed—each corrective turn is followed by a rapid re‑engagement of forward movement—while the failing runs exhibit clusters of consecutive turns or pauses that cause the agent to linger and drift toward obstacles. The high‑reward trajectories also avoid sustained deceleration: after any slowdown the agent quickly accelerates back to full forward speed, whereas the low‑reward cases show pronounced slowing or hesitation before the oscillatory phase begins. By maintaining a steady forward bias and limiting the duration of corrective maneuvers, the high‑reward rollouts accrue the positive reward associated with forward motion and avoid the negative penalties that accumulate during prolonged turning or loss‑of‑control episodes. This consistent pattern of “forward‑most, turn‑briefly, then forward again” explains why those rollouts achieve substantially higher returns.

[WORST POLICY]
The high‑reward rollouts all contain an early, decisive change of action – a sharp turn (often a switch from “2” to “0” or vice‑versa) within the first few frames – and then terminate almost immediately, whereas the low‑reward rollouts spend many dozens or hundreds of steps repeating the same forward action and continuously oscillating back and forth before finally failing. This early, abrupt steering in the better runs prevents the agent from entering long‑lasting, unstable trajectories that accumulate large negative penalties. In contrast, the prolonged monotonic “2” actions in the poorer runs generate repeated overshoots and corrective swings, inflating the total penalty. Consequently, brief, purposeful direction changes that end the episode quickly yield higher (less negative) rewards than extended, oscillatory control that drifts far from the goal.
