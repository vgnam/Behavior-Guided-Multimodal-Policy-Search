High‑reward rollouts all begin with a sustained forward thrust that keeps the state value within a moderate range for several frames before any sharp turn is attempted, whereas the low‑reward episodes either execute an immediate large turn or experience a rapid drop in state value almost immediately after the first action. When a sharp turn is needed, the successful rollouts time the correction shortly after the turn and quickly return to forward motion, showing a brief but decisive “recover‑and‑stabilize” phase; the failing rollouts either omit this recovery or make the correction too late, resulting in prolonged negative rewards or termination. The best trajectories also stay close to environmental boundaries (e.g., following walls of the “C” shape) and use rapid accelerations only when the geometry demands it, while the poor trajectories wander off the safe corridor or collide with obstacles after an aggressive maneuver. Moreover, the high‑reward agents tolerate occasional over‑corrections but immediately damp them with smaller adjustments, preserving a relatively smooth overall speed profile, whereas the low‑reward agents let over‑corrections compound, producing large, unchecked oscillations that lead to instability. These timing and stabilization differences explain why the former accrue large positive rewards and the latter incur early large penalties.
