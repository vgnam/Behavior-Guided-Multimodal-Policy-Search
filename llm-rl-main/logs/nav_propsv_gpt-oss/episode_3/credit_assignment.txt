[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts repeatedly begin with a quick, decisive turn (action 0) followed by several consecutive forward‑oriented steps (action 2) that are kept for longer intervals before any corrective turn, creating a smooth, forward‑driving trajectory; low‑reward rollouts either delay the first turn, alternate rapidly between actions 0, 1 and 2, or insert short forward bursts that are immediately undone, producing fragmented, back‑and‑forth motion. The successful runs also show a clear pattern of postponing corrective actions until the agent has accumulated a sizable positive state value, whereas the poorer runs intersperse corrections almost every few timesteps, leading to frequent over‑corrections and oscillations around the same position. When a negative‑reward action (typically 1) is taken in the high‑reward trajectories, it is followed by a sustained period of the same beneficial action (often another 0 or 2), allowing the agent to recover quickly; in the low‑reward cases a negative action is usually followed by another opposite or random action, preventing recovery and extending the penalty. These timing differences keep the high‑reward agent moving efficiently toward goal regions and limit wasted steps, while the erratic timing in low‑reward rollouts wastes time correcting itself and thus accrues less cumulative reward.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts all begin with a sustained forward thrust that keeps the state value within a moderate range for several frames before any sharp turn is attempted, whereas the low‑reward episodes either execute an immediate large turn or experience a rapid drop in state value almost immediately after the first action. When a sharp turn is needed, the successful rollouts time the correction shortly after the turn and quickly return to forward motion, showing a brief but decisive “recover‑and‑stabilize” phase; the failing rollouts either omit this recovery or make the correction too late, resulting in prolonged negative rewards or termination. The best trajectories also stay close to environmental boundaries (e.g., following walls of the “C” shape) and use rapid accelerations only when the geometry demands it, while the poor trajectories wander off the safe corridor or collide with obstacles after an aggressive maneuver. Moreover, the high‑reward agents tolerate occasional over‑corrections but immediately damp them with smaller adjustments, preserving a relatively smooth overall speed profile, whereas the low‑reward agents let over‑corrections compound, producing large, unchecked oscillations that lead to instability. These timing and stabilization differences explain why the former accrue large positive rewards and the latter incur early large penalties.

[WORST POLICY]
The high‑reward rollouts all feature an early, brief use of action 0 (or a very gentle thrust) that curtails forward momentum before the agent reaches the outer wall, allowing it to stay near the centre and avoid a hard collision, whereas the low‑reward rollouts persist with action 2 for most of the episode, driving the agent into large‑amplitude oscillations that eventually culminate in a sudden crash when a late action 0 is finally issued. In the better rollouts the agent’s speed changes are modest and the state values evolve smoothly, showing a cautious, slowly‑drifting progression; the poorer rollouts exhibit rapid, jagged swings in the state variable, indicating aggressive acceleration and over‑correction. The timing of corrective actions also differs: high‑reward trajectories make small adjustments within the first few steps, preventing the buildup of error, while low‑reward trajectories defer any correction until much later, when the accumulated drift forces a drastic, destabilising maneuver. Because early moderation of thrust reduces wall‑contact penalties and limits the magnitude of oscillations, these patterns lead to consistently higher (less negative) rewards.
