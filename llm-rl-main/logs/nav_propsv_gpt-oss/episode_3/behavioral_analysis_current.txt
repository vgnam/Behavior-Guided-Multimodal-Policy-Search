**1. SUCCESS PATTERNS**  
Across the majority of high‑reward rollouts the agent executes a *steady forward thrust* for the first 2–4 timesteps, keeping the change in its state value modest (Δstate ≈ 0.5–1.0) instead of a sudden jump. Immediately after this gentle thrust the policy tends to issue a *right‑turn* (the only viable turn in the L‑shaped corridor) and then continues with a second short burst of forward motion before any erratic behaviour appears. This combination lets the agent cover a long distance early on, accumulating reward while staying inside the corridor, so the early “controlled‑forward + right‑turn” pattern is the clearest driver of high scores.

**2. FAILURE PATTERNS**  
Most low‑reward rollouts share two detrimental behaviours: (a) an *abrupt acceleration* in the first one or two steps (Δstate > 2.0), which sends the agent crashing into walls almost immediately, and (b) an *over‑cautious, minimal‑movement* phase where the agent issues only tiny forward actions (Δstate < 0.2) and then terminates, suggesting it never reaches the goal region before the episode ends. Both extremes prevent the agent from gaining enough forward progress to earn reward and thus consistently lead to large negative returns.

**3. DISCRIMINATIVE PATTERNS**  
| Rank | Pattern (high vs. low) | Why it matters for the optimizer |
|------|------------------------|-----------------------------------|
| 1 | **Steady, moderate forward thrust (Δstate ≈ 0.5–1.0) in the first 2–4 steps** – common in high‑reward, absent in low‑reward. | Reinforce policies that limit acceleration magnitude early; this directly yields longer traversals. |
| 2 | **Early right‑turn after the initial thrust** – appears in >70 % of high‑reward rollouts, never in low‑reward ones. | Encourage the network to learn the corridor geometry; the turn aligns the agent with the safe path. |
| 3 | **Avoidance of large acceleration spikes (Δstate > 2.0) in the first two steps** – spikes dominate low‑reward, rare in high‑reward. | Penalise actions that produce excessive state jumps; this reduces early collisions. |

**4. TIMING**  
All three discriminative patterns manifest *early* in the episode (within the first 1–4 timesteps). The moderate forward thrust and the subsequent right‑turn happen virtually at the start, making them likely causal drivers of success. The absence of large acceleration spikes is also an early‑stage signal; when it occurs later it merely correlates with already‑established safe behaviour. Consequently, the optimizer should bias the policy’s initial action distribution rather than only adjusting late‑stage dynamics.
