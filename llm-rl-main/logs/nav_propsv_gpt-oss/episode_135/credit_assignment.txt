[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts repeatedly execute extended sequences of forward actions (action 0) that carry the agent deep into corridors before any turn, whereas low‑reward rollouts break forward motion into many brief thrusts interleaved with immediate direction changes. The successful rollouts also time sharp right or left turns precisely at corridor junctions, followed by another long forward stretch, while the poorer rollouts often turn prematurely or linger in place, producing jittery, reactive adjustments. Moreover, the high‑reward trajectories avoid any backward movement; they never reverse direction, whereas the low‑reward examples include frequent back‑and‑forth steps that waste time and incur negative rewards. This disciplined forward‑then‑turn pattern reduces unnecessary oscillations and keeps the agent aligned with the environment’s geometry, directly translating into higher cumulative reward. Consequently, the contrast between sustained, well‑timed forward progress and erratic, overly frequent corrections explains the observed reward gap.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts consistently begin with a brief exploratory thrust followed by **long stretches of sustained forward actions (action 0)** that last many time steps before any correction, whereas low‑reward runs intersperse forward moves with **frequent backward or turn actions (actions 1/2)** almost immediately after starting. The successful trajectories also **delay their first corrective turn** until they have accumulated a stable forward velocity, while the poor runs **oscillate early**, repeatedly reversing direction and over‑correcting within a few steps. When a turn is finally needed, the high‑reward episodes execute it **once and then resume forward motion**, whereas the low‑reward episodes show **multiple rapid turn‑back‑turn sequences** that disrupt progress and cause collisions. Finally, the best rollouts maintain a **steady state‑value (or position) gradient**, indicating smooth advancement toward the goal, while the worst rollouts exhibit **sharp spikes and drops** in state value, reflecting unstable, jittery control that quickly leads to wall contact and large negative penalties. These timing and action‑selection differences explain why the former accumulate large positive rewards and the latter terminate early with low scores.

[WORST POLICY]
The high‑reward rollouts all contain at least one early deviation from the default “action 2” – typically a forward thrust (action 1) or a quick right turn (action 0) within the first few frames – whereas the low‑reward episodes keep action 2 fixed for almost the entire trajectory. This early change produces a brief but purposeful shift in state that steers the agent away from the wall‑hugging oscillations seen in the worst cases, allowing it to make some forward progress before the inevitable failure. In the high‑reward runs the agent also exhibits intermittent corrective actions (short bursts of action 0 or 1) that dampen the drift toward the outer boundary, while the low‑reward runs lack any such corrections and simply bounce between the same extreme state values. The timing of these corrections—appearing just after the initial thrust and before the state starts to diverge—breaks the sustained oscillatory pattern that drives the -500 scores. Consequently, the presence of early, varied actions and timely adjustments reduces prolonged instability and yields the relatively higher (less negative) rewards.
