High‑reward rollouts repeatedly interleave a short burst of forward thrust (action 0) with brief corrective turns (actions 1 or 2) every 5–10 timesteps, keeping the state oscillations modest and preventing drift toward the walls; low‑reward rollouts either sustain long uninterrupted forward thrusts or postpone any turn until after many steps, producing large state jumps that quickly push the agent out of bounds. In the successful trajectories the first corrective turn appears early (by frame 6–8) and is followed by another within the next few frames, establishing a rhythm of “forward → adjust → forward” that stabilises the motion, whereas the failing runs often delay the first turn until after 30–50 frames or execute a single abrupt turn that over‑corrects, leading to loss of control. The high‑reward episodes also maintain a balanced mix of left‑ and right‑turn actions, avoiding prolonged bias in one direction, while the low‑reward cases show a dominant single‑direction turn (often a sudden 90° swing) that sends the agent into a wall. These patterns plausibly explain the reward gap because regular, modest adjustments keep the agent navigating the confined corridor safely, whereas infrequent or overly aggressive direction changes cause collisions and large negative penalties.
