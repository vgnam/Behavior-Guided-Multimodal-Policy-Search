[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts all begin with a sustained forward thrust (action 0) followed by a single, well‑timed turn (action 1 or 2) that aligns with the first corridor junction, after which they keep a long run of forward actions hugging the wall with only occasional corrective turns; low‑reward rollouts, by contrast, insert lateral or backward moves (action 1) almost immediately, producing rapid back‑and‑forth adjustments and many short forward–turn cycles. This early, decisive forward move lets the agent acquire momentum and stay close to the optimal path, whereas the premature side motions in low‑reward episodes constantly reset velocity and cause the agent to overshoot turns. In the high‑reward cases the interval between successive turns is consistently longer (several forward steps), while the low‑reward cases show turn intervals of one or two steps, creating an oscillatory “zig‑zag” that wastes time and leads to collisions with walls. Because the high‑reward behavior preserves a smoother trajectory and avoids unnecessary re‑orientations, it accrues more per‑step reward, while the erratic timing and extra corrective actions in the low‑reward rollouts drain reward by incurring penalties for overshooting and for idle pauses.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts repeatedly interleave a short burst of forward thrust (action 0) with brief corrective turns (actions 1 or 2) every 5–10 timesteps, keeping the state oscillations modest and preventing drift toward the walls; low‑reward rollouts either sustain long uninterrupted forward thrusts or postpone any turn until after many steps, producing large state jumps that quickly push the agent out of bounds. In the successful trajectories the first corrective turn appears early (by frame 6–8) and is followed by another within the next few frames, establishing a rhythm of “forward → adjust → forward” that stabilises the motion, whereas the failing runs often delay the first turn until after 30–50 frames or execute a single abrupt turn that over‑corrects, leading to loss of control. The high‑reward episodes also maintain a balanced mix of left‑ and right‑turn actions, avoiding prolonged bias in one direction, while the low‑reward cases show a dominant single‑direction turn (often a sudden 90° swing) that sends the agent into a wall. These patterns plausibly explain the reward gap because regular, modest adjustments keep the agent navigating the confined corridor safely, whereas infrequent or overly aggressive direction changes cause collisions and large negative penalties.

[WORST POLICY]
The high‑reward rollouts all contain an early, purposeful change of direction – a sharp left or right turn within the first two‑three frames – whereas the low‑reward rollouts spend the majority of the episode executing the same forward action (action 2) for dozens or hundreds of steps before any turn occurs. This early diversification lets the agent avoid immediately colliding with walls or corners, limiting the accumulated penalty, while the prolonged straight‑line thrust in the low‑reward cases drives the agent deeper into confined corridors where small orientation errors snowball into large crashes. Additionally, the higher‑reward runs exhibit brief, corrective adjustments that are timed shortly after the initial turn, preventing the oscillatory over‑corrections that dominate the later frames of the low‑reward trajectories. Because the worst policy’s reward is heavily penalized by late‑stage collisions, the combination of an early turn and timely minor corrections plausibly explains why those rollouts earn relatively less negative scores.
