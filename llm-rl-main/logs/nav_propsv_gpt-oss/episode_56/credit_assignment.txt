[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward episodes all feature long stretches of uninterrupted forward thrust (action 0) that carry the agent deep into the corridor before any turn is attempted, with only occasional brief corrective actions (1 or 2) timed just after a clear corner is reached; this preserves momentum and keeps the agent on the intended path. In contrast, low‑reward rollouts show an early switch from forward to a turning or braking action, often within the first few steps, which produces sudden changes in heading, loss of speed and immediate negative rewards. The successful runs also limit oscillatory behavior, using corrective actions sparingly and only after several stable forward steps, whereas the failing runs continuously alternate between actions, resulting in jerky back‑and‑forth motions that cause the agent to drift off the track. By maintaining a steady forward drive and postponing corrections until they are geometrically necessary, the high‑reward rollouts avoid the destabilising over‑corrections that dominate the low‑reward trajectories and therefore accumulate far higher cumulative rewards.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts repeatedly exhibit long, uninterrupted sequences of action 0 (steady forward thrust) that carry the agent through straight corridors before any turn is taken, whereas low‑reward rollouts introduce actions 1 or 2 almost immediately or switch back and forth between them, causing premature and erratic heading changes. When a turn is required, the successful rollouts delay the first non‑zero action until the geometry of the wall is clearly reached (e.g., around frame 350 in Rollout 2), producing a clean, single‑step adjustment, while the poor rollouts execute sharp, back‑to‑back rotations that overshoot the wall and lead to collisions. The high‑reward trajectories also show a clear pattern of re‑establishing action 0 immediately after each corrective turn, quickly regaining forward momentum; in contrast, the low‑reward episodes linger in oscillatory bursts of actions 1/2, alternating between correction and regression, which prevents the agent from accumulating positive reward. Finally, the successful rollouts maintain a relatively smooth state trajectory with only brief, isolated spikes, whereas the failing rollouts display continuous large fluctuations in state values, indicating over‑correction and loss of control that directly reduces the cumulative reward.

[WORST POLICY]
The high‑reward rollouts all share a brief, decisive maneuver—typically an abrupt turn or a single forward step—followed almost immediately by episode termination, whereas the low‑reward rollouts consist of long sequences of the same forward action that keep the agent moving back‑and‑forth for many frames. This early, aggressive change in direction or rapid stop prevents the agent from lingering in the maze and thus limits the cumulative penalty it can accrue, while the prolonged forward thrust in the low‑reward cases produces sustained oscillations and repeated corrections that add up to a much larger negative return. Moreover, the high‑reward episodes exhibit a clear shift in orientation (e.g., from facing outward to turning inward) within the first couple of steps, a behavior that never appears in the low‑reward runs, which maintain a roughly constant heading throughout. Because penalties are incurred at each time step, the combination of fewer steps, an early decisive turn, and a rapid termination plausibly explains why those rollouts achieve relatively higher (less negative) rewards.
