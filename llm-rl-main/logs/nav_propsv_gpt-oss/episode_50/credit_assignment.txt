[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts all contain an extended early segment of uninterrupted forward actions (action 0) that lasts several hundred timesteps before any turning occurs, allowing the agent to accumulate large positive rewards while making steady progress toward the goal; in contrast, the low‑reward rollouts intersperse forward moves with frequent sharp turns (actions 1 or 2) almost immediately, breaking momentum and generating many negative or zero‑reward steps. When a turn is finally required, the high‑reward trajectories execute only small, incremental adjustments—typically a single right‑turn followed by a few forward steps—whereas the low‑reward runs swing abruptly back and forth, repeatedly overshooting and then correcting, which creates the oscillatory patterns described. The timing of corrective actions also differs: high‑reward agents delay corrections until the forward streak has clearly passed a waypoint (e.g., around t≈400–600), while low‑reward agents react instantly to minor deviations, resulting in rapid over‑corrections. Because sustained forward motion maximizes per‑step reward and minimizes wasted directional changes, these timing and action‑selection differences plausibly explain why the former achieve substantially higher cumulative returns.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts all keep action 0 (the forward thrust) active for long stretches, only inserting brief, timed corrective actions 1 or 2 when the trajectory begins to deviate, which yields steady progress and positive step rewards. In contrast, the low‑reward episodes either abandon the forward thrust almost immediately or replace it with a single aggressive thrust or sharp turn, causing the agent to overshoot, drift, or hit a wall. The successful rollouts also synchronize their corrections to occur after a few frames of forward motion, allowing the state values to settle before any adjustment; the failures apply turns or accelerations without this settling period, producing oscillations that quickly destabilize the path. Because sustained forward motion accumulates reward while premature or excessive turning incurs penalties, this timing and action pattern directly explains the large reward gap.

[WORST POLICY]
The high‑reward rollouts all contain at least one deliberate deviation from the default “action = 2” thrust—either an early turn, a brief pause (action = 0), or a change in steering direction—whereas the low‑reward rollouts stick to action = 2 for every frame. This single corrective action lets the agent move off the initial straight line, briefly align with the curved corridor and make forward progress before the episode ends, while the constant thrust in the low‑reward cases produces large, uncontrolled oscillations around a single position. In the better rollouts the timing of the deviation occurs early (within the first few frames), giving the agent time to gain distance before any failure, whereas the low‑reward trajectories never introduce a corrective signal and therefore never escape the oscillatory region. The presence of an early, purposeful adjustment therefore reduces the magnitude of the negative reward by preventing the agent from remaining stuck in a self‑reinforcing back‑and‑forth motion.
