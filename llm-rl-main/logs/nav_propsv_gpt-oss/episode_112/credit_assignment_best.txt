High‑reward rollouts keep long uninterrupted stretches of the forward action (action = 2), only inserting brief, single‑step turns (action = 0 or 1) after the agent has already progressed several timesteps, which lets the state remain in a stable mid‑range and avoids sudden wall contacts; in contrast, low‑reward episodes insert turns or backward steps almost immediately, often within the first few frames, causing the state to swing toward the boundaries and trigger collisions. The successful runs also exhibit a regular rhythm of forward motion followed by a single corrective turn, producing smooth, monotonic trajectories, whereas the poor runs display rapid, back‑and‑forth oscillations—multiple consecutive turn actions and frequent reversals—that break momentum and generate erratic paths. When a turn is needed, high‑reward agents make a modest adjustment (a single action = 0) rather than a sharp, multi‑step right turn, which prevents over‑steering into walls; low‑reward rollouts frequently over‑correct with aggressive right‑turn sequences that push the agent into corners. Finally, the timing of corrective actions in the high‑reward cases is delayed until sufficient forward distance has been covered, allowing the agent to consolidate progress before fine‑tuning, while the low‑reward cases intervene too early, interrupting forward progress and leading to premature terminations. These consistent differences in action length, turn magnitude, and correction timing explain why the former accumulate large positive rewards and the latter suffer large penalties.
