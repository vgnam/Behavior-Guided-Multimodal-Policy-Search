[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts keep a long uninterrupted stretch of the forward action (action 2) at the start of the episode and reuse it throughout, inserting only a single corrective left or right turn (actions 0/1) when a corner is reached, whereas low‑reward runs break the forward streak with rapid alternating left/right actions that create jittery, back‑and‑forth motion. The successful trajectories also time their corrective turn precisely after a few forward steps, allowing the agent to stay on the optimal corridor, while the poor trajectories display repeated over‑corrections and oscillations over many frames (e.g., frames 5‑10 and 16) that pull the agent off course. In the high‑reward cases the agent’s state values decline smoothly, reflecting steady progress toward the goal; in the low‑reward cases the state values spike up and down as the agent repeatedly accelerates then drifts, eventually colliding with a wall. This disciplined pattern of “forward‑most‑of‑the‑time, minimal and well‑timed turns” preserves momentum and reduces unnecessary lateral movement, directly translating into higher cumulative reward. Conversely, the erratic, turn‑heavy behavior wastes steps, induces collisions, and caps the reward.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts keep long uninterrupted stretches of the forward action (action = 2), only inserting brief, single‑step turns (action = 0 or 1) after the agent has already progressed several timesteps, which lets the state remain in a stable mid‑range and avoids sudden wall contacts; in contrast, low‑reward episodes insert turns or backward steps almost immediately, often within the first few frames, causing the state to swing toward the boundaries and trigger collisions. The successful runs also exhibit a regular rhythm of forward motion followed by a single corrective turn, producing smooth, monotonic trajectories, whereas the poor runs display rapid, back‑and‑forth oscillations—multiple consecutive turn actions and frequent reversals—that break momentum and generate erratic paths. When a turn is needed, high‑reward agents make a modest adjustment (a single action = 0) rather than a sharp, multi‑step right turn, which prevents over‑steering into walls; low‑reward rollouts frequently over‑correct with aggressive right‑turn sequences that push the agent into corners. Finally, the timing of corrective actions in the high‑reward cases is delayed until sufficient forward distance has been covered, allowing the agent to consolidate progress before fine‑tuning, while the low‑reward cases intervene too early, interrupting forward progress and leading to premature terminations. These consistent differences in action length, turn magnitude, and correction timing explain why the former accumulate large positive rewards and the latter suffer large penalties.

[WORST POLICY]
The high‑reward rollouts all contain at least one **non‑constant action** – usually a brief use of action 0 (a reverse or brake) or a turn that steers the agent away from an imminent wall – whereas the low‑reward episodes apply action 2 every step with no corrective maneuvers. This occasional reversal or turn appears early in the episode, creating a short “reactive” segment that pulls the agent back from a collision course, while the low‑reward runs never break the forward thrust and therefore accumulate larger penalty swings. In the higher‑reward traces the agent’s **timing of the corrective action** is clustered around the moment it would hit a boundary, giving it a chance to limit the severity of the failure; the worst rollouts lack any such timing cue and simply oscillate under an unchecked forward acceleration. Consequently, the presence and timely execution of a single opposing action reduces the magnitude of the terminal penalty, producing the modestly better rewards observed in the high‑reward rollouts.
