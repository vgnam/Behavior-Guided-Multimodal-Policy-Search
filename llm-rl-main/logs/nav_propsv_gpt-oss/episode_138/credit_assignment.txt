[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts all keep the forward action (the “0” command) for long uninterrupted stretches, only inserting occasional gentle turns or pauses, whereas low‑reward runs insert abrupt direction changes, backward steps, or sharp turns almost immediately after starting. The successful trajectories also delay any large orientation adjustments until after a stable forward segment, while the failures over‑correct within a few frames, causing the agent to collide with walls. When a turn is needed, high‑reward episodes execute it gradually, producing a smooth curved path; in contrast, low‑reward episodes swing hard left or right, generating oscillatory or jerky motion that quickly destabilises the agent. Finally, the high‑reward behavior avoids any reversal of motion, maintaining a monotonic advance toward the goal, whereas the low‑reward behavior frequently backs up or pauses, wasting time and exposing the agent to hazardous positions. These patterns preserve steady progress and prevent collisions, directly translating into the large per‑step rewards seen in the top rollouts.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts all contain an early phase of brief exploratory actions (often a mix of 1 and 2) followed by a **sustained stretch of action 0** that propels the agent forward for several consecutive frames, whereas the low‑reward episodes either never produce such a forward‑thrust segment or terminate before it can develop. When the agent does deviate from the forward path, the successful runs insert **quick corrective actions** (a single 1 or 2) exactly after a few frames of overshoot, then immediately resume action 0, while the failing runs either make **abrupt, large state jumps** without any corrective step or apply corrective actions too late, leading to collisions. Timing-wise, the best rollouts exhibit **regular, short‑duration oscillations** (one‑step corrections) spaced at predictable intervals, whereas the poor rollouts show **irregular, long‑gap pauses** or sudden spikes in state value that precede termination. This pattern suggests that maintaining a continuous forward drive and promptly counter‑steering small errors keeps the agent on a viable trajectory, while missing the forward streak or correcting too late lets the agent drift into failure states, producing the observed reward gap.

[WORST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts all contain an early decisive change of action – they begin with a forward thrust (action 2) but within the first few dozen steps switch abruptly to a braking or reverse command (action 0), usually when they encounter the first corner. This quick reversal limits how far they travel into the confined corridor and prevents them from accumulating many forward‑thrust penalties, whereas the low‑reward rollouts either never issue the braking command or postpone it until the very last frame, staying in a perpetual forward thrust that produces long‑lasting oscillations. The high‑reward episodes also show a recognizable pattern of an initial straight‑line segment followed by a sharp turn attempt, even if the turn is over‑corrected, indicating that the agent at least attempts to respond to the geometry of the L‑shaped maze. In contrast, the low‑reward trajectories display smooth but aimless forward motion with only minor adjustments, lacking any clear response to upcoming bends. Because the environment penalizes time spent in the wrong region and collisions, the early corrective action in the high‑reward rollouts reduces exposure to those penalties, yielding a comparatively better (less negative) total reward.
