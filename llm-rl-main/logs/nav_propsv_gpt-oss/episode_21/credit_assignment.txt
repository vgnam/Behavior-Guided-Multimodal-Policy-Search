[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts consistently keep a forward thrust for several consecutive steps before executing a turn, allowing the agent to follow the outer wall of the maze smoothly and only correct its heading when a corner is reached; low‑reward rollouts break this pattern with rapid alternations of forward and turning actions, producing jerky, over‑corrected motions that often lead to collisions. The successful episodes also exhibit deliberate, small‑angle turns aligned with sensed wall proximity, whereas the poor episodes show large, abrupt direction changes that ignore sensor cues and overshoot the intended path. Timing-wise, the high‑reward trajectories maintain long uninterrupted forward segments punctuated by brief, measured corrections, while the low‑reward ones insert frequent pauses or backward adjustments that interrupt progress and cause the agent to become stuck or hit obstacles. Finally, the high‑reward behavior tends to stay close to the walls and progress clockwise or counter‑clockwise in a consistent direction, whereas the low‑reward behavior wanders off the wall, reverses direction unpredictably, and fails to complete the loop, directly reducing the accumulated reward.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts all exhibit a rhythm of short forward thrusts (action 0) punctuated by frequent corrective turns or slight backward adjustments (actions 1–2), which keeps the agent near a safe corridor and lets it reap positive incremental rewards; in contrast, the low‑reward episodes contain either a single prolonged straight thrust with no corrections or a sudden large‑magnitude action that drives the state far outside the safe region, causing immediate large penalties. The successful trajectories also delay any major direction change until after a few timesteps of steady progress, allowing the agent to gather momentum before the next correction, whereas the failures show abrupt orientation shifts in the first or second frame that terminate the episode. Moreover, the high‑reward cases maintain a quasi‑oscillatory pattern—alternating forward and small reverse moves—that stabilises position and prevents collisions, while the low‑reward cases lack this balance and either overshoot or never adjust, leading to wall impacts. This systematic presence of regular, modest corrective actions versus their absence or reversal explains the stark reward disparity.

[WORST POLICY]
The high‑reward rollouts all contain at least one brief non‑forward action (a turn or a “stop” action) shortly after the initial thrust, whereas the low‑reward runs keep applying action 2 for hundreds of steps without any correction. This early corrective move redirects the agent away from the wall and limits the build‑up of large state‑value excursions that dominate the low‑reward trajectories. In the better rollouts the forward thrusts are clustered in short bursts followed by a pause or a steering adjustment, while the worst rollouts exhibit long, uninterrupted forward thrusts that produce huge oscillations in position and speed. Those intermittent adjustments keep the agent’s trajectory more bounded and delay or avoid the catastrophic collision that yields the very large negative reward seen in the low‑reward cases. Consequently, the presence of timely corrective actions—and the avoidance of sustained monotonic thrust—appears to be the key behavioural difference driving the reward gap.
