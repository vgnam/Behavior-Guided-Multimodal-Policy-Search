[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts keep long uninterrupted stretches of the forward action (action 0) while only inserting occasional, gentle right‑turns (action 2) to stay flush with the outer or inner wall, producing a smooth wall‑following trajectory; low‑reward rollouts instead flip rapidly between actions (0, 1, 2) producing abrupt direction changes and large jumps in the state value, which leads to collisions or dead‑ends. The successful runs also maintain a relatively constant distance‑to‑wall signal (state values staying within a narrow band), whereas the poor runs show wide oscillations that indicate over‑correction and loss of contact with the boundary. Timing-wise, the good trajectories exhibit extended periods (several frames) of the same action before a turn, while the bad ones switch actions every frame or two, creating jerky motion. Because smooth wall‑following keeps the agent inside the navigable corridor and avoids sudden impacts, it accrues steady positive rewards, whereas erratic, high‑frequency turning drives the agent into obstacles and yields large negative penalties.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts repeatedly interleave forward thrusts (action 0) with brief, well‑timed lateral adjustments (actions 1 or 2) roughly every 50–100 steps, allowing the agent to stay close to walls and quickly re‑align after each turn; low‑reward rollouts either persist in forward thrust without such corrections (e.g., the monotonic forward drift in low‑reward 1) or delay adjustments until they become large, destabilising the trajectory. In the successful runs the agent makes an early, sharp turn that positions it on a favourable corridor, whereas the poor runs often miss this early re‑orientation and only turn later, when momentum is too high to control. The high‑reward behavior also shows consistent small‑amplitude oscillations that continuously correct drift, preventing the agent from veering off the intended path, while the low‑reward cases exhibit either no oscillation or overly aggressive, late over‑corrections that lead to jerky motion and collisions. These timing‑specific corrective patterns keep the agent progressing steadily through the maze, directly translating into the larger cumulative rewards observed.

[WORST POLICY]
The high‑reward rollouts all contain at least one early non‑forward decision – a turn or a brief reduction in thrust – that lets the agent briefly follow a wall or re‑orient before the crash, whereas the low‑reward episodes apply the same forward‑only action (action = 2) at every step and never change direction. This single corrective maneuver appears within the first few frames of the better rollouts, creating a short period of stable, wall‑hugging motion that slows the buildup of large state swings; the worst rollouts lack any such timing cue and therefore oscillate wildly around a single thrust level. Because the better rollouts intermittently break the monotonic forward pressure, they avoid the extreme overshoot and repeated collisions that generate the –500 penalty, while the constantly thrusting low‑reward trajectories accumulate large, uncorrected state fluctuations that terminate with the maximum negative reward. Consequently, the presence of an early directional adjustment and variable action timing is the key behavioral component that separates the relatively higher‑reward outcomes from the uniformly disastrous ones.
