[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts consistently begin with a brief, decisive alignment phase – a single turn (action 0) followed almost immediately by sustained forward motion (action 1 or 2) – whereas low‑reward rollouts linger in the first few steps, alternating turns and forward moves and creating prolonged early oscillations. Once aligned, the high‑reward episodes contain long uninterrupted forward stretches that carry the agent quickly through corridors, while the low‑reward episodes intersperse short forward moves with frequent corrective turns, causing the agent to waste steps and drift near walls. When a corner is reached, the high‑reward policy executes a sharp turn (action 0) and then proceeds forward in the next step, whereas the low‑reward runs often over‑correct with multiple successive turns before moving ahead. This pattern of early commitment, timely corner turning, and extended forward progress reduces unnecessary back‑and‑forth motion, allowing the agent to accumulate more positive reward per timestep compared with the jittery, indecisive behavior seen in the low‑reward rollouts.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts repeatedly interleave a short forward push (action 2) with an immediate corrective turn (usually action 0 or 1) whenever the state drops toward the narrow “reward band” around 1–2, causing the agent to linger in regions that grant the +5.00 bonuses; low‑reward rollouts either continue straight for too long or turn only after a long drift, so they miss those bonus zones and often run off the track. The successful runs also exhibit a tight oscillatory rhythm – a few steps of forward motion followed by a rapid turn – that keeps the trajectory tightly bounded within the corridor, whereas the failures show either a single prolonged forward segment or a delayed, jerky turn that pushes the agent into walls or termination states. Moreover, the best rollouts tend to execute a corrective turn exactly when the state begins to rise after a dip (e.g., at 1.05), re‑stabilising the path before any overshoot, while the poor rollouts either skip this turn or apply it too late, allowing the state to diverge and trigger a crash. This timing discipline prevents early termination and maximises the cumulative reward, explaining the large gap between the two groups.

[WORST POLICY]
The high‑reward rollouts all contain an early decisive move – usually a single forward thrust (action 2) followed within one or two steps by a sharp corrective action (often action 0) that forces an immediate failure, truncating the episode after only a handful of frames. In contrast, the low‑reward rollouts never introduce such a corrective turn; they persist with action 2 for the entire episode, producing long, jittery trajectories that oscillate back and forth for dozens of steps. This timing difference means the high‑reward trajectories accrue far fewer per‑step penalties before the terminal negative reward is applied, whereas the low‑reward runs continually add small penalties through their prolonged oscillations. Consequently, the brief, abrupt crash pattern in the high‑reward rollouts limits total loss, while the sustained, undirected oscillatory behavior in the low‑reward rollouts drives the much larger negative return.
