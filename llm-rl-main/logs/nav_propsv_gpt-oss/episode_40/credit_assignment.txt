[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts consistently interleave a brief forward thrust (action 1) with immediate, precise corrective turns (actions 0 or 2) at short, regular intervals, and each corrective burst coincides with a +5.0 reward spike, keeping the agent’s state tightly bounded (≈ 1–3). In contrast, low‑reward rollouts either postpone the corrective turn, allowing the forward motion to drift into walls, or execute prolonged back‑and‑forth oscillations that never line up with a positive reward, leading to state excursions and collisions. The timing difference is critical: the successful runs apply a rapid turn within a few frames of the forward move, “locking‑in” the reward before the trajectory can overshoot, whereas the failures wait many frames or over‑rotate, causing over‑correction and loss of control. This disciplined, fast‑turn‑after‑move pattern yields steady progress and repeated goal contacts, while its absence or reversal produces unstable trajectories and large negative penalties.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts consistently begin by **orienting the agent with a sharp turn before applying forward thrust**, so the first few actions are turns (actions 1 or 2) that line the vehicle up with the inner corridor, after which a sustained series of forward actions (action 0) carries it forward for several frames; low‑reward rollouts do the opposite, launching immediately with a strong forward thrust (action 2) while still mis‑aligned, which sends them into walls. The successful trajectories also show **regular, low‑amplitude oscillations** – brief corrective turns spaced every 3–5 steps – that keep the state value drifting only slightly, whereas the failures exhibit **large, abrupt state jumps** and long‑lasting direction changes that overshoot the safe region. Moreover, high‑reward episodes maintain **longer uninterrupted forward segments** (multiple consecutive action 0 steps) punctuated by brief adjustments, giving steady progress and accumulating positive reward, while low‑reward episodes interleave forward and turn actions almost every step, producing jittery motion and premature terminations. Finally, the best rollouts delay any aggressive corrective turn until after a clear forward gain, allowing momentum to be harnessed, whereas the poor rollouts react instantly with harsh corrections that cancel momentum and lead to instability, explaining the stark reward gap.

[WORST POLICY]
The high‑reward rollouts all contain an early, decisive change in action – typically a switch from a neutral or forward command to a different discrete action within the first few steps – which quickly drives the episode toward termination and prevents the agent from remaining in the environment long enough to accrue the maximal penalty. In contrast, the low‑reward rollouts stay on the same forward action for hundreds of steps, producing a persistent oscillatory pattern in the state that never resolves and therefore yields the worst possible reward. The brief, purposeful turn or stop seen in the better rollouts also tends to be timed just before the agent’s state begins to diverge sharply, acting as a forced “stop‑gap” that caps the damage. By avoiding prolonged, repetitive forward commands, the high‑reward trajectories limit the amount of overshoot and oscillation that dominate the low‑reward behavior, explaining why they achieve a comparatively higher (less negative) return.
