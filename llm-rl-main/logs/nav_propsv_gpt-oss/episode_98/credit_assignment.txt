[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts consistently interleave a strong forward bias (action 0) with short, well‑timed turn actions (actions 1 or 2) that occur the moment the state value begins to deviate, so the agent quickly re‑aligns and keeps advancing toward the goal. In contrast, low‑reward rollouts either linger in a prolonged forward drift without corrective turns or delay the turn until the deviation has grown large, which lets the trajectory wander off the optimal path. The successful episodes also show brief, decisive turn bursts followed immediately by another forward segment, whereas the poor episodes exhibit either sustained oscillatory corrections (multiple rapid back‑and‑forth actions) or a single large correction that creates a sudden loss of forward momentum. This pattern preserves momentum and limits time spent in negative‑reward states, directly translating into higher cumulative reward. Consequently, the presence of prompt, bounded turn‑then‑forward cycles appears to be the key behavioral difference driving the reward gap.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts repeatedly begin with a sustained forward thrust that lasts several timesteps before any turn is attempted, allowing the agent to gain distance quickly and collect the large positive per‑step rewards; in contrast, low‑reward episodes either turn immediately or make a single abrupt movement that sends the state sharply downward, causing early termination. When a turn is finally made in the successful runs, it is preceded by a brief “pause” in which the agent’s action switches to a low‑magnitude command (often action 2) that steadies the heading, after which the turn is executed smoothly; the failed runs show either no pause or an abrupt high‑magnitude turn that overshoots the corridor and hits a wall. Throughout the high‑reward trajectories the agent interleaves occasional corrective turns with long stretches of straight motion, creating a rhythmic pattern of forward‑then‑adjust‑then‑forward that keeps the trajectory inside the maze’s safe corridor, whereas the low‑reward trajectories lack this rhythm, exhibiting either continuous erratic turning or a single sudden turn that leaves the safe corridor. Finally, successful rollouts display a gradual reduction of reward volatility—reward values stay near the positive range—while the failing rollouts show spikes of large negative reward coinciding with abrupt state drops, indicating that the timing and magnitude of actions directly determine whether the agent stays in control or crashes.

[WORST POLICY]
In the better‑performing rollouts the agent consistently makes an early, purposeful turn—often a right or left deviation that lets it follow the nearest wall instead of continuing straight ahead, thereby staying away from the outer boundary longer. By contrast, the worst rollouts either never change from the default forward action or only switch actions at the very end, resulting in a straight‑line drift that quickly hits a wall. The higher‑reward episodes also feature a brief, high‑magnitude thrust that is promptly followed by a corrective maneuver, whereas the low‑reward sequences show either a constant thrust with no correction or a sudden uncontrolled jump with no subsequent adjustment. These early corrective decisions and their timing prevent premature collisions, which plausibly explains why the high‑reward rollouts obtain significantly less negative returns.
