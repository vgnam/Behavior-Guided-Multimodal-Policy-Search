[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts all begin with a sustained forward action (action 0) that keeps the state within a modest range, then introduce only small, incremental turn actions (1 or 2) to correct the trajectory, whereas the low‑reward episodes start with an abrupt turn or a large thrust (often action 2) that instantly drives the state to an extreme value and ends the episode. In the successful runs the agent’s corrective actions are spaced out over many frames, producing a smooth, oscillatory pattern that gradually steers around obstacles, while the failures exhibit a single, rapid displacement followed by immediate termination. The high‑reward trajectories also avoid long periods of inactivity or “stillness,” maintaining continuous forward momentum; the low‑reward cases contain brief, sharp pauses or sudden stops that precede a crash. By consistently applying modest forward thrusts and only modest, timed adjustments, the agent stays inside the navigable corridor, whereas the aggressive, poorly‑timed maneuvers in the low‑reward rollouts push it into walls or off‑track, directly accounting for the large reward gap.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts consistently begin with several consecutive forward actions (action 0) that carry the agent deep into the maze before any corrective turn is taken, whereas low‑reward episodes typically insert a sharp turn or a sudden acceleration within the first few frames, breaking forward momentum and leading to early collisions. In the successful runs the occasional corrective actions (1 or 2) are spaced out and modest, serving only to fine‑tune the trajectory after a stable forward stretch; the failing runs either over‑correct immediately after a turn or execute abrupt, large‑angle turns that swing the agent far off the intended path. The best rollouts also delay any backward or reverse‑direction moves until the very end of the episode, if they appear at all, while the worst rollouts sometimes reverse direction early, indicating loss of confidence in the current heading. Finally, the high‑reward behavior shows a pattern of smooth, gradual oscillations that keep the agent close to walls without hitting them, whereas the low‑reward behavior exhibits erratic, high‑frequency oscillations that culminate in wall impacts or “fail” states, directly reducing cumulative reward.

[WORST POLICY]
The high‑reward rollouts all contain an early, decisive change in action – within the first few timesteps the agent switches from a straight‑forward thrust (action 2) to a turning action, often a sharp right turn, which redirects its trajectory before large oscillations can build up. In contrast, the low‑reward rollouts stay on action 2 for dozens or hundreds of steps, accumulating sustained forward motion that becomes increasingly noisy and only later make a sudden turn that ends in a crash. This early diversification of actions in the better rollouts limits the duration of unstable drift and gives the agent a chance to correct its heading, whereas the prolonged monotonic forward drive in the poorer rollouts lets errors compound and leads to larger penalties when the eventual turn occurs. Consequently, the presence of an early corrective turn and a shorter, more varied action sequence plausibly explains why the high‑reward episodes lose less reward than the low‑reward ones.
