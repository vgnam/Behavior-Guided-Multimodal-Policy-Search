[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts repeatedly use action 0 (steady forward thrust) for long uninterrupted stretches, only inserting brief corrective actions 1 or 2 when the state begins to drift, whereas low‑reward episodes either terminate after a single forward step or intersperse forward moves with frequent, premature turns that quickly push the agent out of the corridor. In the successful runs the corrective turns occur after a measurable lag—typically after several seconds of forward motion—allowing the agent to build momentum before a small adjustment, while the failing runs apply turns almost immediately (or at the very end) resulting in abrupt state changes and collisions. The best rollouts also display a “smooth‑oscillation” pattern: a dominant forward phase followed by a single, modest turn, then a return to forward motion, which keeps the trajectory inside the navigable region; the low‑reward cases show erratic, back‑and‑forth oscillations with multiple successive turns or backward actions that destabilize the path. Finally, high‑reward episodes avoid any prolonged backward or sideways actions, whereas the poorest rollouts contain sudden backward‑type moves or large angular changes that correlate with large negative rewards, indicating loss of control.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts consistently combine a dominant forward action with frequent, short corrective turns (left or right) that are applied immediately after a change in heading, keeping the agent centered on the corridor and preventing large deviations; low‑reward rollouts either persist with a single action for many steps or make only a single late correction, which lets the trajectory drift toward walls and results in premature failure. The successful rollouts also show a pattern of modest, steady speed—small incremental state increases—whereas the poor rollouts contain abrupt, large state jumps or aggressive thrusts that overshoot the safe region. Timing-wise, the high‑reward agents insert corrective actions within one or two steps of encountering a corner, while the low‑reward agents delay corrections until the agent has already collided or entered an unstable region. Because the environment penalizes collisions heavily, the early and regular micro‑adjustments in the high‑reward runs maintain safe navigation and accrue positive reward, whereas the delayed or absent adjustments in the low‑reward runs lead to loss of control and large negative penalties.

[WORST POLICY]
The high‑reward rollouts all contain a brief, decisive change of direction (a sharp turn or orientation shift) that occurs within the first few‑hundred steps, whereas the low‑reward rollouts spend the majority of the episode maintaining the same forward action (action 2) and only crash after a long period of oscillatory motion. In the better runs the agent’s state values rise steadily at first, then the turn quickly reduces the range of subsequent state fluctuations, keeping the trajectory confined to a narrow band; in the poorer runs the state swings wildly between low and high values for hundreds of steps, indicating repeated overshoot and correction. The higher‑reward episodes also terminate relatively early (≈180–200 steps) after the turn, so the accumulated penalty is limited, while the low‑reward episodes continue for the full horizon (~1000 steps), accruing many more negative rewards before the final abrupt stop. Finally, the successful rollouts exhibit smooth, consistent thrust during forward motion, whereas the failing rollouts show jittery, erratic thrust patterns that produce the large oscillations. These timing and control differences plausibly explain why the high‑reward rollouts incur a smaller total penalty than the low‑reward ones.
