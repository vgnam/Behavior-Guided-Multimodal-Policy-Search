[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts repeatedly begin with an early, decisive sharp right turn (action 2) that aligns the agent with the main corridor, after which they sustain long uninterrupted stretches of forward action (action 0) and only make brief, targeted adjustments; low‑reward rollouts either postpone or omit this initial turn and instead spend most of the episode alternating between forward and corrective actions (1 or 2), producing frequent back‑and‑forth jitter. The successful trajectories also exhibit a clear temporal pattern: a short burst of corrective moves right after the turn, followed by several seconds of steady forward motion before any further adjustment, whereas the poorer runs intersperse corrective moves continuously, never establishing a stable forward phase. Moreover, high‑reward episodes tend to reserve oscillatory micro‑adjustments for tight corners near the end of the maze, using them sparingly, while low‑reward episodes apply such oscillations throughout the path, causing repeated over‑corrections and negative‑reward hits. This contrast suggests that committing to a clean forward thrust after an early proper alignment—and limiting unnecessary corrections—preserves momentum and avoids the penalty‑inducing oscillations that drag down the total reward.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts keep long uninterrupted forward stretches (action 0) before executing a single right turn (action 2), creating a regular rhythm of forward‑move → right‑turn → forward‑move that lets the agent hug the maze walls without overshooting. By contrast, low‑reward episodes insert turns much earlier or combine left turns (action 1) with right turns, producing abrupt direction changes that quickly misalign the agent with the corridor and lead to collisions. The successful trajectories also show smooth, modest adjustments after each turn—small corrective actions that dampen oscillations—whereas the failing runs display sudden, large corrective spikes that amplify drift and trap the agent in tight loops. This disciplined timing and bias toward right‑hand wall‑following preserves momentum and prevents the catastrophic state jumps that dominate the low‑reward behaviors.

[WORST POLICY]
The high‑reward rollouts all contain brief moments where the agent switches away from the default “action 2” forward thrust and executes a corrective turn (often to the right) that keeps it hugging the outer wall or redirects it toward the interior, whereas the low‑reward rollouts never deviate from action 2 and simply thrust forward at every step. In the better rollouts these turns are timed just after the agent reaches a corner or begins to drift, providing an early re‑orientation that prevents a large collision and limits the episode length; the worst rollouts lack any such timely correction and therefore oscillate in place until the timeout penalty is applied. The higher‑reward trajectories also show short, purposeful straight‑line segments followed by a single, decisive change in direction, creating a smoother, more controlled path, while the low‑reward episodes display long stretches of identical thrust interspersed with erratic state jumps that indicate uncontrolled overshoot. By intermittently breaking the monotony of action 2, the agent reduces wall‑contact events and accumulates a less negative reward, whereas the constant‑action pattern leads to persistent drift, repeated collisions, and the maximal –500 penalty. Consequently, the presence of occasional corrective actions and their early timing is the key behavioral difference that explains the reward gap.
