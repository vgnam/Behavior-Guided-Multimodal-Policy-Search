[CURRENT POLICY]
In the successful rollouts the agent interleaves brief forward bursts (action 0) with immediate, well‑timed sharp turns (action 1 or 2) within the first few frames, positioning itself correctly for upcoming bends, whereas the failing episodes either continue straight for many steps before a late turn or make a sudden last‑minute turn that leads to a crash. The high‑reward trajectories keep oscillatory adjustments low, applying only a single corrective action after each turn instead of the repeated back‑and‑forth swings seen in the low‑reward cases. They also maintain continuous forward momentum through corridors without prolonged pauses or lateral shuffling, while the poor rollouts contain extended periods of stillness or side‑wise motions that waste time and bring the agent into hazardous states. Finally, the timing of corrective turns is consistent—occurring just before a corner is reached—preventing wall collisions, whereas low‑reward episodes exhibit missed early turns or overly aggressive late turns that result in termination.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts consistently interleave brief forward bursts (action 0) with immediate corrective turns (actions 1 or 2) every few frames, so the agent continuously re‑aligns its heading and stays inside the corridor, whereas low‑reward rollouts linger in long straight stretches of forward motion before a single, delayed turn that often comes too late to avoid a wall. The successful trajectories also show early, sharp pivots right after the episode starts, establishing the correct lane within the first few steps, while the poor trajectories postpone any major heading change until midway or later, allowing the agent to drift off‑track. Moreover, the high‑reward episodes exhibit small, frequent micro‑adjustments—tiny oscillations that correct minor drift—whereas the low‑reward ones display either no adjustments or large, erratic swings that amplify deviation. This pattern of proactive, regularly timed turning and continual fine‑tuning keeps the agent progressing toward reward‑giving states, while the delayed or absent corrective actions let errors accumulate and lead to collisions, explaining the stark reward gap.

[WORST POLICY]
The high‑reward rollouts all contain an early intervention – either a swift change from the default forward action (2) to the stop action (0) or an abrupt right‑turn within the first few frames – that truncates the episode before the agent can accumulate a large forward‑thrust penalty, whereas the low‑reward rollouts persist with action 2 for the entire horizon, driving a prolonged, unstable forward motion. This early switch curtails the distance travelled and therefore limits the magnitude of the negative per‑step reward, while the continued “always‑forward” signal in the low‑reward cases produces many more steps of drifting and oscillation that pile up large penalties. In the high‑reward cases the abrupt change also prevents the agent from entering deeper parts of the maze where collisions are inevitable, while the low‑reward trajectories never perform such a pre‑emptive correction and eventually hit walls after many erratic hops. Consequently, the presence of a timely action 0 (or sharp turn) early in the episode is the key behavioral component that separates the relatively better outcomes from the disastrous, constantly‑thrusting runs.
