[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts all begin with an immediate burst of action 0 (forward thrust) and keep long uninterrupted stretches of this action, only inserting brief corrective actions 1 or 2 before quickly resuming forward motion; the low‑reward rollouts either delay their first forward thrust, insert several consecutive turns, or spend extended periods on actions 1/2 that yield negative rewards. In the successful runs the corrective turns are tightly timed—usually a single 1 or 2 immediately after a forward segment—so the agent restores alignment and returns to forward motion within a few steps, whereas the poor runs show clusters of back‑to‑back turns that create oscillations and prevent the agent from regaining momentum. Successful trajectories also avoid any late‑stage surge of rapid, erratic action changes that produce large negative rewards, while the worst rollout ends with a sudden series of sharp right turns and accelerations that culminate in a crash. Finally, the high‑reward episodes exhibit a pattern of “forward → brief turn → forward” repeated throughout, maintaining a net positive reward flow, whereas the low‑reward episodes contain “turn → turn → forward” or “forward → prolonged turn” patterns that interrupt the reward stream and lead to lower cumulative scores. These timing and sequencing differences explain why the policy earns substantially higher returns when it front‑loads and quickly re‑establishes forward motion and penalizes it when it lingers in corrective or destabilising actions.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts all begin with an early, small forward thrust (action 0) that yields an immediate positive reward and then quickly settle into a rapid alternation between forward moves and corrective turns (actions 0 and 2) that keep the agent oscillating around a safe corridor rather than committing to long straight motions. This alternating pattern appears consistently after the first few frames and provides frequent modest rewards while preventing the agent from hitting walls. In contrast, the low‑reward rollouts either issue a single, unchecked forward thrust (often action 2) that drives the agent straight into a boundary, or they fail to introduce the corrective turn cycle, leaving the trajectory monotonic and ending in a crash. The presence of early positive feedback combined with continuous corrective oscillations therefore sustains progress and avoids catastrophic collisions, explaining the large reward gap between the two groups.

[WORST POLICY]
The high‑reward rollouts all contain at least one moment where the agent deviates from the default “action 2” (continuous forward thrust) – either by initiating a brief turn, applying a stronger thrust, or switching to a different action that quickly drives it into a wall, ending the episode after only a few hundred steps. In contrast, the low‑reward rollouts never change action 2; they persist with the same forward command for the entire horizon, producing long, oscillatory or chaotic trajectories that keep the agent alive and therefore accumulating a larger sum of negative step penalties. This early, decisive change in control in the higher‑reward cases creates a rapid, directed collision that truncates the episode and limits the total penalty, whereas the absence of any such change in the low‑reward cases lets the agent wander aimlessly and accrue the maximal −500 penalty. Consequently, brief, purposeful deviations from constant thrust appear to be the key behavioral component that lifts the reward, while their omission results in the worst performance.
