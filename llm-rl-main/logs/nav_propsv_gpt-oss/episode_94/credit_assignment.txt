[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts consistently feature long stretches of the forward action followed by only a single‑step turn (right or left) and then another forward stretch, creating a rhythmic pattern of forward‑pause‑adjust‑forward that keeps the agent aligned with the corridor. They also make frequent tiny corrective actions—small oscillations that subtly re‑center the vehicle rather than executing large, abrupt direction changes. In contrast, low‑reward episodes contain very short forward segments, immediate multi‑step or aggressive turns that thrust the state far from the safe path, and they lack the steady micro‑adjustments that maintain alignment. This disciplined pacing lets the high‑reward agent stay inside the track and accrue positive step rewards, while the erratic, sudden motions of the failures lead to collisions and early negative termination. Moreover, the successful rollouts include brief pauses or zero‑action intervals that let the agent stabilize before the next maneuver, a stabilizing pause that is absent from the constantly shifting low‑reward runs.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts consistently begin with a sustained forward motion that lasts several timesteps before any turn is made, allowing the agent to accumulate positive reward early and establish a stable trajectory; in low‑reward rollouts the first turn or sharp correction occurs almost immediately, interrupting forward progress and often leading to early negative reward. When a turn is required, the successful rollouts execute it gradually—using a single “turn” action (e.g., action 2) followed by a short series of corrective “fine‑tune” actions (action 1) that keep the state value increasing—whereas the poor rollouts apply abrupt, large‑angle turns (multiple consecutive turn actions) that overshoot the target and produce rapid drops in state. The high‑reward trajectories also exhibit a clear pattern of re‑establishing forward motion right after each correction, so the agent spends most of the episode moving forward rather than lingering in oscillatory back‑and‑forth adjustments; low‑reward episodes linger in these oscillations, repeatedly alternating between opposing actions without regaining forward momentum. Finally, successful rollouts avoid any late‑stage, high‑magnitude turn that reverses direction, while the failures all contain a final large turn that drives the state into a terminal “fail” region, directly truncating the episode and eliminating remaining reward.

[WORST POLICY]
The high‑reward rollouts all contain a brief, decisive change in action early in the episode—a sudden turn or thrust that quickly moves the agent away from its start and reorients it toward a new wall segment—whereas the low‑reward rollouts persist with the same action (action 2) for the entire run, never introducing a sharp directional shift. This early, aggressive maneuver in the high‑reward cases creates a short burst of forward progress that, even though it ends in failure, avoids the prolonged oscillations seen when the agent repeatedly over‑corrects around a fixed value. In the low‑reward episodes the state value bounces back and forth for many timesteps, indicating the agent is stuck in a narrow corridor and cannot break out of a steady‑state loop. Consequently, the presence of an initial, high‑magnitude turn and the subsequent rapid termination in the high‑reward rollouts prevents the long‑term instability that drags down the low‑reward runs, accounting for the modest reward improvement.
