[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts consistently begin with an early forward thrust (action 0) that is sustained for several steps before any turn is attempted, whereas low‑reward episodes either terminate soon after a brief forward move or spend most of the episode without a clear forward advance. In the successful runs the agent interleaves forward actions with short, purposeful turns (actions 1 or 2) at regular intervals, using these corrections to stay on a productive trajectory instead of waiting until the end of the episode to adjust. The timing of those corrective turns is also distinct: they occur soon after a forward segment and are brief, preventing prolonged oscillations, while the poor rollouts either exhibit a single large, late turn that leads to a sudden divergence or repeat tiny, ineffective adjustments that never produce net progress. Moreover, high‑reward trajectories avoid long periods of stagnation or repeated back‑and‑forth movements, maintaining momentum through the environment, whereas the low‑reward cases show extended loops or idle phases that keep the state near its origin and incur negative rewards. These patterns together explain why the high‑reward rollouts accumulate positive returns—steady forward motion punctuated by timely, modest corrections—while the low‑reward ones lose reward through early failure, excessive late correction, or persistent lack of advancement.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts all contain long uninterrupted stretches of action 0, which push the agent steadily forward and generate a steady stream of positive rewards before any turn is taken; low‑reward rollouts either omit these forward streaks or replace them with a single abrupt maneuver that immediately drives the state to a large value. When a turn is needed, the successful trajectories insert a corrective action (1 or 2) only after the forward run has built enough momentum, and the turn is brief, followed quickly by another forward segment, keeping the state changes modest; the failing runs execute a sharp turn at once, producing a sudden, large jump in the state and a massive negative reward. The high‑reward behavior also displays a pattern of small‑amplitude oscillations around a central path, allowing the agent to continuously re‑align without overshooting, whereas the low‑reward episodes show a single, extreme deviation that pushes the agent out of the navigable corridor. By pacing forward motion, timing corrective turns, and limiting the magnitude of each state change, the good rollouts stay within safe regions and accrue reward, while the bad rollouts sacrifice that pacing and incur catastrophic penalties.

[WORST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts all contain an early, sustained period of forward thrust (action 2) that lasts for dozens of timesteps before any turn or stop is taken, whereas low‑reward rollouts either intersperse frequent direction changes or insert a premature zero‑action that halts progress. In the better runs the agent postpones sharp turns until it has built up a steady trajectory, while the poorer runs exhibit immediate or repeated oscillatory corrections that bounce the state value back and forth. The high‑reward trajectories also avoid abrupt reversals: when a turn is finally made it is a single, decisive rotation rather than a rapid back‑and‑forth swing, which prevents the agent from colliding with walls. Finally, the successful episodes terminate only after a clear boundary encounter, whereas the low‑reward episodes end with an early, sudden stop that truncates any accumulated forward momentum, leading to larger negative returns. These timing and action‑consistency differences explain why the smoother, longer‑lasting forward motion yields higher (less negative) rewards.
