[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts all begin with an early forward thrust (action 0) followed within a few steps by a sharp corrective turn (typically action 2 or 1), keeping the agent moving forward while quickly steering away from walls; the low‑reward rolls either omit that initial forward push or delay the turn, allowing the trajectory to drift into a collision or a dead‑end. In the successful episodes the agent repeatedly interleaves short bursts of forward motion with brief, timed adjustments that keep it at a steady distance from the inner wall, whereas the poor episodes either execute long smooth drifts without any corrective nudges or over‑oscillate with back‑and‑forth actions that never produce sustained forward progress. The high‑reward trajectories also show a pattern of “micro‑stabilization” – a forward step, a one‑step turn, then another forward step – which constantly re‑aligns the heading before errors accumulate; the low‑reward runs lack this rhythm, either staying too steady (so they hit an obstacle) or turning too aggressively, causing overshoot. This disciplined alternation of forward thrusts and timely, modest turns yields steady positive rewards, while the opposite timing or absence of these components leads to the large negative penalties seen in the low‑reward rollouts.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts consistently sustain long stretches of the forward‑motion action, punctuated only by brief corrective turns that quickly bring the agent back onto a stable trajectory, whereas low‑reward episodes either abandon forward motion early or fail to apply any corrective actions after drifting away. In the successful runs the agent accelerates at the start, then settles into a steady forward pattern before using a single corrective action to adjust direction, while the poor runs show a persistent increase (or decrease) in the state value without a stabilising forward phase, indicating uncontrolled drift. The timing of corrections in the high‑reward cases occurs after a predictable interval—once the agent has built momentum—whereas the low‑reward trajectories either never correct or make untimely, large‑angle turns that push them off the intended path. These disciplined forward phases and timely, modest adjustments keep the agent inside the rewarding corridor, while the absence or reversal of those patterns leads to collisions or exits that generate large negative rewards.

[WORST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts all begin with a brief, gentle forward thrust followed by a purposeful, timed turn that brings the agent into close contact with a wall, after which it follows the wall with a steady curvature and only minor adjustments; low‑reward rollouts either never execute such an early aligning turn or keep the same forward action (action = 2) without adapting, causing the agent to drift away from the wall almost immediately. The successful trajectories show a smooth, monotonic increase in the state variable during the initial segment, whereas the poor trajectories exhibit large, abrupt spikes in the state that correspond to oscillatory overshoots and corrective swings. In the high‑reward cases the agent’s speed and direction change gradually, keeping oscillations minimal, while the low‑reward cases display rapid, jerky reversals and over‑corrections that quickly destabilize the motion. This contrast means the high‑reward policy maintains a stable wall‑following behavior that avoids collisions and premature terminations, whereas the low‑reward policy’s lack of early wall alignment and its erratic, high‑frequency state fluctuations lead to loss of control and the worst possible rewards.
