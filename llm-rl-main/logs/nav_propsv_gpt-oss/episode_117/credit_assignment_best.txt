High‑reward rollouts all begin with a sustained sequence of the same forward‑motion action (typically action 1 or 2) that lasts for several dozen steps before any turn is attempted, whereas the low‑reward rollouts introduce sharp turns or switch actions almost immediately and then alternate rapidly between left and right commands. When a direction change is needed, the successful episodes make only a brief, modest correction—often a single‑step turn that quickly returns to forward thrust—while the failures exhibit prolonged back‑and‑forth oscillations, repeatedly flipping between opposite actions for many frames. The good rollouts also keep the state value within a relatively narrow band after the initial few steps, showing early stabilization, whereas the bad rollouts allow the state to drift to extreme positive or negative values before a crash occurs. Finally, positive reward spikes in the high‑reward trajectories line up with periods of uninterrupted forward motion, while the low‑reward trajectories only receive large negative rewards when the accumulated oscillations finally lead to a collision. These contrasting timing and magnitude of action switches plausibly explain why the smoother, more stable control yields far higher returns.
