[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts keep a steady forward thrust (action 0) for extended intervals and only insert brief, well‑timed left or right turns (actions 1 or 2) when a corner is imminent, producing a smooth, progressive trajectory; the low‑reward rollouts break this pattern with sudden halts or backward thrusts (often an unexpected action 0 or a rapid switch to action 2) that immediately generate large negative rewards and lead to collisions. The successful trajectories also show minimal oscillation—each turn is followed by a quick return to forward motion—whereas the failing runs contain frequent jittery adjustments, over‑corrections, and rapid direction flips that destabilize the path. In the high‑reward cases the agent’s orientation changes gradually, allowing its sensory input to anticipate walls, while the low‑reward cases display abrupt orientation spikes that cause the agent to run into inner boundaries. Because continuous forward progress coupled with precisely timed, brief turns maximizes distance covered without incurring penalty, whereas erratic reversals and mistimed stops waste steps and trigger crashes, this contrast directly explains the large reward gap.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts all begin with a sustained sequence of the same forward‑motion action (typically action 1 or 2) that lasts for several dozen steps before any turn is attempted, whereas the low‑reward rollouts introduce sharp turns or switch actions almost immediately and then alternate rapidly between left and right commands. When a direction change is needed, the successful episodes make only a brief, modest correction—often a single‑step turn that quickly returns to forward thrust—while the failures exhibit prolonged back‑and‑forth oscillations, repeatedly flipping between opposite actions for many frames. The good rollouts also keep the state value within a relatively narrow band after the initial few steps, showing early stabilization, whereas the bad rollouts allow the state to drift to extreme positive or negative values before a crash occurs. Finally, positive reward spikes in the high‑reward trajectories line up with periods of uninterrupted forward motion, while the low‑reward trajectories only receive large negative rewards when the accumulated oscillations finally lead to a collision. These contrasting timing and magnitude of action switches plausibly explain why the smoother, more stable control yields far higher returns.

[WORST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts keep the agent’s state confined to a relatively narrow band and deliberately execute a wall‑following turn early in the episode, whereas low‑reward rollouts let the state swing wildly across the full range and never introduce a purposeful change of direction until the final step. In the better runs the agent occasionally inserts a “stop/turn” action (0) well before the episode ends, signalling a planned termination that prevents it from drifting into a boundary; the worst runs only switch to action 0 at the moment of collapse after prolonged, unchecked forward thrusts (action 2). These early corrective turns produce smoother trajectories with modest oscillations, while the low‑reward cases exhibit large, erratic jumps that repeatedly overshoot and then reverse, indicating over‑correction. By staying closer to the wall and limiting extreme excursions, the high‑reward behaviors avoid the catastrophic collisions that generate the large negative rewards seen in the low‑reward rollouts.
