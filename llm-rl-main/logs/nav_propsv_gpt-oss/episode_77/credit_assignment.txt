[CURRENT POLICY]
In the successful rollouts the agent habitually strings together extended sequences of forward‑type actions before issuing a single turn, creating a smooth advance toward the goal, while the failing rollouts make an abrupt turn or a large state jump almost immediately after start‑up. The high‑reward trajectories keep the state magnitude within a moderate band, showing only small corrective adjustments, whereas the low‑reward ones contain sudden spikes to extreme values that directly precede termination. Consistently, the good rollouts interleave exactly one turn action after a stable forward run, allowing proper alignment with the corridor, whereas the bad rollouts either omit this aligning turn or execute multiple rapid direction changes that destabilize motion. This disciplined timing preserves incremental positive reward and avoids collisions, while the erratic timing in the poor rollouts produces over‑corrections and large negative penalties.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts consistently pair a dominant forward action with short, regularly‑spaced corrective turns (actions 1 or 2) that are brief enough to keep momentum but large enough to steer around obstacles, creating an “oscillatory‑control” rhythm. In contrast, low‑reward rollouts either omit these frequent micro‑adjustments—driving straight until they collide—or apply a single, overly‑aggressive turn that abruptly changes direction and leads to loss of control. The timing pattern in the successful runs shows corrective turns occurring every few timesteps, preventing the agent from lingering in a single orientation, whereas the poor runs exhibit long gaps between turns or a solitary massive turn late in the episode. This steady alternation of forward thrust and modest re‑orientation maintains forward progress while avoiding walls, directly translating into higher cumulative rewards.

[WORST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts all contain a brief corrective action (often action 0 or 1) shortly after an initial aggressive thrust, whereas the low‑reward runs never deviate from the constant forward command action 2. This early switch lets the agent re‑orient or brake before it leaves the safe corridor, while the always‑forward policy in the low‑reward cases drives the agent into ever‑larger oscillations and eventual collisions. In the high‑reward episodes the aggressive move is limited to one or two steps and is followed by a pause or opposite thrust, which keeps the trajectory close to the inner wall; the low‑reward episodes sustain the forward thrust for the entire episode, causing the state values to swing wildly and drift outward. Because the corrective step curtails uncontrolled acceleration, the agent incurs fewer large negative penalties, producing the comparatively higher (less negative) rewards.
