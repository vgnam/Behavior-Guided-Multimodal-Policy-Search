[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts consistently begin with a steady series of forward actions (action 0) and only introduce turning or backward actions (actions 1‑2) after the agent has already built up momentum, keeping the state values within a modest band; low‑reward rollouts either start with an abrupt turn or backward move, or introduce many direction changes early, causing large spikes in the state and frequent collisions. The successful trajectories exhibit sparse, well‑timed corrective adjustments—typically a single short turn or reverse that quickly restores alignment—whereas the poor trajectories display continuous oscillatory control, repeatedly over‑correcting and swinging back and forth between actions 1 and 2. In the high‑reward cases the agent’s path remains largely smooth, with long uninterrupted forward segments that carry it through corridors, while the low‑reward cases contain short forward bursts punctuated by erratic, rapid direction switches that waste steps and incur negative rewards. This contrast suggests that disciplined forward progression combined with minimal, precisely timed corrections yields higher cumulative reward, whereas excessive or mistimed turning/back‑stepping generates instability and penalizes performance.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts keep action 2 (the forward move) dominant for the first several steps, building steady momentum before any turn, whereas low‑reward runs insert sharp left/right actions (often action 0 or 1) almost immediately, causing abrupt direction changes. The successful trajectories also space their turns – typically a single directional action after a block of three or more forward steps – while the poor ones cluster multiple turns together, producing jerky, over‑corrected motion. When a turn is made in the high‑reward cases it is modest (a brief switch to action 1 or 2) and quickly followed by a return to forward motion, keeping the state value inside a safe band; in contrast, low‑reward episodes execute large swings (e.g., from action 2 to action 0) that push the state outside that band and lead to collisions. Finally, the high‑reward agents delay any aggressive acceleration until the environment is well‑aligned, whereas the low‑reward agents accelerate aggressively early and then crash, so the smoother, paced forward‑then‑turn pattern consistently yields the larger cumulative reward.

[WORST POLICY]
The high‑reward rollouts all contain a brief but decisive change of action (usually a single “0” turn) after many steps of forward thrust, which sharply reduces the state value and pulls the agent away from the boundary before termination; the low‑reward rollouts never make this corrective turn and instead repeat action 2 for the entire episode, allowing the state to wander far and oscillate until a catastrophic collision. In the better rollouts the agent’s early forward motion is modest (state changes of only a few units per step) and the trajectory stays roughly centered, whereas the worst rollouts exhibit large, uncontrolled swings in the state even while taking the same action, indicating a lack of positional feedback. The timing of the corrective turn in the high‑reward cases—typically after the agent has approached a dangerous region—creates a short, purposeful retreat that limits total negative reward, while the low‑reward cases delay any change until the very end, when a forced stop produces a huge penalty. Finally, the high‑reward trajectories show brief periods of smooth, directed progress followed by an abrupt, purposeful maneuver, whereas the low‑reward ones consist of persistent, unmodulated forward thrust with only noisy oscillations, which prevents the agent from ever stabilising and leads to far lower returns.
