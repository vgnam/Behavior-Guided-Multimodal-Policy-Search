[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts keep a steady forward thrust for many consecutive frames and only apply small, gradual turns to follow walls, whereas low‑reward episodes start with a large, aggressive acceleration that quickly devolves into erratic, over‑corrected motion or immediate crashes. In the successful runs the agent rarely reverses direction; backward or hesitating steps appear only as brief, fine‑tuned adjustments, while the poor rollouts contain abrupt backward jumps or sudden large orientation changes that break momentum. Timing-wise, the high‑reward trajectories delay sharp turns until they encounter a clear obstacle, using smooth curvature to stay near the boundary, whereas the low‑reward cases make frequent, abrupt left‑hand turns early and repeatedly, producing a jagged path. This disciplined forward‑biased pacing preserves velocity and reduces collision risk, directly boosting cumulative reward, while the chaotic, high‑amplitude corrections in the failures dissipate energy and increase the chance of hitting walls, resulting in large negative penalties.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts repeatedly exhibit a prolonged stretch of the forward‑movement action (action 0) early in the episode, establishing a steady advance through the maze before any turns are attempted; low‑reward runs either abandon forward motion almost immediately or intersperse action 0 with frequent switches to actions 1 or 2, causing early collisions or premature terminations. When turns are needed, the successful rollouts apply a single corrective action and then quickly return to action 0, producing only brief, purposeful deviations, whereas the failing rollouts display rapid, repeated oscillations between actions 1 and 2 that over‑correct the trajectory and push the agent against walls. The high‑reward trajectories also delay any large directional changes until after a stable forward segment (typically after t≈150–200), allowing the agent to build momentum and accumulate positive reward, while the low‑reward episodes introduce abrupt direction reversals or sharp turns at much earlier timesteps, leading to immediate negative rewards. Finally, the best rollouts maintain relatively monotonic state values (steady decrease) during the forward phase, indicating consistent progress, whereas the poor rollouts show sudden spikes in state values that correspond to the erratic back‑and‑forth motions that terminate the episode. These patterns—sustained early forward action, minimal and timely corrections, and avoidance of rapid oscillations—explain why the high‑reward rollouts gather far more reward than the low‑reward ones.

[WORST POLICY]
The high‑reward rollouts all contain an early, deliberate wall‑following segment in which the agent uses a brief burst of the forward‑thrust action (2) followed almost immediately by a turn‑or‑stop action (0 or a slight left/right adjustment) that keeps it hugging the inner boundary of the corridor; this prevents it from drifting into open space and limits the number of large‑penalty collisions. In contrast, the low‑reward rollouts persist with action 2 for many timesteps, never issuing a timely corrective turn, so they wander far from the walls, accumulate larger positional swings and eventually crash when they finally switch to action 0. The better rollouts also show a clear timing pattern: a single, well‑placed corrective action occurs within the first 10–15 steps, after which the trajectory remains stable, whereas the poorer rollouts either lack any early correction or apply a corrective action only after the agent has already built up excessive speed and oscillation. This early, purposeful turn reduces cumulative negative reward by avoiding the high‑penalty crashes that dominate the low‑reward episodes. Consequently, the presence of an early, bounded corrective action—and the absence of prolonged, unchecked forward thrust—accounts for the systematic reward gap between the two groups.
