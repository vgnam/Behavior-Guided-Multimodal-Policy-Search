[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts repeatedly pair a brief forward thrust (action 0) with an almost immediate corrective maneuver (often action 2 or 1) within the same or next timestep, keeping the agent’s state tightly bounded around the target corridor; low‑reward episodes either omit this quick correction or postpone it, allowing the state to drift far before a large, destabilising adjustment is attempted. The successful rollouts also insert short “pause” frames—moments of no thrust or minimal action—right before a direction change, which lets any overshoot settle before the next thrust, whereas the poorer rollouts show abrupt direction flips without an intervening pause, producing overshoot and loss of control. Moreover, the high‑reward trajectories generate frequent small positive‑reward events (≈5 pts) at regular intervals, reflecting steady progress, while the low‑reward runs have long gaps between such spikes or experience a single large negative correction that terminates the episode. This pattern of rapid, localized correction combined with brief stabilization periods prevents cumulative error buildup, explaining why the same oscillatory style yields much higher total reward when those timing cues are present.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts repeatedly introduce short, timely action switches—most often flipping between action 0 (forward) and action 2 (away) within a few frames after the first negative reward—so the agent can quickly correct a slip and stay near the goal, whereas low‑reward runs tend to persist with a single action for long stretches, allowing drift or overshoot to accumulate unchecked. In the successful episodes the agent makes an early, decisive turn when it reaches a junction (usually within the first 5–8 frames), then oscillates back and forth around the target corridor, which keeps its state values bounded; the poor rollouts either skip this early turn or continue straight until termination, producing a monotonic decline in state value. Whenever a negative reward appears, the high‑reward trajectories immediately respond with an opposite action, converting the penalty into a corrective maneuver, while the low‑reward trajectories ignore the signal and keep the same motion, letting the penalty compound. Finally, the high‑reward behavior deliberately “wiggles” around the goal—alternating actions to hover close to the optimal region—whereas the low‑reward behavior exhibits a single aggressive thrust that pushes the state far from the goal and results in divergence.

[WORST POLICY]
The high‑reward rollouts all terminate after only a few steps, usually after an initial forward thrust (action 2) is followed almost immediately by a decisive change—either a switch to action 0 or a collision that ends the episode—so the agent accrues only a short burst of per‑step penalty. By contrast, the low‑reward rollouts linger for many frames, repeatedly applying the same forward action 2 without any corrective switch, which produces long‑lasting oscillatory drifts and continuously adds negative reward. The better runs also include a brief, purposeful turn or wall‑following maneuver that quickly drives the agent into contact with a boundary, whereas the poorer runs never make such a decisive directional change and instead wander aimlessly. This early termination and single corrective action sharply limit cumulative loss, explaining why the high‑reward trajectories achieve far less negative returns.
