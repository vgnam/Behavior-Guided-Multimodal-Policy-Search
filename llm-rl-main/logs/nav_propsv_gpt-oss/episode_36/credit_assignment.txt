[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts consistently begin with a decisive forward thrust (action 0) that quickly carries the agent into the corridor, whereas low‑reward runs either start with a modest move or immediately reverse direction, losing momentum. After this initial push, the successful episodes intersperse brief, sharp turns (actions 1 or 2) with short corrective bursts that keep the agent hugging the walls without lingering, while the poorer episodes linger longer before turning and often over‑rotate, causing the trajectory to wobble and stall. The timing of these corrections is tight in the high‑reward cases – each adjustment occurs within a few timesteps of a deviation – but in the low‑reward cases the agent hesitates for many steps, producing prolonged oscillations and occasional backward steps. Moreover, the high‑reward trajectories repeatedly stabilize inside inner corners, using quick back‑and‑forth nudges to settle, whereas the low‑reward paths either fail to enter those corners or bounce out of them without settling. This pattern of an early strong forward drive followed by rapid, precise corrective actions keeps the agent progressing along the optimal path and thus yields substantially higher cumulative reward.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts consistently keep action 0 (forward thrust) dominant, only inserting brief corrective turns (actions 1 or 2) and immediately returning to forward motion, which preserves steady progress and limits the accumulation of negative turn rewards; low‑reward rollouts either turn too early, linger in turning actions, or make a single abrupt turn that quickly leads to loss of control. The timing of those turns in the successful runs aligns with clear geometric cues such as corners, so the agent corrects just before a wall and then resumes straight travel, whereas the poorer runs apply turns in open corridors or miss the cue entirely, causing collisions. Additionally, high‑reward episodes exhibit a regular rhythm of short forward bursts followed by a single, well‑placed adjustment, preventing oscillatory over‑correction, while the low‑reward examples show irregular bursts, extended pauses, or rapid back‑and‑forth switches that destabilize the trajectory. This disciplined pattern of maximizing forward steps and minimizing time spent in corrective actions yields higher cumulative rewards by reducing negative penalties and avoiding crashes.

[WORST POLICY]
The high‑reward rollouts all contain at least one moment where the agent switches away from the default “action 2” thrust—often a brief, positive‑reward thrust or a terminal “action 0” that stops the motion—whereas the low‑reward rollouts never change action and apply “2” continuously from start to finish. This single deviation creates a short interval of smoother, directed movement (evident from the modest positive rewards and modest state changes) before the agent inevitably drifts into a wall, while the constant‑action runs produce unchecked oscillations that never settle into any purposeful trajectory. In the higher‑reward episodes the timing of the switch occurs early (within the first few frames), giving the agent a chance to gain a small reward and to temporarily reduce the amplitude of its state swings; the all‑action‑2 runs lack any early corrective timing and thus explode in magnitude, hitting the –500 penalty. Consequently, the presence of an early, alternative action that briefly stabilises the motion—and the absence of that corrective step in the low‑reward cases—explains why the former achieve relatively higher (less negative) returns.
