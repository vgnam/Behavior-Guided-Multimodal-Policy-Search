[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts all begin with a sustained stretch of pure forward actions (action 0) lasting at least several seconds before any turn is taken, whereas the low‑reward episodes either turn immediately or interleave turns and forward moves from the outset. When a turn is finally executed in the successful runs, it is a single, well‑timed right‑hand action (action 2) that follows a long forward run and is immediately followed by another forward streak, creating a clean “go‑straight‑then‑turn‑go‑straight” rhythm; the poor runs either chain multiple turns together or alternate forward and turn actions rapidly, producing jittery, oscillatory motion. The successful trajectories also include brief “pause” or zero‑thrust moments (no‑action frames) right after a turn, allowing the agent to settle before resuming forward thrust, a stabilization step that never appears in the failing rollouts. Conversely, the low‑reward episodes exhibit chaotic over‑corrections—quick back‑and‑forth swings between actions 1 and 2—leading to early collisions or premature terminations. This difference in temporal sequencing (long forward epochs → single, timed turn → brief settling → forward) keeps the agent on the intended path and accumulates positive reward, while the erratic, turn‑heavy patterns cause loss of control and negative outcomes.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts keep a long stretch of the “forward” action (action 0) before any correction, whereas low‑reward runs intersperse forward moves with immediate, abrupt turns (actions 1 or 2). When a correction is needed, the successful episodes apply only a single small‑adjustment step after several forward steps, while the failures make rapid, successive adjustments that swing the state back and forth. The best trajectories also delay large‑magnitude state changes until the agent has built momentum, whereas the poor trajectories jump to extreme state values early, leading to early collisions or wall contacts. Moreover, the high‑reward runs exhibit a smooth convergence toward a stable orientation near the episode’s end, while the low‑reward ones end with chaotic, high‑frequency oscillations that push the agent out of the navigable corridor. These timing and magnitude differences let the former accumulate steady positive reward and stay within bounds, whereas the latter incur penalties and premature terminations.

[WORST POLICY]
The higher‑reward rollouts all contain brief switches from the default “2” thrust to the “0” action, producing short, steady forward moves or deliberate turns that keep the state value within a modest range, whereas the low‑reward rollouts stay on action 2 almost continuously and drive the state to swing wildly between extreme values. This occasional use of action 0 creates a controlled, low‑amplitude trajectory and often triggers an early, decisive change (e.g., a sharp turn or stop) that terminates the episode before many negative time‑step penalties can accumulate. In contrast, the low‑reward episodes exhibit persistent, repetitive oscillations between two far‑apart states, reflecting over‑correction and a lack of any corrective “0” actions. Because the worst‑policy receives a penalty at each time step, the shorter, less‑oscillatory sequences in the high‑reward rollouts naturally yield a higher (less negative) total reward. Thus, intermittent “0” actions, limited state amplitude, and early termination are the behavioral components that distinguish the relatively better rollouts from the consistently poor, oscillatory ones.
