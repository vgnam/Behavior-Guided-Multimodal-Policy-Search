[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts consistently begin with an extended series of forward‑thrust actions (action 2) that carry the agent well away from the start wall before any turning is introduced, whereas low‑reward rollouts often turn immediately or oscillate between forward and reverse actions within the first few steps, leading to early collisions. When turns do occur in the successful trajectories, they are timed after a stable forward segment and are executed as single, decisive actions rather than rapid back‑to‑back changes, which keeps the agent on a coherent path; the poor rollouts exhibit clusters of consecutive turn actions (or alternating turn‑forward pairs) that produce jittery, over‑corrected motion. The high‑reward sequences also display longer uninterrupted forward runs later in the episode, allowing the agent to accumulate positive reward steadily, while the low‑reward episodes break forward motion with frequent, short thrusts interleaved with pauses or opposite‑direction moves, preventing sustained progress. Finally, successful rollouts avoid “sharp‑turn‑then‑immediate‑reverse” patterns that appear in the low‑reward cases, a behavior that likely triggers negative rewards by bumping into walls or exiting the navigable region. These timing and action‑selection differences explain why the former accumulate large positive returns and the latter terminate with low or negative scores.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts consistently begin with a brief forward thrust (action 0) and then introduce a quick corrective turn (action 1 or 2) within a few steps, creating short, alternating bursts of motion rather than long uninterrupted runs; low‑reward rollouts often linger in a prolonged forward streak before a sudden, large‑angle turn that frequently leads to a collision. The successful episodes also make frequent, modest‑amplitude corrections whenever the state value begins to drift, keeping the agent centered on a viable corridor, whereas the poorer episodes allow the drift to grow and only react with a single, drastic adjustment late in the episode. In the high‑reward cases the agent repeatedly interleaves forward moves with immediate “fine‑tuning” actions, which yields steady positive rewards and prevents the accumulation of negative penalties, while the low‑reward cases exhibit delayed or missing fine‑tuning, resulting in overshoot and terminal failures. This timing of timely, incremental corrections versus late, aggressive turns plausibly explains the large reward gap between the two groups.

[WORST POLICY]
The high‑reward rollouts all contain at least one non‑steady control input – typically a brief “0” (stop or sharp correction) or a single early turn – that interrupts a long run of the forward‑thrust action, whereas the low‑reward rollouts apply the same forward‑thrust action (action 2) continuously from start to finish. This intermittent braking or turning in the better rollouts breaks the monotonic thrust, preventing the agent from building up the large oscillations and overshoots that dominate the poor rollouts. By inserting a corrective action early, the high‑reward episodes redirect the trajectory toward a wall or a goal and then halt before a catastrophic collision, yielding a less negative reward. In contrast, the always‑on thrust in the low‑reward cases forces the agent to repeatedly overshoot and bounce back, creating persistent oscillatory motion that never stabilises and drives the reward to its minimum. Consequently, the presence of timed corrective actions and varied action sequences explains why the higher‑reward rollouts achieve comparatively better outcomes.
