[CURRENT POLICY]
The high‑reward rollouts all exhibit a regular rhythm of a forward thrust (action 0) followed quickly by an alternating left‑right correction (actions 1 and 2), keeping the agent’s state within a narrow band and preventing large excursions; the low‑reward rollouts break this rhythm, either omitting the corrective turn or inserting it after a long delay, which lets the state drift and forces abrupt over‑corrections. In the successful episodes the agent’s adjustments are modest and timed almost immediately after each forward move, producing smooth, bounded oscillations, whereas the poor episodes contain sudden, high‑amplitude jumps in state followed by erratic, rapid direction changes that destabilize the trajectory. Consistently, the high‑reward trajectories avoid prolonged periods of pure forward motion without correction, while the low‑reward ones either linger too long moving straight or reverse direction excessively, squandering the reward associated with forward progress. This disciplined alternation of thrust and turn maintains proximity to the optimal path and yields higher cumulative reward, whereas the chaotic timing and lack of balanced corrections cause the agent to waste steps and incur penalties.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts consistently use a pattern of modest forward steps followed by a single right‑turn action, keeping the state changes smooth, whereas low‑reward episodes contain an abrupt turn or large displacement that immediately drives the agent into a crash zone. The successful runs insert tiny corrective adjustments every few frames, damping oscillations and preventing drift toward walls; the failures lack these micro‑corrections and let the trajectory deviate until it hits an obstacle. They also begin each episode with sustained forward motion that builds distance before any major turn, giving the policy time to align with corridor geometry, while poor rollouts often start with an immediate sharp turn that mis‑aligns the agent with the maze layout. Moreover, high‑reward trajectories keep state magnitudes within a moderate range, avoiding sudden spikes that signal over‑aggressive actions, whereas low‑reward cases exhibit rapid state jumps indicating reckless moves that cause termination. This rhythmic forward‑turn‑forward pattern matches the alternating straight and corner sections of the environment, and breaking this rhythm with erratic or missing forward steps leads to loss of control and lower reward.

[WORST POLICY]
The higher‑reward rollouts all contain a noticeable early deviation from the default “forward‑only” action – they switch from the constant action 2 to either a stop (action 0) or a sharp turn within the first few dozen steps, which prevents the agent from drifting far into the maze and hitting a wall. By contrast, the low‑reward rollouts keep action 2 for almost the entire episode, producing long periods of unchecked forward thrust that lead to large oscillations and a late‑stage collision that accrues a much larger negative sum. The high‑reward trajectories also show brief, purposeful corrective thrusts that quickly re‑orient the agent away from obstacles, whereas the low‑reward runs display chaotic, uncorrected motion with no clear steering effort. This timing difference – early, decisive action changes versus prolonged uniform motion – plausibly explains why the former accumulate less penalty and achieve relatively better scores.
