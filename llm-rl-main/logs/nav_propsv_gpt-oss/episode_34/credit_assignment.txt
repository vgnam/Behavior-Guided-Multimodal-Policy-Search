[CURRENT POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts keep long uninterrupted stretches of forward thrust (action 0) and only insert very brief turning bursts (one or two steps of actions 1 or 2) to correct heading, so the agent spends most of the episode moving forward and accumulating positive reward; low‑reward rollouts either thrust forward immediately into a wall or break forward motion into frequent, prolonged turn–forward alternations that quickly dissipate momentum. In the successful runs the corrective turns are timed just after a forward segment, producing a clean “forward‑then‑adjust” rhythm, whereas the poor runs show turns that occur arbitrarily or linger for many steps, creating jitter and loss of control. The high‑reward trajectories also align the agent with corridor walls before advancing, using the positive reward signal to stay on a clear path, while the low‑reward episodes display early mis‑alignment and over‑corrections that push the agent into obstacles or cause it to oscillate in place. Consequently, the disciplined forward‑dominant pattern and precisely timed, minimal corrections in the high‑reward rollouts directly translate into sustained progress and higher cumulative reward, whereas the erratic, turn‑heavy or premature‑forward behaviours of the low‑reward rollouts lead to collisions, wasted steps, and lower returns.

[BEST POLICY]
High‑reward rollouts consistently begin with a decisive forward thrust (action 0) and keep a strong forward bias throughout the episode, only inserting brief, low‑impact adjustments (actions 1 or 2) that are immediately followed by another large forward push; this rapid “correct‑then‑accelerate” rhythm keeps the agent moving toward high‑value states and prevents drift. In contrast, low‑reward rollouts either start with a backward or neutral move, linger on a single adjustment action for many steps, or fail to apply a reinforcing forward thrust after a turn, which lets the state drift away from rewarding regions and often ends in a collision. The high‑reward trajectories also show short, well‑timed bursts of forward motion that quickly recover any overshoot, whereas the low‑reward cases exhibit prolonged oscillations or pauses that accumulate negative reward before a terminal failure. This systematic difference in maintaining forward momentum and promptly correcting minor deviations plausibly explains the large gap in episodic returns.

[WORST POLICY]
The high‑reward rollouts all contain at least one deliberate deviation from the default “forward” action (action = 2), typically a brief turn or thrust reduction (e.g., switching to action = 0) that occurs shortly before the episode ends, whereas the low‑reward rollouts keep action = 2 for the entire trajectory and never introduce a corrective maneuver. This early or mid‑episode correction limits the magnitude of the state jumps, producing more moderate fluctuations rather than the large spikes seen in the low‑reward runs, which helps the agent stay farther from walls longer and thus accrue a higher cumulative reward. Moreover, the high‑reward episodes show a pattern of an initial aggressive thrust followed by a quick, oscillatory “settling” phase with smaller, more frequent adjustments, while the low‑reward episodes remain in a prolonged, high‑amplitude oscillatory regime without any settling period. The timing of the corrective action—appearing after the first few steps rather than only at the very end—prevents the agent from building up excessive momentum that would otherwise cause a hard collision. Together, these intermittent, timed corrections and the resulting smoother state trajectory plausibly explain why the high‑reward rollouts lose far less reward than the continuously unchecked low‑reward ones.
